## Part 1 - Meaning of the term Paśu-pālana (Animal Husbandry)

Part 1 - Meaning of the term Paśu-pālana (Animal Husbandry)

The Animal husbandry (paśu-pālana) is a common name in the modern period. It is meant for the care and management of cattle, sheep, horses etc. The raising of animals is as old as civilization itself. Men first practised agriculture, which included the raising of domestic animals. As the cattle are so closely related with the practice of agriculture, so that, agriculture and animal husbandry have been dealt with simultaneously in ancient literature. Animal husbandry deals with the production of domestic animals or livestock, and it includes breeding, feeding and nourishment, rearing and tending and management of cattle. The protecting of cattle of the Gurus (teacher) formed a necessary part of ancient Indian education.[1] A lot of information is available on keeping of animals in the Vedic and Purāṇic age. The Aryans at the time of the Ṛgveda had domesticated the horse, sheep, cattle, goat, ass, dog and buffalo; of these, cattle represented a principal unit and the general expression for wealth.[2]

The word paśu indicates cattle (both singly and collectively). It is derived as—

sarvamaviśeṣeṇa paśyati, dṛś-ku, paśādeśaḥ.[3]

The term paśupālanam is used to denote animal husbandry, which literally means the tending or rearing of cattle[4] (paśūn pālayati iti).[5] M. Monier Williams in his A Sanskrit-English Dictionary too defines paśupālanam as the tending or rearing of cattle (the duty of a Vaiśya).[6] Animal husbandry was the occupation of most of the people since pre-Vedic time. Animals play a key role in keeping the balance of the environment. That’s why animals have been considered the backbone of Indian economy. Actually, the importance of animals was known to man well before he started organized agriculture. Man gets a number of useful products from animals, such as food, skin, cover, manure, power, etc. and thus, the domesticated animals have become an integral part of agriculture and rural life.Indians of Vedic age recognized the importance of animals and, therefore, they began to manage the forests and pastures for protecting and grazing their animals. The Ṛgveda mentions that the tasks of taking the cattle to the pastures and forests for grazing and bring them back safely were assigned to the herdsman. They used to graze their cattle by taking them to pastures or grasslands in the morning and bringing them back in the evening,[7] the practice which is still seen in the villages of India.This practice was also prevailed in the days of the Purāṇas.[8]

There are voluminous treatises on animal husbandry.We find the name of Śālihotra, who is said to be the founder of Aśvavidyā (the science of horse).Therefore, the science of horse is not only the Aśvaśāstra but also Śālihotra.Since it describes treatment of diseases of horses, so it is called also Aśvacikitsā, Aśvavaidyaka or Aśvāyurveda, i.e., ‘science of healing horses.’ Moreover, the Aśvavaidyaka of Jayadattasūri and the Aśvacikitsā of Nakula also deals with the healing of diseases of horses as well as their breeding and training.[9]

The originator of the science of elephants is said to be wise Pālakāpya.[10] The treatise on Hastyāyurveda (the science of healing elephants) is the Pālakāpyasaṃhitā which is attributed to sage Pālakāpya.It deals with anatomy, physiology, pathology, major and minor diseases with medical and surgical treatments, and drugs and diet.The Mātaṅgalīlā of Nīlakaṇṭhācārya is also a work on the science of healing elephants.[11]

## Part 11 - The Account of Uplifting the Mountain Govardhana

Part 11 - The Account of Uplifting the Mountain Govardhana

The Bhāgavata Purāṇa mentions about the arrangements for the annual sacrifice to the rain-God Indra(Indra-yāga) for the protection of the agricultural products in its tenth Skandha.[1] In the Bhāgavata Purāṇa Nanda explained to curious Kṛṣṇa that god Indra is the presiding deity of the rainfall and clouds are his manifestations. They shower water which is the very source of pleasure and existence of all creatures.[2] People worship Indra, the ruler of clouds and regulator of the Earth, by performing sacrifices.[3] It is on the leftovers of the sacrifice that people survive for achieving their three purposes in life (viz. dharma, artha and kāma, which are known as trivarga). The rain-god is the genuine bestower of fruits to individuals who exercise in cultivation, trade and commerce etc.[4] He, who gives up the traditional religious rites, out of passion, greediness, panic or hatred, does not attain his real good.[5] Hearing his father’s words Kṛṣṇa replied with a view to arise extreme anger in Indra.[6] Kṛṣṇa said that it is not necessary to regain the favour of a divinity for the profit of mankind because a being comes into existence only by its own past deeds i.e. karma and passes away through the force of its own karmas.[7] A particular God is not to be worshipped. They who leave one God and run to worship another God do not gain anything.[8]

According to the Viṣṇu Purāṇa, there are four branches of knowledge-logical, scriptural, practical and political—

ānvīkṣikī trayī vārttā daṇḍanītistathāparā/ 
vidyācatuṣṭayaṃ tvetat…//[9]

Agriculture, commerce and tending of cattle; the knowledge of these three professions comprises the practical science (vārtā).Agriculture is the survival of farmers, buying and selling are the survival of traders and kine are people’s support.

with the objects which were collected for the worship of Indra.[10] In the Viṣṇu Purāṇa also it is stated by Kṛṣṇa that they are bound to worship the mountains and to offer sacrifices to cattle because cattle and mountain are their gods.[11] Kṛṣṇa further suggested that various eatables such as sweet dishes from rice, boiled in milk with sugar, puddings and cakes might be prepared. Various types of gifts might be given to the Brāhmaṇas, grass to the cows and sacrifice to the mountain Govardhana.[12] Circumambulation to the cows, the Brahmins and the mountain Govardhana might be performed.[13] Following the advice of Kṛṣṇa, all the inhabitants performed sacrifice to the mountain Govardhana, worshipped the Brāhmaṇas and offered grass to the cows. Nanda and all the women-folk with other cowherds circumambulated the mountain Govardhana.[14] Then, Kṛṣṇa himself assumed another form of huge size and consumed the food which were offered to the mountain, announcing that he is the mountain Govardhana itself.[15] Seeing the other form of Kṛṣṇa, the inhabitants of Vraja exclaimed with surprise that the mountain takes any form he desires, he punishes those who show disrespect to him.

So, for the welfare of all, they must bow with reverence to him. Thus, the mountain, the cows and the Brāhmaṇas were worshipped according to the directions of Lord Kṛṣṇa.[16]

When Indra came to know that his worship had been stopped, he got very angry upon the cowherds and Nanda.[17] Considering himself as the Supreme Ruler of the Universe, Indra then ordered the host of clouds called Saṃvartaka to ruin the Universe saying that the cowherds who are forest dwellers, depending on a mortal like Kṛṣṇa, had disregarded a deity.[18] Riding on the elephant Airāvata and accompanied with the troop of Maruts, Indra followed the cloud Saṃvartaka, with a view to destroy the whole locality of Nanda and the Cowherds.[19] Thus, Indra commanded and freed the clouds from their bonds as a result of which the clouds forcefully tormented Gokula with heavy showers.[20] Suffering from the torrential downpour, the cowherds then resorted to their rescuer Kṛṣṇa who gave them courage and requested to defend them from angry Indra.[21] Observing the words of the cowherds Kṛṣṇa came to know that it was the work of offended Indra, he asked the cowherds to enter comfortably into the hollow under the mountain Govardhana, which he uprooted with one of his hand and held it up caringly as if it were a mushroom.[22] Kṛṣṇa thus upheld the mountain for seven days.[23] Observing the power of Kṛṣṇa, Indra was extremely surprised. Being shorn of pleasure and saddened of his plan, he controlled his Saṃvartaka clouds.[24] Thus, Kṛṣṇa caused the clouds to stop pouring down and saved the cowherds from the horrible anger of Indra whom he fully defeated.

Here, the belief in the divinity of the mountains led to the worship of the mountains. Indra became angry caused by the disrespect shown to him and the rain fell in consequence of it. As a result of worshipping the mountain Govardhana, the clouds forcefully tormented Gokula with heavy showers, according to the command of angry Indra. This clearly brings out the tradition of worshipping the mountains for timely rain. Timely rain is very much necessary for the process of agriculture. Therefore, this myth can be related with agriculture.

In the Viṣṇu Purāṇa it is clearly pointed out that when the clouds are seen distended with rain, the Earth becomes neither barren of corn, nor uncovered with abundance, and nor is man distressed by hunger—

nāśasyā nātṛṇā bhūmirna bubhukṣārddito janaḥ/ 
dṛśyate yatra dṛśyante vṛṣṭimanto balāhakāḥ//[25]

S.S. Dange opines in his The Bhāgavata Purāṇa: Mytho-Social Study that the heap of boiled rice offered to the mountain Govardhana makes it the corn-spirit, and a special festival of offerings on the mountain identified with Kṛṣṇa shows Kṛṣṇa to be a vegetation God.[26] The Viṣṇu Purāṇa too narrates the story of uplifting the mountain Govardhana.[27] According to the Viṣṇu Purāṇa, as Kṛṣṇa preserved the cattle of Gokula by raising up Govardhana, the chief of mountains, so Indra being grateful, installed Kṛṣṇa as Upendra. And as Lord Kṛṣṇa was Indra of the cows, so he was called as Govinda.[28]

## Part 12 - Establishment of Vārtā-Vṛtti

Part 12 - Establishment of Vārtā-Vṛtti

Regarding the establishment of vārtā-vṛtti an account is given in the Matsya Purāṇa. According to this Purāṇa, at the end of Kṛtayuga both Sandhyā and Sandhyāṃśa appeared. Then the combination with Tretāyuga was achieved in accordance with the movement of times.[1] Satyayuga consists of 4,000 Divine years. Its Sandhyā consists of 400 Divine years and the Sandhyāṃśa of the same number of years. The Sandhyā and Sandhyāṃśa of the remaining three Yugas are also calculated in the same way as enumerated above; if a Yuga consists of one thousand Divine years then its Sandhyā and Sandhyāṃśa will consist of one hundred Divine years.[2] The term Sandhyā indicates union, morning or evening, twilight, the period which precedes a Yuga, the time intervening between the expiration of one Yuga and the commencement of another.[3] Sandhyāṃśa indicates the period at the end of each Yuga.[4]

At that time of rainfall, the oṣadhīs were sprung up and vārtā-vṛtti was established in towns and villages—

oṣadhīṣu ca jātāsu pravṛtte vṛṣṭisarjjane/ 
pratiṣṭhitāyāṃ vārttāyāṃ grāmeṣu ca pureṣu ca//[5]

This occurred in the Tretāyuga during the period of Svāyambhuva Manu.[6] According to the Bhāgavata Purāṇa, vārtā comprises of tending cattle, tilling agricultural fields, carrying on trade and lending for profit. The main profession of the Vaiśyas was to carry on vārtā-vṛtti.[7]

Thus, various myths are found in the Purāṇas which are directly or indirectly associated with agriculture.

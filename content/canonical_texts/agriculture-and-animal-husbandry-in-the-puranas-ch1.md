## Part 1 - The Meaning and Scope of the word Purāṇa

Part 1 - The Meaning and Scope of the word Purāṇa

The Purāṇas are an unfailing source of information that helps us to reconstruct the history of Indian culture and civilization. By culture and civilization we mean the history of Indian polity, of Indian society, the history of religion and philosophy, legal history, the history of arts and crafts, architecture and iconography besides royal dynasties and the period of their rule.[1] The only species of Indian literature, which can claim, next to the Vedas, to reach back to great antiquity, is the Purāṇa.[2] Long before the beginning of the Christian era it came also to be used as the designation of a class of books dealing, among other matters, with old-world stories and legends.[3] The word purāṇa means old story (purāṇam ākhyānam).[4] The Śabdakalpadruma and the

Vācaspatyam explain Purāṇa as that which remains in the past and deals with the past.[5] Purāṇas are the old narratives or ancient legends having informative contents.

Yāska in his Nirukta defines Purāṇa as–

purā navaṃ bhavati[6]
i.e. it was new in the days of yore.

purā navaṃ bhavati[6]

i.e. it was new in the days of yore.

Sāyaṇācārya in the Ṛgveda[7] explains the word purāṇyā as—

purā kṛtayā.

So the term purāṇa indicates ‘ancient one’ according to this explanation.

The etymology of the term purāṇa is furnished by the Vāyu Purāṇa as follows—

yasmāt purā hyanatīdaṃ purāṇaṃ tena tat smṛtam[8]
i.e. that which lives from very remote times.

yasmāt purā hyanatīdaṃ purāṇaṃ tena tat smṛtam[8]

i.e. that which lives from very remote times.

The Purāṇas have their roots in the Vedic literature; many a legend, from the Ṛgvedic hymns and from the Brāhmaṇas, reappears in the Purāṇas.[9] They are thus composed with a view to get the knowledge revealed in the Vedas through stories. The Brāhmaṇa, who may know the four Vedas with the Aṅgas and Upaniṣads, should not really be regarded as having attained proficiency, if he should not thoroughly know the Purāṇa. He should reinforce the Veda with the Itihāsa and Purāṇa.[10]

The majority of people act according to the popular rituals which are recorded in the Purāṇas. The Purāṇic tradition is a combination of the Vedic ritualistic and popular mythical traditions.

That is why the Purāṇa is regarded as the fifth Veda in addition to the four Vedas i.e., the Ṛgveda, the Yajurveda, the Sāmaveda and the Atharvaveda—

purāṇaṃ pañcamo vedaḥ.[11]

The Chāndogya Upaniṣad too mentions the Itihāsa-Purāṇa as the fifth Veda—

itihāsapurāṇaṃ pañcamaṃ vedānām.[12]

The Skanda Purāṇa states that the Purāṇas are not inferior to the Vedas. The Purāṇas are as steady as the Vedas. All the Vedas are firmly rooted in the Purāṇas. It opines that whatever is not seen in the Vedas or Smṛtis, is seen in the Purāṇas.[13] The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa mentions Itihāsa and Purāṇa as subjects of study by a householder. It has been mentioned in the context of revelation on Pañcamahāyajña.[14] The Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa opines that it is called Purāṇa because it was in olden times.[15]

The Śabdakalpadruma[16] too contains the same meaning—

purā purvasmin kāle bhava iti
i.e., which was in ancient times.

purā purvasmin kāle bhava iti

i.e., which was in ancient times.

The Matsya Purāṇa gives the definition of the word purāṇa as—

purātanasya kalpasya purāṇāni vidurbudhāḥ.[17]
‘containing the records of past events’

purātanasya kalpasya purāṇāni vidurbudhāḥ.[17]

‘containing the records of past events’

The Padma Purāṇa says that it is called Purāṇa because it is interested in the past and therefore, it describes about the past.[18] Amarasiṃha in the Amarakoṣa describes Purāṇa as ancient tales.[19]

The Purāṇas have been utilized through centuries not only for educating the mass mind and infusing it with the nobler ideas of life but also for tactfully solving the religious, social and economic problems which were created in ancient India by the rise of various religious movements and the repeated invasions made by foreign races.[20] These works afford us, more than other works of the time a great insight into all phases and aspects of Hinduism.[21] For the modern Hinduism, the Purāṇas are religious treatises of divine origin. Hence, they have been a great source of inspiration to the mass-mind, especially, on religious matter.[22] That is why, the Purāṇas ought to have more careful study than has previously been dedicated to them.

## Part 2 - Characteristics of the Purāṇas

Part 2 - Characteristics of the Purāṇas

The Matsya Purāṇa and the Vāyu Purāṇa speak of five characteristics to distinguish a Purāṇa.[1] They are as follows—

Creation (sarga),

Dissolution and re-creation (pratisarga),

The lineage line (vaṃśa),

Jurisdiction of the fourteen Manus, belonging to the solar and lunar races (manvantara), and

The genealogy of the kings of the solar and lunar races (vaṃśyānucarita).

The Purāṇas, with these five characteristics, sing the glory of Brahmā, Viṣṇu, Arka and Rudra.They also describe the creation and dissolution of the Earth.[2] Moreover, the Purāṇas describe about the four Vargas i.e. Dharma, Artha, Kāma and Mokṣa.[3]

Amarasiṃha also describes the five characteristics of the Purāṇas in the Amarakoṣa.[4] The Agni Purāṇa too mentions the five characteristics.[5] The five fold characteristics of Purāṇas are also mentioned by some other Purāṇas such as the Śiva Purāṇa, Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa, Viṣṇu Purāṇa, Skanda Purāṇa, Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa and the Kūrma Purāṇa.[6]

But the Bhāgavata Purāṇa mentions the ten characteristics of Purāṇas as described by the Brāhmaṇa sages in conformity to Vedas and Śāstras.[7] It has also elaborated all the characteristics of the Purāṇas thoroughly.

They are as follows—

1. Sarga (subtle creation)

According to the Bhāgavata Purāṇa, sarga consists of the growth of mahat from the interruption in the balance of stability of the three guṇas of the prakṛti,leading to the development of three-fold ahaṃkāra (sāttvika, rājasa and tāmasa),the evolutes of which are subtle elements (bhūtas), the sense organs and their objects(gross elements and the deities presiding over the sense organs).[8]

2. Visarga (gross creation)

Visarga is the creation of the gross mobile and immobile nature.[9]

3. Vṛtti (sustenance)

The immobile creation(food-grains, fruits, roots) and some products of mobile creation too form the Vṛtti (sustenance) of the mobile creation. In case of human beings the Vṛtti is determined by their character, wish and scriptural commands.[10]

4 Rakṣā (protection of the universe)

Rakṣā consists of the incarnations and the sportive acts of Lord Viṣṇu, from age to age appearing among sub-human beings, human beings, sages and among gods, and the annihilation of the haters of the Vedas.[11]

5 Hetu (cause or the lust for Karmas)

Hetu of the universe is Jīva (individual soul). He is the doer of actions under the influence of ignorance.[12]

6 Antara (the periods of Manu)

Manvantara(Manu-period) is characterized by six special aspects. They are the period presided over by the particular Manu, the set of gods, the sons of Manu, Indra, seven sages and the incarnations of Hari.[13]

7 Vaṃśa (dynasties of kings)

Vaṃśa is the race of kings who were born of god Brahmā and continued throughout the three divisions of time–the past, present and future.[14]

8 Vaṃśānucarita(history of the kings)

Vaṃśānucarita is the history of the kings and their descendants.[15]

9 Saṃsthā (physical annihilation)

The destruction of the universe created by Māyā is called Saṃsthā. This destruction is of four types-Naimittika(Occasional or brought about by a particular cause), Prākṛtika(of the Prakṛti), Nitya(Constant) and Ātyantika(Absolute).[16]

10 Apāśraya (ultimate reality)

Apāśraya is the highest goal of the characteristics of the Purāṇas. Apāśraya (the last support or ultimate reality) is Brahman. It is depicted as a observer to what is caused by Māyā, as in the three phases of Jīva(viz. wakefulness, dreaming and dreamless deep sleep) and is also somewhat different from them as in Samādhi.[17]

The Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa also describes the ten main characteristics of the Purāṇas—

sṛṣṭiścāpi visṛṣṭiśca sthitisteṣāṃ ca pālanam/ 
karmaṇāṃ vāsanā vārtā manūnāṃ cākrameṇa ca// 
varṇanaṃ pralayānāṃ ca mokṣasya ca nirūpaṇam/ 
tatkīrtanaṃ harereva vedānāṃ ca pṛthak pṛthak//

These are as follows—

sṛṣṭi (original creation),

visṛṣṭi (creation of other movable and immovable things from mūla prakṛti),

sthiti (period of life),

pālana (defense),

karma vāsanā (necessity which makes a man liable for his acts or the desire for work),

manuvārtā (information about different Manus),

7. pralaya-varṇanā (the account relating to the eternal dissolution of the world),

mokṣa-nirūpaṇa (the determination of the nature of redemption),

harikīrtana (the eulogies of Hari) and

vedakīrtana (reciting of the Vedas separately).[18]

This Purāṇa further mentions that the Mahāpurāṇas are to deal with these tenfold characteristic.[19]

The Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa mentions the five-fold feature too. But according to this Purāṇa, these are appropriate for the Upapurāṇas only.[20]

The Padma Purāṇa[21] looks the various Purāṇas as the various limbs of Viṣṇu. the Padma Purāṇa is said to be Viṣṇu himself,—

the Brahma Purāṇa being the head,

the Padma Purāṇa the heart,

the Viṣṇu Purāṇa the right arm,

the Śiva Purāṇa being the left arm,

the Bhāgavata Purāṇa the two thighs,

the Nāradīya Purāṇa the navel,

the Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa the right shank,

the Agni Purāṇa being the left shank,

the Bhaviṣya Purāṇa the right knee,

the Brahmavaivarta being the left one,

the Liṅga Purāṇa the right ankle,

the Varāha Purāṇa being the left,

the Skanda Purāṇa the hair in the body,

the Vāmana Purāṇa the skin,

the Kūrma Purāṇa the back,

the Matsya Purāṇa the fat,

the Garuḍa Purāṇa being the marrow and

the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa the bones.

## Part 3 - Authorship of the Purāṇas

Part 3 - Authorship of the Purāṇas

The Purāṇas contain various references regarding their authorship. According to the Matsya Purāṇa, Vyāsa, the son of Satyavatī is the author of all the eighteen Purāṇas.[1] It states that Vyāsa has reduced the sense of the Vedas in the Purāṇas.

Vyāsa, after accumulating the eighteen Purāṇas, indulged in the composition of the Mahābhārata, with a view to enlarge the meaning of Vedas, which was an addition to the Purāṇas.[2] The Vāyu Purāṇa also mentions that Vyāsa, the son of Parāśara has composed the Paurāṇikī kathā(ancient chronicle story).[3] The Matsya Purāṇa[4] and the Nāradīya Purāṇa[5] opines that in the beginning there was only one Purāṇa, that was written by Vyāsa.[6] Vyāsa, after dividing the single Purāṇa, containing four lakhs of ślokas, into eighteen Purāṇas, spread them in the world.[7]

The Vāyu Purāṇa[8], the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa[9] and the Viṣṇu Purāṇa[10] give an account, how the original Purāṇa came into existence. According to those three Purāṇas Kṛṣṇa Dvaipāyana divided the single Veda into four and arranged them, and thus, he was called Vyāsa. He handed them over to his four disciples, one to each, namely Paila, Vaiśampāyana, Jaimini and Sumantu. Then with tales (ākhyāna), anecdotes(upākhyāna), songs(gāthā) and lore (kalpajokti) that had commentary down from the ages, he compiled a Purāṇa,[11] and taught it and the Itihāsa to his fifth disciple, the Sūta Romaharṣaṇa or Lomaharṣaṇa.[12]

Romaharṣaṇa made that Purāṇa-Saṃhitā into six versions and taught them to his six disciples,—

Ātreya Sumati,

Kāśyapa Akṛtavraṇa,, Bharadvāja Agnivarcas,, Vasiṣṭha Mitrayu,, Sāvarṇi Somadatti, and

Suśarman Śāṃśapāyana.

Three of them, Kāśyapa, Sāvarṇi and Śāṃśapāyana, made three separate Saṃhitās, which were called by their names. Romaharṣaṇa's Saṃhitā and those three were the root-compositions (mūla-Saṃhitā). They consisted of four divisions (pāda). Excepting Śāṃśapāyana's Saṃhitā, each contained four thousand verses.[13]

After the composition of the original Purāṇa, by Vyāsa, his disciple Romaharṣaṇa taught it to his son Ugrasravas.[14] Ugrasravas, the Sauti, appears as the reciter in some of the extant Purāṇas.[15]

The Bhāgavata Purāṇa mentions six persons to be traditionally noted as the teachers(paurāṇika) of the Purāṇas. They are—

Trayyāruṇi,, Kaśyapa,, Sāvarṇi,, Akṛtavraṇa,, Vaiśampāyana and

Hārita.[16]

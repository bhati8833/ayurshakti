9. Some Tentative Interpretations (of the Rasaratna-samuccaya)

We may now attempt to provisionally interpret some of the Rasaratna-samuccaya terms related to the ferrous materials. Nearly 2500 years before Rasaratna-samuccaya was composed, India had made for the first time wrought iron by solid state carbothermic reduction process. The samples naturally contained some inclusions of slag.

IX-24 Over centuries, it was found that the soft variety of wrought or pure iron could be carburised by contact with hot burning charcoal, and this produced some hardening of the samples and at least on the surface. Evidently, austenite, ferrite, pearlite phases were formed in the carburised samples. There was a reference to hot forging in the Kauti liya Arthasastra (7.3.8-9). tejo hi samdhana karanama | namate lohe lohena samdhatta iti  Third century B.C. Pandurajar Dhibi samples of sickle were found to contain 0.22 weight p.c. carbon and distinct grains of tempered martensite (De and Chattopadhyaya, 1989) which must have arisen due to intentional quenching, hardening, and conversion from the austenite phase (solid solution of carbon in gamma-iron). field reported that the second century B.C. Taxila contained 1.2 to 1.5 percent carbon. samples A HadWe do not know when India started producing crucible or 'wootz' iron/steel which contained more than 1.7 percent carbon. Probably this technology also was started before the Christian era, as inferred from the Greek and Roman travellers cum writers. Such high percentage of carbon resulted in the cementite (Fe,C) phase, which contributed to hardness as well as brittleness. The Wootz swords and implements could however be made ductile by careful forging at a not-too-high temperature of 650°C -850°c, when the coarse cementite grains were compressed and elongated in one direction, surrounded by pearlite and ferrite matrix. Reheating and quenching would result in some martensitic transformation. On selective etching, these 'Damascus steel swords' would show white cementite streaks. The finished product, a high carbon steel with cementite streaks in pearlite-graphite-martensite

IX-25 matrix possessed ideal combination of hardness (even at the edges) and ductility. These steel materials, sword etc., were described as 'of a dull blue colour, marked with ten millions of meandering lines' which were the distinctive 'damask' or surface pattern of seggregated white cementite (Sherby and Wadsworth, 1985). The Gupta era (4 th 5 th Century A.D.) in India witnessed spectacular iron technology in Ujjain, where big iron objects were forged in special furnaces. The 4 th century iron pillar, now at Meharauli, Delhi, and the 10 th century Dhar pillar (43'8'' high, the highest in the world) of king Bhoja are unique examples of hot forging of wrought iron. Both Subandhu (400 A.D.) in his work Vasavadatta, and king Bhoja (1000 1055 A.D.) in his Yuktikalpataru referred to iron works, pillars and lohasastra texts on iron technology. Subandhu specifically mentioned in his work the then knowledge on different kinds of magnets and magnetised iron. We have mentioned in the previous chapter Varahamihira's detailed description about quenching 26) of steel swords (Bs.50.23- which definitely resulted in martensitic transformation and hardening of steel. Uses of water, oil, milk, of blood and other quenching media resulted in different rates cooling and different degrees of martensitisation and hardening. With the aforesaid brief historical and technical discussion we may now interpret the Rasaratna-samuccaya terms provisionally. Munda loha was presumably wrought iron obtained by direct solid state reduction. The name probably originated from Mundia, one of the metalsmith tribes in the district of Bastar, whose

IX-26 ancient iron-making process has been scientifically observed and recorded by Prakash and Igaki (1984). Low carbon material mrdu was soft; 'drutadrava' is better translated as 'softening quickly' rather than 'melting quickly', since wrought iron would not have a low melting point. With more carbon, kuntham and kadaram varieties became less ductile. Kadaram was even brittle; black fracture indicated graphite at the interface. Tiksnam represented in general, carburised iron with appreciable hardness, which could be hypoeutectoid (less than 0.83 percent carbon) or hypereutectoid (more than 0.83 percent carbon) steel. 'Pogaras' meant hair-like lines which may be surmised as white cementite streaks, coarse enough to be seen by naked eyes (microscope had not been discovered yet). In the khara variety, cementite pogaras were too fine to be seen, but the brittleness and the 'mercury-like shining fracture surface' were evident. In the hrnnala, vajira and kalayasa varieties, hot forging and quenching had resulted in hardness as well as ductility. We have explained this point earlier while discussing the medieval practice of damascening of swords. The hrnnala variety was 'Chhedane atiparusam very hard to pierce. Vajira was described as 'pogara vajra samkasa suksmarekha' full of hard pogaras and overspreaded by dense thin linings. Kalayasa variety was described (5.82) as nilakrsna prabha sandre masrnam guru bhasvaram lohaghane api abhamgatmadhara kalayasam matam ||  11 bluish black, dense, smooth, heavy, bright and its sharpened edge not spoiled by hammering. This description is close enough to that of the damascened steel swords of a dull blue colour and with meandering lines'.

IX-27 The technique of producing 'Damascus steel and sword' from Indian wootz was learnt and perfected by the Arabs in Damascus. Around the time when the Rasaratna-samuccaya was written in India, the city of Damascus was conquered by Timur or Tamerlane (1401 A.D.), and the best metalsmiths were taken to Samarqand (Pant, 1985). Thus, the Indian technology was gradually taken to the Middle East and Central Asia. The kantaloha, as we have explained, was the iron made from magnetitic ore, and specially magnetised. Probably it was believed that iron made from magnetite and hematite had different properties. The different types of kantaloha merely referred to the different levels of magnetisation. The classification under headings of bhramaka, cumbaka etc. had existed for at least one millennium before Rasaratna-samuccaya Subandhu (400 A.D.) had mentioned these types in his work Vasavadatta. Rgveda to Rasaratnasamuccaya represents a wide time-span of three millennia, during which the Indian literature recorded the progress of knowledge in the areas of minerals, metals and alloys.. Archaeological evidences of ancient Indian metallurgy are no doubt mor underlying direct and conclusive but are necessarily mute. 8. Ferrous Materials discussed in Rasaratna-samuccaya

The Rasa-ratna-samuccaya, though primarily a medicinal text, provides valuable informations regarding the 12 th - 14 th century A.D. status of Indian knowledge related to the ferrous materials. Such a detailed coverage

Ix-21 (Rasaratna-samuccaya 5.70-5.99) had hardly been presented in any earlier literature. It was stated that there are three kinds of iron mundam (wrought iron), tiksnam (cast or carburised iron or steel) and kantam (the magnetic variety or the variety made from magnetite) (Rasaratna-samuccaya 5.70). Mundaloha could be midu type, which softens quickly, is smooth or glossy and does not break or is without fissure (abisphotam it could be of kuntham variety which expands with great difficulty on hammering; or it could be Kadaram type which breaks when struck with a hammer and has a black fracture (5.71-72). There are six varieties of tiksnam (5.76) The khara variety is hard, breaks on bending, free of pogaras (hair follicles or hair-like lines) and on fracture, exhibits mercury-like shining surfaces (5.77). The sara variety, 'obtained from white earth', has pogaras and its edges can be destroyed quickly (5.78). The hrnnala variety is whitish black, has pogara and is hard to pierce (5.79). The brittle variety looking like hair follicle is known as anga/ vanca pogara/taravatta (5.80). Vajira looks greyish black, and is full of hard pogaras, over-spreaded by dense thin linings (5.81). Kalayasa is bluish black, dense, smooth, bright, and its sharpened edge is not spoiled by hammering (5.82). These tiksna These tiksna varieties are hundred times better (satottaram) than other non-tiksna varieties (5.83).

IX-22 The kantalohas could be classified according to colour pita, rakta and krana after the trinity gods (5.84-86), or according to their magnetic properties: gramameta, lohajanam yat tat kantam bhramaka manam cumbayet cumbakam kantam karsayet karsakam tatha saksat yat dravayet loham natu kontam dvavaka manam tat romakantam syutinata vato romomo bhavet  Bhramaka type is weakly magnetic, causing movement of ferrous materials; cumbaka type 'kisses' or causes adherence; Karsaka attracts, and dravaka is strongly magnetic. The fifth variety of kantaloha, giving rise to hair-like structures is called romaka (5.90-91). We conjecture that kantaloha was made by reduction of magnetitic ore ksetram khatva grahitavyam tat prayatnena dhimana | marutat paviksiptam karmayeta natra samsayah ||  'obtained by carefully digging it out of the mines; that which had remained exposed to the sun and the atmosphere was avoided' (5.94), presumnably because the oxidised hemati te on the surface mines would have different reducibility properties. It was known that kantaloha (iron obtained from magnetite) vessel was not wetted by oil nailavindurna liptam parka dugdham bhavati sikharakaraka bhati bhumau  or boiling milk which does not overflow but rises high like a peak' (5.95). The surface of kantaloha probably adsorbed odoriferrous and

IX-23 organo-leptic compounds, as gandham himgum vyajitaca tiktatam nimbakalkah  it was stated that asafoetida gives up its odour, and the bitterness of neem (Azadirachta indica) decoction is lost when these materials are kept in a vessel made of kantaloha (5.95). Such accurate observations about the non-wetting by oil or milk, and adsorption properties of the kantaloha surface are indeed very fascinating. The different varieties of iron were 'killed' for medicinal purposes by roasting in air or with mercury, sulphur or cinnabar (5.96-5.148). It was known that ye guna marite gunde te guna gunda ki ke tasmat sarvatra gandara rogasanyai prayojayeta  the qualities of 'killed' mundaloha (roasted in air) and of the mundakittaka mandara or the rust of iron are the same, and therefore the latter was also acceptable for medicinal purposes (5.148). We know from modern science that both are oxides of iron. The high reactivity of mercury (an intoxicated elephant) with kanta loha (bent hook to restrain or fix the animal) was poetically described (5.91): madonmattagajah sutah kantam amkusam ucyate

1. Archaeology and History

We have critically surveyed in the previous chapters (II to IX) the references to gems, non-gem minerals and metals in the Sanskrit literatures, from the Rgveda to the Rasa-Ratna Samuccaya, which cover the entire span of three millennia of the ancient India. A part of our work has been published elsewhere (Biswas et al, 1990). Since mere recapitulation would be unnecessarily repetitive, we would restrict ourselves in this last chapter, to our observations on the following points: (a) correlations of the literary evidences with the archaeological data and other historical facts, and (b) the revelations about the scientific concepts and socio-philosophical attitude in ancient India. Archaeology and History—Archaeology digs not only the earth but also into the past in a telling manner. In this modern age of sophisticated instrumentation, the ancient artefacts can be accurately analysed and C 14 - dated through the accompanying organic residues; as a contrast, there are controversies on the dates of several works of literature. On the other hand, the archaeological samples of minerals and metals are mute, and often do not tell whether these were imported or locally made, and in the latter case,

X-2 the status of scientific knowledge of the indigenous manufacturers. The literary evidences tell us about the stateof-art and the status of concepts and philosophy. Even though the literary evidences on minerals and metals in the Indian scene are scanty, scattered and occasionally of uncertain date, these must be viewed as complementary to the archaeological evidences. The Rgveda describes the transition from the neolithic to the chalcolithic period in the Indian history (earlier than second millennium B.C.), the use of conch-shell and conch-pearl, different ill-defined metals, and widespread trade and navigation. Homer referred to the import of tin and ivory from India. The post-Rgvedic literatures describe purification. steps, sieving, winnowing, smelting in furnaces using bellows and different kinds of metals the ornamental and barter meta of gold and silver, low melting point metals lead and tin, copper and the new metal syama or iron obtained through solid state carbothermic reduction. The art of forging or joining of metals was mentioned. archaeological The problem of correlating archaeological and literary evidences is distinctly severe with the Rgveda and the epic Mahabharata. The epic was definitely expanded from time to time, and the references to ant-gold, marakata or emerald and gem-incrustations must have been later-day insertions. We cannot accept that marakata was known to the residents of 1200 B.C. Hastinapura.

X-3 On the other hand, the art of making iron, well-known in 1100 B.C. Atranjikhera, was never fully recorded. in the literature. High carbon steel was definitely made in the pre-Christian era Taxila, but the scattered literary evidences came much later. Similarly, zinc and brass were known in Taxila and during the Kautilya's time, and yet the first clear reference to the making of these items came much later from the celebrated Nagarjuna's pen. The problem of correlating archaeological and literary evidences is not so great with Panini and Kautilya. The writings of Panini and Kautilya clearly reflect the contemporary scenes (5 th to 3 rd century B.C.) of material civilization in India. The coins, the multi-bellowed furnaces and beryl mentioned by Panini, and vitta the circular dises of carburised iron, tiksna, the steel and arakuta, the high-zine brass of Kautilya have all been found in the different pre-Christian era strata of Taxila. The Gupta era splendour was fully reflected in the contemporary literatures. The then uses of gems, coins, copper, zinc, brass, iron and steel, the international trade with Sri Lanka and Rome, and the Graeco-Romjan influence in India, etc. were revealed in many writings, Ratnasastras, Angavijja, Amarakosa, Brhatsamhita and so on. The Rasasastra texts appeared after the decline of the Gupta era, and portrayed the decadent scenes of the Tantric period, the continued practices of zinc, brass and

X-4 ferrous metallurgy, the use of mineral-based medicines and the technological contact with China through Tibet. Ray (1956 97 believed that the knowledge of zinc metallurgy came to India from China, but this was an erroneous view. India was the first to make zinc and brass; what we learnt from the Chinese were precipitation of copper from solution by scrap iron, the arts of making paper and gunpowder etc.

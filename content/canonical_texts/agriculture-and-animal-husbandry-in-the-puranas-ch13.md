## Part 7 - The Myth behind the Creation of Agriculture

Part 7 - The Myth behind the Creation of Agriculture

According to the Vāyu Purāṇa, in the Tretā yuga, creating the conjugal copulation of the progenies, Brahmā devised their agricultural mainstay.[1] Disturbed by sorrow the progenies, being thirsty and hungry, their regenerations got restored once again in the Tretā yuga.[2] Achieving the agricultural activity by the desire of the progenies, the waters of rain became full of lively activity and spread gradually through the raised surface.[3] By rain came into existence the currents, the excavated wells, the low lying streams or rivers or canals.[4] At a specified distance the watery stocks increased gradually on the land routes by the contact of the water and the Earth and the plants came into existence.[5] In the Tretā yuga, there grew the plants through the process of cultivation.[6] Brahmā milked the Earth once again making Sumeru as the calf.[7] Then the seeds were produced on the surface of the Earth.[8]

He created the plants ripening by cultivation—

tataḥ prabhṛtyathauṣadhyaḥ kṛṣṭapacyāstu jajñire/[9]

When the plants grew up Brahmā devised plans for the purpose of agricultural cultivation.

He ordained agriculture and animal husbandry or cattle rearing for the Vaiśyas—

pāśupālyaṃ vāṇijyaṃ kṛṣiṃ caiva viśāṃ dadau//[10]

According to the Vāyu Purāṇa cultivators of the land, as organizers of means of livelihood, were known as Vaiśyas—

vaiśyānyeva tu tānāhuḥ kīnāśānvṛttisādhakān//[11]

Myths about the Creation of Agricultural Products:

The Matsya Purāṇa speaks that śāli (paddy), mudga (green-gram) and ikṣu (sugarcane) were produced from the drops of nectar fell from the mouth of the Sun while He was drinking it.[12] The same Purāṇa says that from the perspiration of ViṢṇu’s body, as he smote the demon Madhu, were produced the tila (sesame), the kuśa (sacred grass) and the māṣa (black gram).[13] The red variety of the kūṣmāṇḍa (cucumber) was produced from the fears of the Viśvedevas.[14] According to the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa, the date-palms were produced from the heads of the three sons of Varatrin, when they were killed by Indra.[15] Again the Skanda Purāṇa mentions that laśuna (garlic) and gṛñjana (turnip) were produced from the drops of the ocean at the churning by gods and demons, along with wine.[16]

## Part 8 - The Myth of Gautama and Gaṇeśa

Part 8 - The Myth of Gautama and Gaṇeśa

The Koṭirudrasaṃhitā of the Śiva Purāṇa[1] narrates the account of Gautama and Gaṇeśa. The sage Gautama was living in the forest named Daṇḍaka. He asked a boon from Brahmā to protect the corn-stalks in his field from any danger. Brahmā did that. Once upon a time there broke out a terrible starvation which had lasted for twelve years. In that continued famine, Gautama practiced penance and propitiated the god Varuṇa to give rain. When Varuṇa told that there was drought due to the wish of the gods then Gautama asked him to water by a different method. Then Varuṇa advised Gautama to prepare a well. Gautama prepared a well. This was filled in by divine water and Varuṇa wished that may there be water which would last forever. And that would be a holy place bearing the name of Gautama.[2] Gautama then planted many trees there. He sowed different kinds of food-grains and barleys. Trees, flowers and fruits were produced in plenty there. Once he sent his disciples to bring water from that place. But there were many other women along with the wives of other sages; and they wanted to take water first. The disciples told this to Ahalyā, who was the wife of Gautama. She then went there and brought water to Gautama. At this matter the wives of other sages got angry and asked their husbands to punish Gautama. The sages agreed. They propitiated Gaṇeśa to arrange for the dishonoring of Gautama at their hands. Gaṇeśa tried to convince them from the ill desire but they did not get convinced. So Gaṇeśa agreed.

He became a very weak cow and began grazing in the field of Gautama, filled with barley and paddy—

gautamasya ca kedāre tatrāsanvrīhayo yavāḥ/ 
gaṇeśastatra gaurbhūtvā jagāma kila durbalā//[3]

As Gautama tried to put off her with the help of a stalk, the cow fell down dead. After that the sages came on the sight and prescribed expiation. Either Gautama should go round the Earth thrice or he should bring the Gaṅgā there and take bath at that place in the water of Gaṅgā. Gautama then propitiated Śiva going to the Himalaya mountain and as a result of which the great river Gaṅgā was released, the dead cow got washed by the water of Gaṅgā and got up and the river Gaṅgā began to flow continuously.

In this myth of sage Gautama as we find the references of field of Gautama, full with barley and paddy, so it clearly indicates the practicing of agriculture prevailed in the days of Purāṇas.

## Part 10 - The Account of King Ākāśa

Part 10 - The Account of King Ākāśa

The custom of tilling the sacrificial ground with a golden plough is reflected in the account of king Ākāśa, who was the son of Mitravarmā.

According to the Skanda Purāṇa, king Ākāśa found a girl while he was ploughing the ground by golden plough on the banks of the river Araṇi for the sake of a sacrifice—

yajñārthaṃ śodhayāmāsa bhuvamāraṇitīrataḥ/ 
kāñcanena halenaiva kṛṣyamāṇe dharātale//[1]

The king named the girl Padminī.[2] Later Padminī was married to Śrīnivāsa. Reference of using golden plough for tilling is met with in this story. This too, indicates the practice of agriculture in the days of the Purāṇas.

## Part 9 - The Legend behind the Name Govardhana-Tīrtha

Part 9 - The Legend behind the Name Govardhana-Tīrtha

In the Brahma Purāṇa[1], we find a legend of Govardhana-tīrtha. This tīrtha is said to be on the bank of the Gautamī.

According to the Brahma Purāṇa, once there was a Brāhmaṇa farmer, Jābāli by name, who lived on the cultivation of the land—

brāhmaṇaḥ karṣakaḥ kaścijjābāliriti viśrutaḥ//[2]

He did not release the bullocks from the plough even at mid-day, and kept on stabbing them with the pointed staff even when they were tired—

na vimuñcatyanaḍvāhau madhyaṃ yāte’pi bhāskare/ 
pratodena pratudati pṛṣṭhato’pi ca pārśvayoḥ//[3]

Seeing this Surabhi(Kāmadhenu), the divine cow, reported about the matter to the Bull Nandī, who was the vehicle of Śiva. Getting permission of Śiva, Nandī took away the whole species of the cow from all the worlds. When the gods approached Nandī, he advised the gods to perform the Gosava sacrifice. In the Brahma Purāṇa the Gosava is said to have been performed for the growth of cattle, at the bank of the Gautamī. It is said to be a Vedic ritual, wherein the sacrificer enacted the bull, approaching his near female relations.[4] According to the Jaiminīya Brāhmaṇa, it was performed by Janaka Vaideha.[5] Being agreed with Nandī, the gods then performed Gosava sacrifice on the bank of the Gautamī. As a result the cattle grew in plenty.

The place where this sacrifice was performed came to be known as Go-vardhana i.e. the increaser of Cows—

gautamyāśca śubhe pārśve gāvo vavṛdhire tataḥ/
govarddhanaṃ tu tattīrthaṃ devānāṃ prītivardhanam//.[6]

Here, in this myth, it is met with that people in the days of Purāṇas, used bullocks for the act of ploughing. This myth too gives us reference about the practice of agriculture which prevailed in the days of the Purāṇas.

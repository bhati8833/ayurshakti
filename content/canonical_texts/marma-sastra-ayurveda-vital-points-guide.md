Preface

PREFACE The entire world consists of teachers for the wise, and enemies for the fools. Therefore the knowledge conducive to health, longevity, fame and excellence coming from any unfamiliar source should be received, assimilated and utilized with earnestness, as a step towards attaining perfection of Ayurveda. Charaka Samhita Science, is essentially a quest, an incessant, insatiable quest for knowledge. And as long as mysteries abound in the universe, the quest would go on. Rooted deep in experience and observation, the quest has progressed right from the times of primitive man started observing things around him, and has fledged out to limitless outerspace today, where he is observing things now. It is this impassionate quest which is the life line of science. Caught between the two extremes of the vanished past and the unknown future - it is this immanent zeal that impels us through the cornucopia of the secrets of life, is synonymous with.

Even in this age of sophistication the main tools of scientific enquiry remains essentially the same as it was ages ago experience and observation. As tools, these are as effective as ever because the fact remains that nature is inexorable and acts only through immutable laws which She never transgresses. It is in our attempt to identify and understand these laws that the various disciplines of science came about; one among which is medicine. Somewhere in the dark ages, when man started curiously observing the behavior of the wild animals and started experimenting with juices, fruits, flowers, leaves, roots and tubers of the plants around him, the seed of medicine was sown. In India, this scientific tempo was sustained for a long time and reached its peak around the period when the treatise began to be written - popularly known as the Samhita Kala. At the same time, elsewhere in the west, the quest to understand and to conquer nature developed an attitude of drifting away from its all encompassing effects somewhere during the Renaissance period. Rapid strides of scientific progress diverted the attention of the scientists into the eyepiece of the microscope. And away from the all important relationship of man and his environment, the holistic view of man as an epitome was totally forgotten. ii

Fortunately for India this was not the case. Here, it was this very concept of holism that formed the mainstay of both philosophical speculation as well as medicine. Thus when the western intelligentsia came up with the Germ theory, oriental seekers were speaking in simpler terms of the different forces and facets of nature, of the harmonious interplay of these in all living beings, of man himself as epitome of the universe, of ambient harmony as health, of dis-equilibrium, vitiation and disharmony as a disease, of Pancabhuta, of Tridosha and of Agni. Medicine was essentially an art to begin its with, even in the west. It remained so till the eighteenth century. It had long given up philosophical speculation with the advent of Hippocrates on the scene somewhere around 460 370B.C. Today when the line of demarcation is growing thinner and thinner day by day and blending imperceptibly into one another, the art of medicine comes to lie in the skillful application of all scientific disciplines to human problems. With the fast horizon of knowledge and the astonishing rate of growth of our understanding of the world around us, the wheel has come full circle. The more we understand life the more we realise that 111

there is ever more to it. We realize that the biological systems are indeed very complex and that life cannot be studied by dissecting it into minuscule units and in isolation. It has to be studied in toto; as a whole, as an inevitable and integral part of nature. This subtle but revolutionary paradigm shift, in the field of sciences, has had the salutary effect of shifting the focus once again on holism and oriental science like Ayurveda that had advanced, advocated, and adhered to this concept. Though belated, the realization has drawn intellects from all over the world to the oriental way of thinking and the theories and concepts it expounded. Now it has been recognized that traditional medicine, like the earth itself has special powers of healing particularly particularly the deep seated disorders, that was overlooked in the rush to develop a standardized technology based medicine. The concept of Marma in Ayurveda and Tamil manuscripts is one among the concepts that has attracted world wide attention. levance In Ayurveda the word marma refers to certain vital points on the human anatomy. It is a main part of Salyatantra. According to Susrutha these are the conglomeration of Mamsa, Asthi, Sira, Snayu, Sandhi. iv

Marmas are the seats of life and any injury to these marma points will invariably produce diverse symptoms of injuries even instantaneous death in some cases. In Tamil scriptures a very exhaustive as varmas are description of Varma points is given extensively used in warfare in general and self defence in particular. Also a vivid description is available in this literature on the treatment of each and every marmabhighata (injury to marma). These are mostly prevalent in the Nadar community and those who practise medical system are called as Varmani or Marmani. There is yet another type of practice which is mostly in Malabar, that is North Kerala called Abhyasa marma. This is mostly practised as a part of martial art and the ones who teach this are called Gurukkal. The present study is undertaken with a view to pin point various marma points, compare them anatomically and also to correlate these with Tamil and Kulayana schools of marma points. Though specific references to the treatment of marmabhighata are pitiably rare, if not lacking altogether, in classical Ayurvedic literature, the same has been extensively dealt with in the Tamil literature. It was felt that a study of the classical Tamil

literature and voluminous Ayurvedic exposition on the various aspects of marma with its special reference to its applied anatomy would be very much in the interest of updating the knowledge on the subject. I became interested in both these systems at an early age. My father, being a renowned practitioner in this particular field has always encouraged and exposed me to the depths of this vast treasure of knowledge. After having examined both systems in detail, it became clear to me that the differences between the two are more semantic than substantial, by changing a few key terms the corpus knowledge of one system can be translated into the other, thus widening the scope of each. This thesis consist of Preface Introduction 1. 2. 3. Classification of marma 4. Concept of Trimarmeeya 5. Comprehensive study of marma with its applied anatomy 6. Conclusion 7. Appendix 8. Introduction to Marmabhighata Samprapti (injured marmas)

Each and every disease has got its own samprapti. The study on the mechanism of the manifestation of disease is samprapti. Even a slight discomfort either physical or mental has got its own reasons. Disease is a derangement of the physiological systems. It is the ultimate outcome manifested as a result of derangements or physiological processes caused by the different nidanas undergone by the individual. According to Ayurveda, doshas, dhathus and malas are the fundamental factors which conduct the whole physiological processes of the body. It has already been mentioned that severe pain, deformity, or death will be resulted due to injuries on marmas. For such fatal result also, there should be some minor or major impairments in the physiological process. According to Vagbhata and Susruta the seven hundred siras in the body which are classified into 11

four as Vatha, Pitha, Kapha and Sudhasonithavahisiras nourish the whole body and they are said to be circulating all over the body centralizing on the marmas. Therefore the injuries to the marmas will cause trauma to the Siras (blood vessels) resulting in sudden raktasrava (hemorrhage). It is a fact that the nourishment of the whole body is through rakta (blood) and loss of blood due to the injuries results in dhathukshaya, causing the vitiation of vatha. The vitiated vatha causes severe pain and also the vitiation of other dosas, mainly the pitha. As a result of the vitiation of pitha thirst, dryness, giddiness, vertigo, perspiration and relaxation of joints occur. This results in the failure of the vital functions of the body causing death. 23 Susruta does not mention the vitiation of pitha. He observes that the four types of siras, being centralized on the marmas always nourishes the snayus, asthis, mamsas, sandhis, the ultimate result of these factors is the total nourishment of the body." 24 Though the siras are classified into four as vatha, pitha, kapha and sudha sonitha vahees, it is nothing but the vatha which conducts the circulation 12

25 of blood in the whole body. The vyanavayu being seated in the hrdaya conducts the circulation of blood all over the body. So whenever there is disturbance in the circulation, naturally the vatha will vitiate. The same thing will happen in marmabhighata also. When the marmas are injured, loss of blood occurs along with the vitiation of vatha. The vitiated vatha causes excruciating pain, and the patient becomes unconscious due to this and as a result of the failure of the whole system the individual dies. From the detailed description above it can be enumerated that the mechanism of fatal effects of injuries to marmas are as under :- 1. Excess loss of blood due to kshata of blood vessel. It is of two types namely primary shock and secondary shock. The primary shock is a neurovascular reaction, syncope or fainting which develops immediately after some injury and is usually transient. Secondary shock does not occur immediately after some injury, but is a type of circulatory failure due to agony and abnormal permeability of capillary wall, Anyhow, shock is the result of circulatory deficiency. Introduction to Marma

Ayurveda INTRODUCTION Early reference of the word Marma is seen in Atharva Veda. During vedic period knowledge of marma was known to kings and warriors. It was applied in battle fields to hit and achieve maximum fatal effect against their enemies. Therefore it can be assumed that this science was used both in warfare and surgery. An adequate knowledge of Anatomy and Physiology has been considered very essential for the exact study of surgical problems by Indian surgeons. During their study they found that injuries over certain parts of the body needed more attention and such areas were termed as Marmasthanas. Charaka says that those vaidyas who are well versed in Anatomy and Physiology of every organ will never commit mistakes in treatment due to ignorance.1 Charaka in another context says that a physician can be said to know Ayurveda only if he knows each and every aspects of the human body."

Susruta says that, one could be called visharada only after he attains thorough theoretical and practical knowledge of the human body." The knowledge of marma is the existing clear evidence of the vast knowledge on Anatomy acquired by ancient scholars of Indian Medicine. Etymology Etymologically it is said that, each letter of the word Marma has got a significant meaning. Ma means Prana or Vayu, The Repha indicates house or seat. Hence the word Marma means Seat of Vayu or Prana.ª Vaghbata expresses the same idea. 5 The term Marma is derived from the 6 Sanskrit term Mring marane or Mru prana tyage. That which causes death or death like similar miseries. 7 According to Dalhana the commentator of Susruta 8 Samhita, marma is that which kills. The Astanga Sangraha, Vahata describes the marma3 as that particular part of the body that which is fatally vulnerable to injury. Charaka says that the intensity of pain will be more on marmasthana when compared to the other parts of the body. This is because the chetana or prana is situated here. 10 2

In Raja nighantu it is said marma is the 11 sthana of life. In Apte's Sanskrit English dictionary this word is explained as a vital point of the body. Definition 12 Vaghbata is of the view that structures which show irregular pulsation and where the pain on pressure persists can be labelled as Marmasthana. He also opines that Marma is the place where mamsa (muscle), sira and dhamani (blood vessels), snayu (ligaments and nerves), asthi (bone) and sandhi (joints) meet. 13 According to Susruta, marma is conglomeration of anatomical structures namely mamsa (muscle), sira (blood vessels), snayu (ligaments and nerves), asthi (bone) and sandhi (joints). 14 The above statements clearly help in deriving a conclusion that there are certain very vital anatomical points in the body, which are having a secret and significant life values and they are composed of nerves, muscles, blood vessels, joints, ligament and bones. It is not necessary that all these structures should be present collectively at a time for the composition of marma. Even if only two structures are present it may constitute a marma point.

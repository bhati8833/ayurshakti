## 15. Part 8.1 - Contents of the Agni Purāṇa

Agriculture and Animal husbandry in the Puranas

This page relates ‘Contents of the Agni Purana’ of the study on agriculture and animal husbandry in the Puranas—a category of Sanskrit literature describing the culture and heritage of ancient Indian society. Besides detailing animal care, the six chapters of this essay also documents various Puranic agricultural science: ploughing, sowing, soil-types/fertility tests, transplantation, manuring, irrigation/water-management, tanks, rainfall–Sun dependence, horticulture, plus crop lists (e.g., Vrihi, Yava, Godhuma) and festivals/rites.

Part 8.1 - Contents of the Agni Purāṇa

According to the Matsya Purāṇa, the Purāṇa which begins with the description of Īśāna kalpa and which is narrated to Vasiṣṭha by Agni is called as the Agni Purāṇa.[1] In the Agni Purāṇa also it is defined that the Purāṇa which god Agni narrated to the sage Vasiṣṭha is known as Agni Purāṇa.[2] The Agni Purāṇa contains the great and eternal science of Brahmā, which is invisible, incomprehensible and stable and is the reason of fish and other forms. This Purāṇa was narrated to Agni by Viṣṇu, and in the ancient time it was narrated by Brahmā to the celestials.[3] The Padma Purāṇa mentions the Agni Purāṇa as a Tāmasika Purāṇa.[4] This Purāṇa is the best of all shapes that Viṣṇu had ever been assumed. According to the Agni Purāṇa itself the God Janārdana is the author and hearer of the Agni Purāṇa. Hence it is the essence of all the Vedas.[5] Variation occurs regarding the number of the verses of the Agni Purāṇa. Even this variation occurs in the Agni Purāṇa itself. In the last chapter of the Agni Purāṇa it is stated that this Purāṇa contains fifteen thousand ślokas.[6] But in an another chapter it mentions that this Purāṇa contains twelve thousand ślokas.[7] Again, the Matsya Purāṇa states that the Agni Purāṇa contains 16000 verses.[8] There are three hundred and eighty three chapters in the Agni Purāṇa.

Considering himself as Viṣṇu, Kālāgni and Rudra the god Agni converses the Agni Purāṇa which is the core of learning, best of all sciences and the source of all. Moreover, which contains accounts of creation, dissolution, various families, periods of Manus and genealogies etc.[9] The Agni Purāṇa which is excellent among the Purāṇas and which is known as Āgneya (or Agni), gives satisfaction to all the celestials. The Purāṇa is spoken by Agni and designated by the Vedas as Āgneya. It gives Bhukti (enjoyment) and Mukti (salvation) to those people, who read or hear it.

The Agni Purāṇa discusses about all branches of knowledge.[10] Knowledge according to the Agni Purāṇa is of two sorts-Parā Vidyā (the superior) and Aparā Vidyā (the inferior). The Vedas namely the Ṛgveda, the Yajurveda, the Sāmaveda and the Atharvaveda, the Vedāṅgas (the six auxiliaries of the Vedas), namely Śikṣā (phonetics), Kalpa (rules governing rituals), Vyākaraṇa (grammar), Nirukta (etymological science), Chanda (science of prosody) and Jyotiṣa (the science dealing with the movement of the luminary bodies), Abhidhāna (lexicon), Mīmāṃsā (investigation of the interpretation of the rituals of the Vedas), Dharmaśāstras(law-books), Purāṇas (18 in number), Nyāya (logical philosophical system), Vaidya (Medical Science), Gandharva (Musical Science), Dhanurveda (the science of archery) and Arthaśāstra (polity)-these all belong to Aparā Vidyā. The Parā Vidyā is that by which Brahman is realized.[11] This Purāṇa deals with the great and eternal science of Brahmā which is invisible, incomprehensible and stable.[12]

The Agni Purāṇa is sacred and admirable. It gives pleasure, satisfaction and liberation to men.[13] The superiority of Agni Purāṇa is declared in the last chapter of this Purāṇa itself. There is nothing richer in substance than the Agni Purāṇa. Nothing is a better friend than this Purāṇa. There is no greater treatise than this Purāṇa. It is the best of the scriptures, the best of the Smṛtis and the best of the philosophies. There is no superior knowledge than this Purāṇa. This Purāṇa is extremely auspicious. It is the foremost among the Purāṇas.[14] The Agni Purāṇa encloses chapter on Harivaṃśa,[15] incarnations of lord Viṣṇu as Matsya etc. (matsyāvatārādyā), Gītā, Rāmāyaṇa (gītā rāmāyaṇaṃ tviha), Mahābhārata(harivaṃśo bhārataṃ ca) etc.[16] Moreover, it deals with Āgamas of the Śaiva, Śākta and Saura, Maṇḍalas, Vāstuśāstra, various types of Mantras, Pratisarga, Brahmāṇḍa, Dvīpa, Bhuvanakoṣa etc.[17] Discussions on topics like Astrology, victory in war, Manvantaras, Prāyaścitta, Rājadharma, Dānadharma, Vratas, Sūryavaṃśa, Somavaṃśa, Dhanurveda, Āyurveda, Gāndharvaveda, Arthaśāstra, Mīmāṃsā, Nyāya etc. are also met with in this Purāṇa.[18]

Part 8.5 - Contents of the Viṣṇu Purāṇa

The Viṣṇu Purāṇa is a sectarian work belonging to the Pañcatantras, and purely Vaiṣṇava from beginning to end.[1] In this work Viṣṇu is praised and glorified as the supreme being, as the one and only god with whom also Brahmā and Śiva are identical.[2] The Viṣṇu Purāṇa is an early work composed most probably in the last quarter of the third or the first quarter of the fourth century A.D.[3] Pargiter in his Ancient Indian Historical Tradition, too opines that it is a late Purāṇa composed as a single whole upon a consistent plan, and not a collection of materials of various times, as we find in the Vāyu, Brahma and Matsya. From its account of Buddhism, and Jainism it appears to have been composed after Brāhmaṇism had recovered its supremacy, so that it cannot be earlier than about the 5th century A.D., and it is Brāhmaṇical.[4] According to the Agni Purāṇa, the Viṣṇu Purāṇa was narrated by sage Parāśara. It deals with the events which were occurred in the cycle of time known as the Varāha-kalpa. This Purāṇa contains twenty three thousand verses.[5] In the last chapter of the Viṣṇu Purāṇa it is declared that this Purāṇa is the best of all preservatives for one who is frightened of worldly reality and this is the remover of all deficiencies.[6]

The origin of the Viṣṇu Purāṇa is cited in the last chapter of the Viṣṇu Purāṇa itself. According to it, this Purāṇa was originally composed by the sage Nārāyaṇa. Then it was spoken by Brahmā to Ṛbhu. Ṛbhu imparted it to Priyavrata. Priyavrata then communicated it to Bhāguri.[7] Bhāguri gave it to Tambamitra, and Tambamitra to Dadhīci. Dadhīci recited it to Sārasvata, Sārasvata gave it to Bhṛgu.[8] Bhṛgu imparted it to Purukutsa, who then communicated it to Narmadā. Narmadā imparted it to the Nāga king Dhṛtarāṣṭra, and to Apūraṇa of the same race,[9] by whom it was then communicated to their monarch Vāsuki. Vāsuki imparted it to Vatsa, and Vatsa to Aśvatara.[10] Aśvatara repeated it to Kambala and Elāpatra After that the sage Vedaśirā going down to Pātāla, received the whole Purāṇa from the Nāgas.[11] Vedaśirā gave it to Pramati, and Pramati communicated it to intelligent Jātūkarṇa.[12] Jātūkarṇa taught it to some other holy persons, and through Vasiṣṭha’s consents it came to Parāśara and Parāśara finally imparted it to Maitreya.[13] It is divided into six books called aṃśas.

Again, each of the parts consists of a number of chapters.

(1) The first book is occupied chiefly with the details of creation, primary (sarga) and secondary (pratisarga); the first i.e., sarga explains how the universe proceeds from prakṛti or eternal crude matter; the second i.e., pratisarga explains in what manner the forms of things are developed from the elementary substances previously evolved, or how they reappear after their temporary destruction.[14] This book begins with a dialogue between Parāśara, the grandson of Vasiṣṭha and his pupil Maitreya. Maitreya inquires Parāśara, the origin and nature of the universe.

This book deals with details of creation, recreation[15], measure of time[16], origin of the four castes[17],origin of Rudra[18], legend of the churning of ocean[19], legend of Dhruva[20], story of Vena and Pṛthu[21], legend of Prahlāda[22], etc.

(2) The second book opens with a continuation of the kings of the first Manvantara; amongst whom, Bharata is said to have given a name to India, called, after him, Bhārata-varṣa.[23] It gives a fantastic description of the world at first[24]. The seven continents and the seven oceans are described, in the midst of which Jambudvīpa is found with the golden mountain of Meru-the abode of the gods. In Jambudvīpa lies Bhāratavarṣa i.e. India whose countries, mountains and rivers are enumerated. In connection with the name Bhāratavarṣa, then[25] a legend is told of the ancient king Bharata.

(3) The third book deals firstly with the account of the several Manus and Manvantaras, then, names of the twenty-eight Vyāsas who lived in different ages, division of the Vedas[26],duties of religious student, householder, hermit[27], funeral sacrifices,[28] etc.

(4) The fourth book contains all that the Hindus have of their ancient history. It is a tolerably comprehensive list of dynasties and individuals: it is a barren record of events.[29] It deals with dynasties of the kings, birth of Ikṣvāku,[30] dynasty of the Moon,[31] dynasties of the kings of the Kali age,[32] etc.

(5) The fifth book of the Viṣṇu Purāṇa is exclusively occupied with the life of Kṛṣṇa.[33] It is the longest book in this Purāṇa which gives us the account of marriage of Vāsudeva and Devakī, Yogamāyā who enters into the womb of Yaśodā and Hari who enters into the womb of Devakī,[34] holding of Govardhana Mountain by Kṛṣṇa,[35] Kṛṣṇa’s marriage with Rukmiṇī,[36] destruction of the Yadu race[37] etc.

The last book contains an account of the dissolution of the world, in both its major and minor cataclysms; and, in the particulars of the end of all things by fire and water, as well as in the principle of their perpetual renovation, presents a faithful exhibition of opinions that were general in the ancient world.[38] The sixth book is the shortest among all the books. It describes the nature of the Kali age,[39] nature of elemental dissolution (prakṛta pralaya),[40] description of Brahma yoga, and the greatness of the Viṣṇu Purāṇa.[41]

## 19. Part 3 - Importance of Agriculture

Agriculture and Animal husbandry in the Puranas

This page relates ‘Importance of Agriculture’ of the study on agriculture and animal husbandry in the Puranas—a category of Sanskrit literature describing the culture and heritage of ancient Indian society. Besides detailing animal care, the six chapters of this essay also documents various Puranic agricultural science: ploughing, sowing, soil-types/fertility tests, transplantation, manuring, irrigation/water-management, tanks, rainfall–Sun dependence, horticulture, plus crop lists (e.g., Vrihi, Yava, Godhuma) and festivals/rites.

Part 3 - Importance of Agriculture

In the Kṛṣi-parāśara the importance of kṛṣi is very aptly brought out by Parāśara. According to Parāśara, agriculture is holy and it is the life of all the creatures—

kṛṣirdhanyā kṛṣirmedhyā jantūnāṃ jīvanaṃ kṛṣiḥ.[1]

One who is related to agriculture can become a king—

kṛṣyanvito hi loke’smin bhūyādekaśca bhūpatiḥ.[2]

Agriculture is the only means of livelihood for which one is not compelled to be a beggar—

ekayā ca punaḥ kṛṣyā prārthako naiva jāyate.[3]

Parāśara again states that rice, which is the main food of the majority of the people all over the world, is produced from the agricultural crop paddy and without agriculture paddy cannot be produced—

annaṃ hi dhānya sañjātaṃ dhānyaṃ kṛṣyā vinā na ca.[4]

It is rice which besides providing sustenance to the gods, demons and human beings (devāsuramanuṣyāśca sarve cānnopajīvinaḥ) constitutes the very vitality (annaṃ prāṇā), vigour (balaṃ cānnam) and the means of fulfillment of all the ends of life (annaṃ sarvārtha sādhanam).[5] A person may be with gold ornaments in his neck, ears and hands (kaṇṭhe karṇe ca haste ca suvarṇaṃ vidyate yadi), when denied of rice, is inevitably threatened with starvation and death (upavāsastathāpi syādannābhāvena dehīnām).[6]

The Taittirīya Upaniṣad too states that living beings are born from food; when born, by food they grow.It is fed upon, and it feeds on beings; so it is called food—

annādbhūtāni jāyante/ jātānyannena vardhante/ adyate’tti ca bhūtāni/ tasmādannaṃ taducyata iti.[7]

It also adds that one shall not condemn food; that shall be his vow (annaṃ na nindyāt/tadvratam).Life, verily, is food(prāṇo vā annam), the body the eater of food (śarīramannādam). In life the body is set (prāṇe śarīraṃ pratiṣṭhitam); life is set in the body(śarīre prāṇaḥ pratiṣṭhitaḥ).Thus, food is set in food(tadetadannamanne pratiṣṭhitam).[8] Therefore, Parāśara has enjoined the people to take up agriculture at the cost of everything else.[9] Thus, importance of the agricultural products in general and of rice as a daily food in particular is emphasized by Parāśara because it is the agricultural crop, especially rice, that keeps the population alive by providing it with food and nourishment.

In the Purāṇic society, agriculture was a means to produce food grain so that human beings and other living creatures can sustain their life.

The Viṣṇu Purāṇa states that agriculture is the survival of farmers—

karṣakāṇāṃ kṛṣirvṛttiḥ.[10]

According to the Vāyu Purāṇa, agriculture along with the profession of trading and animal husbandry was not for Brāhmaṇas.[11] The Vāyu Purāṇa mentions that of the four castes, the Vaiśyas were specially connected with agriculture and commerce.[12] The Bhāgavata Purāṇa mentions that the main profession of the Vaiśyas was to carry on business (vārtā-vṛtti). Vārtā comprises of tending cattle, tilling agricultural fields, carrying on trade and lending for profit.[13] The Vāyu Purāṇa states that Svayambhū Brahmā devised plans for the purpose of agricultural cultivation.[14] He ordained animal husbandry or cattle rearing, trade and agriculture for the Vaiśyas.[15] The Viṣṇu Purāṇa states that Brahmā, the great parent of creation, gave to the Vaiśyas, the occupation of commerce and agriculture and the feeding of flocks and herds, for means of livelihood.[16] The Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa too points out that agriculture and tending of cattle were the means of livelihood for the Vaiśyas.[17] The custom of field-gifting is also met with in the Purāṇas.The Agni Purāṇa states that by making the gift of a field in full harvest, to a Brāhmaṇa, a man attains everything.[18] It is also mentioned in the Viṣṇu Purāṇa that in the Kali age, oppressed by famine and taxation, men will desert their native lands, and go to those countries which are fit for coarse grains.[19]

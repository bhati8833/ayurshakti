## 3. Preface

Agriculture and Animal husbandry in the Puranas

This page relates ‘Preface’ of the study on agriculture and animal husbandry in the Puranas—a category of Sanskrit literature describing the culture and heritage of ancient Indian society. Besides detailing animal care, the six chapters of this essay also documents various Puranic agricultural science: ploughing, sowing, soil-types/fertility tests, transplantation, manuring, irrigation/water-management, tanks, rainfall–Sun dependence, horticulture, plus crop lists (e.g., Vrihi, Yava, Godhuma) and festivals/rites.

Preface

Since the ancient period, Agriculture and allied activities have been providing livelihood for most of the population. Agriculture and Animal Husbandry can neither be regarded as an industry, nor a means to make profit. In fact Agriculture and Animal Husbandry is the mainstay of living beings. For at least the last 4500 years the Indian society has been primarily an Agricultural one. The ancient practice of Agriculture and Animal Husbandry are still very much popular. Even in the 21st century this ancient practice is much more relevant. Among the ancient treatises, the Vedas and the Purāṇas may be regarded as the source of inspiration for the practice of Agriculture and Animal Husbandry in India.

Right from the days of doing my post-graduation in Sanskrit, I had a keen desire for doing research on such an uncommon topic which has relevance even in this era of science and technology. From then on, when I moved towards my revered teacher Dr. Nalini Devi Misra, Professor, Gauhati University, she guided me to select the topic and encouraged me to go for the same. The Purāṇas were selected to do research with, because it was noticed that regarding Agriculture and Animal husbandry in the Vedas, a lot of books are available. But the Purāṇas are seen having no specific books on the subjects cited above. Moreover, the Purāṇic records regarding Agriculture and Animal husbandry are scattered here and there. Therefore, a keen interest developed in me for doing a detailed and systematic study covering the major Purāṇas and thus, a modest attempt has been made to set up a thesis in five chapters on the topic entitled–Agriculture and Animal Husbandry in the Purāṇas—A Study, for the degree of Ph.D. of the University of Gauhati. But due to the limitation of space and time all the eighteen major Purāṇas are not included in my dissertation. Only five major Purāṇas have been covered mainly. They are the Agni Purāṇa, Bhāgavata Purāṇa, Matsya Purāṇa, Vāyu Purāṇa, and Viṣṇu Purāṇa. For this systematic study, various books on Agriculture and Animal Husbandry have helped me a lot. I have taken the design of arranging the methods orderly from those books, but the investigation is purely mine. The less data the Purāṇas have regarding Agriculture have been incorporated in the second chapter and Animal Husbandry has been included in the fourth chapter. It is hoped that this humble effort will be of some interests for the lovers of Sanskrit literature with regard to the role of the Purāṇas in the field of Agriculture and Animal Husbandry.

With these prefatory words, I with all modesty and sincerity put forward this work to the learned world. It is the outcome of my academic quest for last six years under the supervision of my revered preceptor, Prof. Nalini Devi Misra. I shall consider rewarded if this work can arise some curiosities in the minds of the readers.

## 18. Part 1 - Introduction and history of ancient Indian Agriculture

Agriculture and Animal husbandry in the Puranas

This page relates ‘Introduction and history of ancient Indian Agriculture’ of the study on agriculture and animal husbandry in the Puranas—a category of Sanskrit literature describing the culture and heritage of ancient Indian society. Besides detailing animal care, the six chapters of this essay also documents various Puranic agricultural science: ploughing, sowing, soil-types/fertility tests, transplantation, manuring, irrigation/water-management, tanks, rainfall–Sun dependence, horticulture, plus crop lists (e.g., Vrihi, Yava, Godhuma) and festivals/rites.

Part 1 - Introduction and history of ancient Indian Agriculture

Agriculture is one of the most essential aspects of human life. Agriculture and allied activities have been providing subsistence for most of the population, since the ancient period. Agriculture came to be practised when man gave up his nomadic habits and settled in favourable climate and topography.Initially depending on wild roots, fruits and seeds for his sustenance, man eventually adopted the practice of tilling the land to grow crops.[1] Archeologists and historians opine that in the Indian subcontinent, agricultural activities began around 7000 BC in the Neolithic period.The only remains of agricultural settlements of that period are found at Mehrgarh, i.e. presently situated in Baluchistan of Pakistan. But if the climatic condition, ecological situation, implements of hunting etc., mentioned in the Zend Avesta (the sacred book of Zoroastrianism) of the Middle East (the transcontinental region centered on Western Asia, Turkey and Egypt) and in the Ṛgveda of India are compared, it becomes clear that the evolution of agricultural practices in India simultaneously took place with the Middle East about 10,000 BC.[2] Both these holy treatises are of the same period and deal with the same thought regarding agricultural practices. Although it has not been ascertained when the early inhabitants of India took the farming as their chief occupation, the practice of agriculture has been traced back to the Indus Valley Civilization.Therefore, for at least the last 4500 years the Indian society has been primarily an agricultural one.[3]

Although Indus Valley Civilization, according to available facts, is supposed to be an urban civilization, yet agriculture was the mainstay of life and provided sound base to their economy. The archaeological remains of large granaries of Harappa and Mahenjo-daro prove it that agricultural activities systematically developed fully.[4] The discovery, of some stone implements ‘much too heavy to have served as weapons’ suggests that these were ploughshares ‘quite efficient in the stoneless alluvial soil of the Indus plains’.[5] Two incomplete curved blades of copper recovered from Mahenjo-daro were probably used as sickles for harvesting purposes.[6]

Harappan people cultivated several cereals, pulses, oilseeds, fibre crops, fruits and vegetables.They knew crops of different seasons like kharif and rabi.Among cereals they cultivated wheat, barley, rice, jowar, bajra(pearl millet), ragi(finger millet), foxtail(Italian millet);among pulses lentil, chick pea(Bengal gram), field pea, green gram, black gram; linseed, mustard, sesame and among fibre crops they cultivated cotton.[7] Information of animal husbandry is also found. The figure of a bull on a Mahenjo-daro seal is interpreted as indicating that great importance was attached to cattle breeding in those days.The skeletal remains of the humped bull, buffalo, sheep, elephant, and camel unearthed in the Indus valley area show that these animals were domesticated.[8]

Agriculturists in ancient India were quite aware of the nature of soil and its relations to the production of a particular crop of economic importance. The Aryans were an active pastoral people and agriculture was the backbone of economy at that time.[9] According to the historians, the Aryan tribes entered India from Central Asia in the 2nd millennium BC.[10] The Vedas are the main source of information regarding agricultural activities practiced by the Aryans. The Vedas attach great importance to agriculture. Among the ancient Vedic treatises mainly the Ṛgveda records various aspects of agriculture. In the Ṛgveda, the gambler is advised to stop gambling and engage himself in agriculture to improve his condition.[11] The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa has mentioned the four principal operations of agriculture. They are ploughing, sowing, reaping and threshing.[12] It also refers to the practice of yoking six, twelve or twenty-four bullocks.[13] The epic Rāmāyaṇa clearly states that people, who are engaged in agriculture and cattle rearing, can live happily and can prosper in life.[14] The Purāṇas too provide many additional aspects regarding agriculture.These are discussed in details in this chapter.

Part 20 - Ancient Indian Horticulture (the science of planting)—Introduction

The science of planting is very ancient in India. Its full-fledged development has been mentioned in the Arthaśāstra, which states that the Sītādhyakṣa must be possessed of knowledge of the science of agriculture dealing with the plantation of plants being assisted by those who are trained in this science.[1] The art or practice of garden cultivation and management is known as Horticulture. It is a branch of agriculture that deals with the cultivation of garden plants-generally fruits, vegetables, flowers, and ornamentals such as plants used for landscaping. Propagation, the controlled perpetuation of plants, is the most basic horticultural practice.Its objectives are to increase the numbers of a plant and to preserve its essential characteristics.Successful horticulture depends on extensive control of the environment, including light, water, temperature, soil structure and fertility, and pests.[2] Tree-planting in pleasure gardens indicated developed horticulture in the Purāṇas.

Planting:

The Bhaviṣya Purāṇa states that a person who plants a tree, that blooms with flowers and fruits and gives shade, in a temple or on a road, helps the release of the ancestors from any sin they might have committed.[3] In the Agni Purāṇa, Dhanvantari deals with the hygienic effects of planting various trees and shrubs around a residence. Moreover, many procedures of sprinkling different cereals for promoting the growth of plants and trees are prescribed in this Purāṇa.[4] It is pointed out there that the plakṣa trees (yellow fig tree; Ficus infectoria), should be planted in the north of a residence, the vaṭas(banyan tree) in the east, the mangoes on the south and the aśvatthas(pepul) on the west.[5] In the Brahma Purāṇa, plakṣa was crowned by Brahmā as the king of vegetables.[6] According to the Agni Purāṇa the aforesaid trees planted and grown in those particular directions is favourable for its master.[7]

On the other hand, the thorny shrubs should be planted in the Southern boundary of a house.[8] Apart from general care of trees and flowery creepers, garden care is also attested.In the context of characteristics of a good house, it has been mentioned that at the left of a house a garden should be nurtured[9] and blooming plants of sesamum orientate should be cultivated in the flower gardens.[10] The trees should be planted after having worshipped the Brāhmaṇas and their sovereign, the Moon-god and under the auspices of the asterisms presided over by the Wind god, Prajāpati and the god Viṣṇu respectively or of those known as the Mūlā, the Hastā and the Dhruvas.[11] Trees planted in rows, twenty cubits apart, should be considered the best planted, while those having a space of sixteen or ten cubits left intervening between them, should be considered ranking second best in respect of fruitfulness.[12] Trees should be transplanted twelve times, they should not be planted too close and if the branches touch one another, they should be lopped off with a chopper, as otherwise they would bear no fruits.[13] In case where barrenness would be apprehended, the leaves and branches should be sprinkled over with a solution of powdered viḍaṅga, clarified butter, pulverized mudga, māṣa and kulattha pulse.[14]

Watering

It is mentioned in the Purāṇas that the trees should be sprinkled with cold water, containing a solution of viḍaṅga (a medicinal plant) and clarified butter.[15] The trees are said to be grown by being sprinkled with fish-water.[16] Fish and meat, powdered and mixed with a quantity of pulverized viḍaṅga, should be sprinkled over a tree for the purpose of curing it of all distempers.[17] If the fruit-bearing trees get their fruit ruined, it is suggested there in the Agni Purāṇa, to sprinkle a mixture of kulattha, māṣa, mudga, tila or yava and cold water saturated with clarified butter over it. By doing so they are abundant with bumper flowers and fruits.[18] It is advised there in the Agni Purāṇa that any trees which are carefully planted in a garden, should be watered each morning and evening during the summer season, on each alternate day during the winter season and at the break of dawn during the rainy season, when the earth would get dry and require watering.[19]

## 63. Part 5 - The Cow in the Purāṇas—Introduction

Agriculture and Animal husbandry in the Puranas

This page relates ‘Cow in the Puranas—Introduction’ of the study on agriculture and animal husbandry in the Puranas—a category of Sanskrit literature describing the culture and heritage of ancient Indian society. Besides detailing animal care, the six chapters of this essay also documents various Puranic agricultural science: ploughing, sowing, soil-types/fertility tests, transplantation, manuring, irrigation/water-management, tanks, rainfall–Sun dependence, horticulture, plus crop lists (e.g., Vrihi, Yava, Godhuma) and festivals/rites.

Part 5 - The Cow in the Purāṇas—Introduction

The cow and the horse were the most important of the domesticated animals. The respect for the cow which is seen in the Vedas[1] is prominent in the Purāṇas. The Vāyu Purāṇa speaks that the cow, which symbolizes the gāyatrī metre[2], had its birth from the belly of the Creator.[3] In the Agni Purāṇa, Dhanvantari suggests that a king should preserve the breed of cows in his country.[4] According to him the cows are holy and propitious.[5] The benefits of donating cows to a Brāhmaṇa are mentioned in the Matsya Purāṇa.[6] It states there that a cow should be given along with her calf and utensils made of kāṃsya. Kāṃsyam is fit to be a milking vessel and it is used for gift along with a cow.[7] The giving away of such a cow results in immense good advantages. The Matsya Purāṇa states that the cow is like the Earth with its mountains, forests, etc., till it bears calf in her womb. At that time one who gives it away he gets the profits of having given away the land with four oceans.[8] The urine of the cow is endowed with the quality of neutralizing all sorts of poison.[9] The body of a cow always has a pleasant smell.[10] The cows are the daughters of heavenly cow Surabhi and mothers of the three worlds. It is stated in the Agni Purāṇa that all animals depend on the cow for their nourishment. The cow serves the gods with the food they live upon, which only consists of libations of clarified butter offered to them during a religious sacrifice.[11] The gods in the heaven are pleased with the libations of clarified butter prepared from the milk of a cow which are offered to them in the course of a sacrifice, consecrated with the Mantras of the Vedas. The gods show food on earth in return of the libations of clarified butter which are offered to them.[12]

About the purity of the cow it is said in the Purāṇas that except the mouth her whole body is pure.[13] Their backs are also considered pure.[14] The cow is the sole purifying principle among all other animals.[15] The place which has once been sprinkled over with the urine of a cow, bad luck never effects that place.[16] Cows provide the best nourishment to all sorts of animal.

They are the holiest and the best of all auspicious sights. They are the stepping stones to heaven and the most blessed animal—

gāvaḥ pavitraṃ paramaṃ gāvo māṅgalyamuttamam/ 
gāvaḥ svargasya sopānaṃ gāvo dhanyāḥ sanātanāḥ//[17]

Cows are mentioned as the abodes of all beauty, they are also considered the holiest daughters of the god of creation i.e. Prajāpati.[18] The cows and the Brāhmaṇas form the two branches of the same family. The mantras reside in the Brāhmaṇas and the libations reside in the cows.[19] In the Garuḍa Purāṇa, the Lord prays that the cows may remain ever in his heart.May the cow, the majesty of all living beings, dispel his sins. Again the Goddess Lakṣmī is prayed to assume the form of a cow and remove the sins.[20] Even the cow-stall was also considered sacred in the Purāṇas.

At the śrāddha ceremony if fire is not available to offer into, then the offering may be placed in a cow-stall.[21] The Agni Purāṇa enjoins that the pools from where a cow would drink should be regarded as a holy place.[22] The ground on which cow stands upon was considered sacred. No sort of diseases could spoil that ground which was purified by the breath of a cow.[23] If a cow comes in front in the course of journey, it was deemed as a good scene.[24] The tail of a cow was waved round the face of a person for auspiciousness. The term gopucchabhramaṇam, indicates waving of cow’s tail over children to remove fear from their minds, which was an ancient Yādava practice.[25] Goḥ karīṣam refers to dried cow dung placed on the head of afflicted children to remove evils; which was also an ancient Yādava practice.[26] Yakṣī, one of the ten pīṭhas for images with three mekhalās, is the Vedī of prosperity.It gives one plenty of cows.[27]

## 78. Part 19 - Horse in the Purāṇas—Introduction

Part 19 - Horse in the Purāṇas—Introduction

The horse is said to have born of the Creator’s feet.[1] When the king Pṛthu, occupied the place of the presiding deity of the universe, at that time Hiraṇyagarbha Brahmā made Uccaiḥśravā the lord of horses, the bull the lord of cows and Gajendra Airāvata the lord of elephants.[2] Uccaiḥśravā, who came out of the churning of the ocean,[3] was the vehicle of the Devas.[4] The horse Uccaiḥśravā was of different colours, such as white, red, tawny, deer-coloured, green and grey.[5]

Breeds of Horses:

Four varieties of horses are mentioned in the Agni Purāṇa. They are—

1) Bhadra—the type of horse known as bhadra could be trained well.
2) Manda—the manda could be trained well by making use of a stick.
3) Mṛga—the type of horse known as mṛga is classified taking the shank of a deer as the basis, and
4) Saṅkīrṇa—the above characteristics would be found mixed in the saṅkīrṇa class.[6]

The Agni Purāṇa classifies the horses into four classes—

1) Brāhmaṇa—The horse that eats sugar, honey and fried rice and has a good smell is said to be clean and belonging to the brāhmaṇa class.
2) Kṣatriya—The horse that belongs to the kṣatriya class would be shiny, docile and intelligent.
3) Vaiśya—If the above qualities are found in lesser proportions it is known as the vaiśya class, and
4) Śūdra—The horse that is impure, unsteady, dull, ugly, foolish and wicked belongs to the śūdra class.[7]

The horses with [the following] signs are well known as pañcolūkhalikā [pañca-ulūkhalika].—

gomūtra,

kuṭilā,

veṇī,

padamaṇḍala and

mālikā.

These types of horses are excellent in their duties.[8]

Characteristics of Horses:

Various characteristics of horses are mentioned in the Purāṇas. Inauspicious and bad qualities are also mentioned there. According to the Agni Purāṇa, a horse with lesser number of teeth, one that is without teeth, one that is having shocking appearance, one that has a black sense of taste and black tongue, one that is born as a twin, one that has no testicle, one that has a cracked-hoof, one that has a horn, one that is tricoloured, one that has the colour of a tiger, an ass and ash, one that isn't having an excellent colour, one that has a lump, one that has white scrapes on the skin, one that is ridden by the crows, the voice of which looks like the voice of an ass, one whose eyes looks like the eyes of a monkey, one that has black manes, one whose anus is black, one whose nostrils have a black tone and are pointed, one that has the colour of a tittiri bird and one that has imbalanced legs having white scrapes on the foot,one that is without settled rings and has rings showing ominousness, ought to be avoided.[9]

Ten hair-whirls are said to be auspicious; two each at the skull and flanks. Two each at the head and at the chest, one each at the forehead and at the neck.[10] The whirls at the following places are considered most auspicious-at the ends of the mouth, at the forehead, at the root of the ear, at the throat, and at the roots of the arms; but at other places they are inauspicious.[11] The horses that are of the color of a parrot, the indragupa insects or the Moon, those that have colour like that of the crow, of the color of the hawk, and having a soft view are excellent.[12] Those having long necks, elongated eyes, prominent heads and short ears are good for the kings for victory at wars.[13] The horses that possess the above enumerated signs must be kept in the stud by which the offspring and possessions of its master would increase;otherwise misfortune would come to its master.[14] The horse is considered the holiest of the beasts. Therefore, it is sacrificed in the Aśvamedha.[15]

Various details are contemplated at various parts in the body of a horse, and in his other characteristics.[16] They are as follows:

a) In his mind the god Brahmā (brahmā citte),
b) In strength Viṣṇu (bale viṣṇuḥ),
c) In advances Garuḍa (vainateyaḥ parākrame)[17],
d) At the sides the Rudras (pārśve rudrā),
e) In intellect Guru (gururbuddhau),
f) At his vitals Viśvedevas (viśvedevāśca marmasu)[18],
g) At the whirl, at the curves of eyes the moon, at the sight the sun (dṛgāvarte dṛśīndvarkau),
h) At the ears Aśvins (karṇayoraśvinau tathā)[19],
i) At his stomach the Agni (jaṭhare’gniḥ),
j) In perspiration Svadhā (svadhā svede),
k) At the tongue Vāc (vāgjihvāyāṃ),
l) In the speed Anila (jave’nilaḥ)[20],
m) At his back the plain of heaven (pṛṣṭhato nākapṛṣṭhaḥ),
n) At the hooves all mountains(khurāgre sarvaparvatāḥ),[21]
o) At pores on his body the stars(tārāśca romakūpeṣu),
p) In heart the digit of the Moon (hṛdi cāndramasī kalā),[22] q) In ability Agni (tejasyagnī),
r) At the buttocks the goddess of love (ratiḥ śroṇyāṃ), s) At the fore-head the lord of the world (lalāṭe ca jagatpatiḥ),[23]
t) At his neigh the planets (grahāśca heṣite caiva) and
u) In the chest the chief of the serpents Vāsuki (tathaivorasi vāsukiḥ).[24]

Positions of Horses:

Various postures of horse are referred to in the Agni Purāṇa. They are—

1) Lokana—Having taken a firm seat on the saddle, the horse should be made to touch his legs with his face.This position is known as Lokanam.[26]

2) Udvakkana—Again, the posture known as the Udvakkanam consists in firmly clutching the two legs round the waist of a horse.[27]

3) Tāḍana—It is considered Tāḍanam if there is binding of the legs with the rein and letting it go by freeing the fore-legs.[28]

4) Moṭana—A wise man should know the loss of consciousness and destruction of a horse and the fourth rule known as the Moṭanam. A horse whose legs do not touch the ground in a small circle, that foot should be restrained by means of Moṭanam and Udvakkanam.[29]

5) Saṅgrahaṇa—It is said to be Saṅgrahaṇam when it is held with excertion and when one goes slowly holding it after fastening well on the seat.[30]

6) Grāhya-kaṇṭakapāyana—The posture known as Grāhya kaṇṭakapāyanam consists in hitting a horse in its flank and then holding the bridle with the feet.[31]

7) Hanana—Hananam consists in hitting with a stick, the leg of a horse, not making steps in a desired way, while running in one of the three motions, such as the trot, the gallop or the canter.[32]

8) Ucchvāsana—Ucchvāsanam consists in leading a horse to a different place, with its mouth restrained by means of rectangular bridle,[33] and

9) Mukhavyāvartana—It is considered Mukhavyāvartana, if the nature of a horse to turn his face and move towards the place from which it has been taken out, is restrained and held.[34]

After restraining by any one of the three ways in order, the horse should be controlled by taking to the courses such as the circles and the like in order.[35]

Favourable Period for Riding a Horse:

A horse should be first ridden under the influence of the asterisms aśvinī, hastā and the three uttarās.[36]

The seasons winter, the spring, and the forepart of the cold months, are the most favourable period to ride a horse, on the other hand in summer and autumn, a horse should not be harnessed or ridden upon—

## 97. Part 9 - Animal Husbandry in Post-Purāṇic Sanskrit Works—Introduction

Agriculture and Animal husbandry in the Puranas

This page relates ‘Animal Husbandry in Post-Puranic Sanskrit Works—Introduction’ of the study on agriculture and animal husbandry in the Puranas—a category of Sanskrit literature describing the culture and heritage of ancient Indian society. Besides detailing animal care, the six chapters of this essay also documents various Puranic agricultural science: ploughing, sowing, soil-types/fertility tests, transplantation, manuring, irrigation/water-management, tanks, rainfall–Sun dependence, horticulture, plus crop lists (e.g., Vrihi, Yava, Godhuma) and festivals/rites.

Part 9 - Animal Husbandry in Post-Purāṇic Sanskrit Works—Introduction

[Full title: Animal Husbandry in Post-Purāṇic Sanskrit Works and its Relevance with the Purāṇas—Introduction]

According to Kauṭilya, agriculture, cattle-breeding was the occupation for all classes of people.[1] As it gives grains and cattle, forests produce and free labour, so it is most useful.[2] Animals were used for various purposes. It is noticed that different animal products like pig’s fat, cow-dung, cow-bones etc. were required for fertilizing seeds or other parts of plants as pre-plantation treatment. Broad use of animal bones was required for this purpose.[3] Requirement of animal power (bullock) for raising water from well, for irrigation is also noticed.[4] The Bṛhatsaṃhitā and the Vṛkṣāyurveda mentions extensive use of various products of different species of animals in nourishment of plants. The Bṛhatsaṃhitā suggests that two āḍhakas (128 palas) of the powder of the dung of goats and sheep, one āḍhaka of sesamum, a prastha (16 palas) of wheat particles, a tulā (100 palas) of beef and a droṇa (256 palas) of water kept for seven nights should be sprinkled always for increasing the yield of flowers and fruits of trees, creepers and shrubs etc.[5] The Vṛkṣāyurveda mentions that the seeds of good quality (sādhuvīja) of kūṣmāṇḍa, vārttāka, paṭola and other vegetable fruits should be soaked in majjā of female beer (kroḍī) and after that should be sown in land. For irrigation, the water mixed with same animal majjā as stated above should be used. This produces fruits without seeds.[6]

Part 14 - Agriculture in the Modern Age—Introduction

[Full title: Agriculture in the Modern Age of 20-21st Century]

The Consultative Group on International Agricultural Research (CGIAR) states that sustainable agriculture is the successful management of resources to satisfy the quality of environment and conserving natural resources.[1] There is no doubt that the ancient treatises like the Vedas as well as the Purāṇas are the origin of agriculture and animal husbandry. The modern system of agriculture and animal husbandry has definitely inherited the process of the same from the Vedas and the Purāṇas. Obviously there may be some changes in the traditional agricultural system; some processes may be excluded from the indigenous system of agriculture and some different processes may be added to the modern agricultural system. But we cannot disregard our traditional knowledge system regarding agriculture and animal husbandry which is still prevailing in most of the parts of our country. The indigenous system of agriculture and animal husbandry has been achieving value day by day. The modern agriculturalists have improved these traditional methods gradually by applying science and technologies in the field of agriculture as well as in animal husbandry. So difference is observed in the system of agriculture of modern periods with that of the ancient texts. We have to give a look into the modern system of agriculture and animal husbandry to find out its relevance with the Purāṇic system. In the direction of modern agricultural research the establishment of Imperial Council of Agricultural Research (ICAR) in 1929, was the most significant step.[2]

## 113. Part 25 - Animal Husbandry in the Modern Age—Introduction

Agriculture and Animal husbandry in the Puranas

This page relates ‘Animal Husbandry in the Modern Age—Introduction’ of the study on agriculture and animal husbandry in the Puranas—a category of Sanskrit literature describing the culture and heritage of ancient Indian society. Besides detailing animal care, the six chapters of this essay also documents various Puranic agricultural science: ploughing, sowing, soil-types/fertility tests, transplantation, manuring, irrigation/water-management, tanks, rainfall–Sun dependence, horticulture, plus crop lists (e.g., Vrihi, Yava, Godhuma) and festivals/rites.

Part 25 - Animal Husbandry in the Modern Age—Introduction

[Full title: Animal Husbandry in the Modern Age of 20-21st Century—Introduction]

Numerically, India possesses the largest number of bovines in any country in the world, carrying one-fourth of the world’s stock of cattle and two-third of its stock of buffaloes.[1] Animal husbandry is mainly pastoral-based, which creates employment among the rural people and provides income to them. Livestock forms a very essential part of the agricultural economy. They provide the purpose for various agricultural operations, such as ploughing, irrigation, transportation etc. They produce manure for the fields, and supply milk and milk products, which are the only source of animal proteins in the diet of the chiefly vegetarian population of the country. The farmers increase their income by selling milk and milk products. M.S. Randhawa in his Agriculture and Animal Husbandry in India opines that development of cattle can be taken up fruitfully only if we know the quality and potentialities of the stock that is available in the country. The Indian Council of Agricultural Research has been making a systematic study of the indigenous breeds, and the more important breeds of recognized cattle and buffaloes in the country have been defined. The Central and the various State Governments have established cattle breeding farms for the development of breeds in different parts of the country so that superior bulls may be available for stud purposes in their respective areas.[2] With the objective of identifying and encouraging breed formation societies, the Government of India participated in the International Convention which was established at Rome in October 1936. According to this, each breed was to have a herd book in each country.

The herd books were supposed to have—

(a) register of births,
(b) final register of males, and
(c) final register of females.

The registers were to maintain detailed information on production records of each animal, prizes won in competitions etc.[3]

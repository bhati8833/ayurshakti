## Part 8 - Protection and Care (of crops)

Part 8 - Protection and Care (of crops)

Adequate attention is given to the protection and care of crops. The plantation (vṛkṣaropaṇaṃ) according to Surapāla is recognized as a source for attaining the puruṣārtha catuṣṭaya i.e. dharma, artha, kāma and mokṣa.[1] Rainy season is the best time for sowing all types of seeds and planting trees, etc. After rains the seeds should be planted. Autumn and spring season are average seasons for the purpose.[2] Winter and summer are both contraindicated for plantation. By the way all seasons are equally good for plantation if extra efforts are given on watering.[3] Kauṭilya states that the sprouting of seeds can be inferred from the Sun, the formation of grain can be inferred from the position of Jupiter, and rainfall can be inferred from the movement of Venus.[4]

On the occasion of the sowing of seeds for the first time a handful of the seeds bathed in water with a piece of gold should be sown and the following mantra should be recited—

prajāpataye kāśyapāya devāya namaḥ sadā/ 
sītā me ṛdhyatāṃ devī bījeṣu ca dhaneṣu ca//

In this mantra, salutation is given to the divine Kāśyapa and it is prayed that agriculture may flourish always and the goddess may reside in seeds and wealth.[5]

Reference of such recitation of mantra written in leaf is also met with in the Agni Purāṇa. According to it the following mantra written on a leaf, amidst the heap of rice under the influence of asterisms known as the three Pūrvās i.e., Pūrvaphālgunī, Pūrvāṣāḍhā, Pūrvabhādrapada, Viśākhā, Dhaniṣṭhā and Śatabhiṣā gives increase in the rice—[6]

dhanadāya sarvadhaneśāya dehi me dhanaṃ svāhā/
nave harṣe ilādevī lokasaṃvardhini kāmarūpiṇi dehi me dhanaṃ svāhā//

The Bṛhatsaṃhitā devotes a chapter to the growth of crops.[7] It states that when the sun enters into Vṛścika, Jupiter be posited in Kumbha, the Moon in Vṛṣabha, Mars and Saturn in Makara then there is great prosperity of corn.[8] Again when Venus or Mercury, or both, are posited in the second house from the Sun (in Vṛścika), or similarly in the twelfth house, summer corn will grow well.[9] When Venus, the Moon and Mercury are posited in their order in the 11th, 4th and 2nd from the Sun in Vṛścika, corn will thrive well. If in the above Yoga, Jupiter is posited in the 10th, great prosperity of cattle is also assured.[10] The Upavana-Vinoda mentions that trees get diseases through the affections of vāta, pitta and kapha. One should diagnose the diseases through their symptoms and cure them radically.[11]

## Part 11 - Rearing and Tending of Animals

Part 11 - Rearing and Tending of Animals

[Full title: Animal Husbandry in Post-Purāṇic Sanskrit Works and its Relevance with the Purāṇas (2) Rearing and Tending of Animals]

Rearing of animals of State’s property was carried under the office of superintendents for separate species of domestic animals.[1] It is suggested in the Arthaśāstra that cattle rearers shall apply remedies to calves and aged cows which suffer from various diseases.[2] Cattle of ten similar colour are grouped at the time of grazing. Cowherds shall take the cattle to graze in pastures either far or near according to their ability to protect them.[3] Cowherds shall graze the cattle in forest regions which are allotted as pasture grounds for the purpose of grazing for various seasons and which are protected from thieves and other molesting beasts by hunters.[4] A pony after the tenth day of its birth shall be given kuḍaba flowers mixed with one-fourth quantity of clarified butter and one prastha milk till it becomes six months old. During each following month the above rations should be increased half as much, with the addition of one prastha of barley till it becomes three years old, and thereafter with the addition of one droṇa of barley till it grows four years old. At the age of four or five it attains full development.[5]

The Arthaśāstra mentions the group of persons to attend the needs of the elephants. They are—

cikitsaka (elephant doctor),

anīkastha (trainer),

ārohaka (expert rider),

ādhoraṇa (who grooms them),

hastipaka (the protector),

aupacārika (one who massages),

vidhāpācaka (the cook),

yāvasika (one who procures grass for them),

pādapāśika (those who has tighten them to posts),

kuṭīrakṣaka (the sweeper of the stable) and

aupaśāyika (one who keeps watch in the stables at night).[6]

It is also advised there that the elephant doctors shall apply necessary medicines to elephants, while making a journey or suffer from diseases, over work or old age.[7]

The Śukranītisāra suggests some points that are to be noticed in case of managing a horse. One should strike the horse at the breast if it is terrified, at the neck if it neighs, at the posterior if it slips, at the mouth if going astray, at the tail if it gets angry, at the belly if it is absent minded.[8] It is also prohibited to strike the horse very often and at wrong places.[9] It is suggested there that one should ride the horse in the morning and evening in October and November, winter and spring, in the evening in summer, in the morning in autumn.[10] One should not use the horse in the rainy season or on uneven grounds.[11]

Reference of rearing and tending of animals are found in the Purāṇas. It is pointed out in the Matsya Purāṇa that a king’s Commander-in-chief should be expert in managing horses and elephants.[12] Regarding horse-care, it is said that the groom of a king should know about horses and how to treat the horses when ill.[13] A horse should be first ridden under the influence of the asterisms aśvinī, hastā and the three uttarās.[14] The seasons winter, the spring, and the forepart of the cold months, are the most favourable to ride a horse, on the other hand in summer and autumn, a horse should not be harnessed nor ridden upon.[15]

## Part 10 - Breeds of Animals (in Post-Purāṇic Sanskrit Works)

Part 10 - Breeds of Animals (in Post-Purāṇic Sanskrit Works)

[Full title: Animal Husbandry in Post-Purāṇic Sanskrit Works and its Relevance with the Purāṇas (1) Breeds of Animals]

The Arthaśāstra mentions different kinds of domesticated animals such as horse, donkey, camel, buffalo, goat, sheep and pig etc.[1] The horses belonging to the breed of Kamboja, Sindhu, Araṭṭa and Vanāyu countries were the best horses for use in battle etc; those of Vāhlīka, Pāpeya, Sauvīra and Taitala region were of middle quantity. The rest of the breeds were considered ordinary.[2] Regarding the quality of horses it is mentioned in the Śukranītisāra that those horses are good which run with legs thrown from a height, whose movements are like those of tigers, peacocks, ducks, parrots, pigeons, deer, camels, monkeys and bulls.[3]

In case of bulls it is stated by Śukra that the bull which is neither idle nor wicked but good for carrying load with a well-formed body and a good back is the best of all.[4] Reference of camels is also met with in the Śukranītisāra. It is mentioned there that the camel which is strong-built, has a good face, carries burden and goes thirty yojanas (1 yojana=4 miles[5]) a day is appreciated.[6] The bull and the camels should be governed by strings with which the nose can be pulled.[7]

The Śukranītisāra mentions four classes of elephants and their characteristics. The four classes according to Śukranītisāra are—

Bhadra,, Manda,, Mṛga and

Miśra.[8]

The elephants whose tusks are like the colour of honey, which are strong and well-formed, round and fat in body, have good face and excellent limbs are called as Bhadra class of elephants.[9] The elephant which has fat belly, lion-like eyes, thick skin, throat and trunk, medium limbs and a long body is known as Manda elephant.[10] The Mṛga elephant is that which has small or short throat, tusks, ears and trunk, big eyes and very short lips.[11] The Miśra elephant has the above said characteristics in mixture.[12]

The Arthaśāstra classifies elephants into four groups according to the training they are given. They are:

1) the elephants which are tameable (damya);
2) the elephants which are trained for war (sānnāhya);
3) the elephants which are trained for riding (aupavāhya); and
4) the rogue elephants (vyāla).[13]

According to the Arthaśāstra, the elephant which is seven aratnis in height, nine aratnis in length, ten aratnis in circumference, and is 40 years old is considered the best.[14] The Bṛhatsaṃhitā mentions the signs of cows[15], dogs[16], goats[17], horses[18] and elephants[19] in details. In the Purāṇas too we find the references of breeds of animals such as horses, cows, bulls, elephants etc.

In case of horses four varieties are mentioned. They are—

bhadra,, manda,, mṛga and

saṅkīrṇa.[20]

In case of elephants also there are four varieties. They are mentioned in the Matsya Purāṇa as—

Kumuda,, Añjana,, Vāmana and

Airāvata.[21]

Various types of bulls are mentioned. They are—

Nandīmukha,

Samudra and

Nīla Vṛṣabha.[22]

Moreover, mention of goats, sheep, buffaloes, camels, dogs is met with in the Purāṇas.

Animal Breeding:

The people fully knew the necessities of number of males of different animal species per herd of one hundred females of different types of domestic animals for the purpose of breeding. Kauṭilya in his Arthaśāstra mentions that in a herd of one hundred asses and mules there shall be five male animals and in the case of herds of goats and sheep there shall be ten males per hundred. For a herd of ten heads of cows or buffaloes there shall be four male animals each.[23]

## Part 15 - Major crops and Staple food

Part 15 - Major crops and Staple food

[Full title: Agriculture in the Modern Age of 20-21st Century (1) Major Crops]

Rice is the staple food of nearly three-fourth of the population in India. Its cultivation goes back to the unrecorded ages extending beyond 3,000 B. C. The customary use of rice in the religious ceremonies of the Hindus, which are associated with birth, marriage and death, is evidence to its great antiquity.[1] In India, generally three crops of paddy are raised in a year: autumn, winter and summer. Of these, the winter crop is the most important, reporting for about 90 percent of the total; the autumn crop accounts for less than 10 percent, and the summer crop constitutes the negligible balance.

The sowing and harvesting seasons of the three crops are as follows:[2]

Other food crops grown in India are wheat, millet, barley, maize, pulses, potato, etc. Various kind of oilseeds such as ground nut, mustard, sesamum, castor, linseed, safflower, etc. and fibre crops such as cotton, jute etc. are also grown in India. In Assam, among the cereals, rice is the single most important crop followed very remotely by wheat and maize.[3]

Agriculture in Assam depends upon rainfall. For this reason and for the agro-climatic difference, in Assam, there are three distinct rice-growing seasons. They are:

1) Śāli or winter rice(June/July to November/December);
2) Āhu or autumn rice(March/April to June/July) and
3) Boro or summer rice(November/December to May/June).[4]

Major pulses grown in Assam and adjoining north-eastern states are black gram (Vigna mungo) and green gram (Vigna radiata) during kharif and summer seasons and field pea (Pisum sativum), lentil(Lens culinaris) and lathyrus(Lathyrus sativus) during rabi seasons.[5]

In recent years the following plant species were introduced into India[6] :

1) Sunflower (Helianthus annuus); 
2) Tree-tomato (Cyphomandra betacea); 
3) Spearmint (Mentha arvensis); 
4) Kiwifruit (Actindia chinensis); 
5) Gum-arabic (Acacia senegal);

In the Purāṇas also various kinds of grains are mentioned. People in the days of the Purāṇas could recognize the crops. Mention of seventeen kinds of grāmya oṣadhis and fourteen kinds of yajñīya oṣadhis is met with in the Purāṇas.[7] Some of the agricultural crops mentioned in the Purāṇas are-tila[8] dhānya[9] (eighteen sorts of grain), vrīhi[10] (a kind of paddy), śyāmāka[11] saktu[12] (the flour of grain), nīvāra[13] (a kind of corn for śrāddha), māṣa[14] (a kind of grain), masūra, yava and godhūma[15] etc.

## Part 22 - Description of the Akṣaya Tṛtīyā festival (i.e., Akha Teej)

Part 22 - Description of the Akṣaya Tṛtīyā festival (i.e., Akha Teej)

[Full title: Festivals Related to Agriculture (1) Akṣaya Tṛtīyā, or Akha Teej]

Akṣayya Tṛtīyā is an agricultural festival, which is observed on the 3rd bright of the month Vaiśākha(April).[1] At present time, on this day farmers in Rajasthan sow seeds, especially paddy in the field, ceremonially. After ablution in a river or tank, wearing new cloths, the farmers carry the seeds in new baskets to the field. Then they make offerings to Goddess Lakṣmī in the field and sow the seeds ceremonially. Goddess Lakṣmī is prayed in the farmer’s home for abundant crops.[2] In the Bhaviṣya Purāṇa, reference of Akṣaya Tṛtīyā is met with. Pacification of the manes is a specialty of this day. Special things are mentioned to be given as gifts on this day. They are-water-jars, water-pitchers, umbrellas, gold, a piece of land and agricultural products such as yava, godhūma, caṇaka, saktu, etc.[3] It is believed that things gifted on this day would never be in shortage. So this day is called akṣayya [akṣaya].[4]

## Part 23 - Description of the Dīpāvalī festival

Part 23 - Description of the Dīpāvalī festival

[Full title: Festivals Related to Agriculture (3) Dīpāvalī]

In rural areas Dīpāvalī signifies harvest as it is celebrated in the month of October/November, which coincides with the end of the harvesting season. In Western part of India on the second day of Dīpāvalī a ritual which strongly indicates the origin of Dīpāvalī as a harvest festival is performed. On this day delicacies are arranged from pulverized semi-cooked rice termed as pohā.[1] This rice is taken from the fresh harvest available at that time.

In the Skanda Purāṇa, the festival Dīpāvalī is mentioned which is to be observed in the month of Āśvina/ Kārttika.The festival lasts for four days i.e., the 14th dark of Āśvina, the 15th dark(Amāvasyā), the 1st bright of Kārttika and the 2nd bright of Kārttika.[2]

This festival is called Dīpāvalī, as the waving of the lights is an important rite in i—

tdīpairnīrājanādatra saiṣā dīpāvaliḥ smṛtā.[3]

On the 14th dark of Āśvina, bath should be taken early in the morning, applying oil to the body, because on this day the Goddess Lakṣmī stays in oil and the Gaṅgā in water.[4] The eating of the broth of the leaves of black-gram (māṣa) is also mentioned as a special rite on this day.[5]

## Part 24 - The festival of Poṅgal of Tamil Nadu

Part 24 - The festival of Poṅgal of Tamil Nadu

[Full title: Festivals Related to Agriculture (4) Poṅgal of Tamil Nadu]

The beginning of the Uttarāyaṇa(when the apparent movement of the Sun is Northwards) event is celebrated as Poṅgal. Poṅgal is a harvest festival. The Poṅgal is celebrated for four days. The celebration of Poṅgal marks a period of abundance, harmony and pleasure. Moreover, it is celebrated in honor of the sun praying for abundant crops. Families gather to express joy and to share their joy and harvests with others. Milk and rice are offered to the Sun.[1] Likewise we find in the Purāṇic texts the mention of festivals like, Āgneya-Utsava[2], Indra Mahotsava[3] Madana Mahotsava[4] and Saṃkrānti,[5] which are closely connected with agriculture.

## Part 23 - The festival of Bihu of Assam

Part 23 - The festival of Bihu of Assam

[Full title: Festivals Related to Agriculture (2) Bihu of Assam]

Assam is a land of fairs and festivals. The basic occupation of the Assamese people is agriculture. Therefore, most of the festivals are usually celebrated around the pre-harvesting or post-harvesting period. Among the rituals which are related to agriculture, there are the three Bihus which are observed by the people of Assam at three different periods of agricultural operation during a year.

They are—

the Bohāg Bihu (Raṅgālī Bihu),

the Kāti Bihu (Kaṅgālī Bihu), and

the Māgh Bihu (Bhogālī Bihu).

In fact, Bihu is a festival to celebrate fertility.

The Bohāg Bihu starts from the last day of the last month (Caitra) of the Assamese year. On ‘Saṃkrānti’ day, the ‘Garu Bihu’ is observed. Household cattle get special attention and they are decorated with colourful garlands of flowers and given good food. This Bihu portrays the importance of cow in practicing agriculture. So the first day of Bohāg Bihu is devoted to worship of cow to increase the physical strength and production of milk.

Like Bohāg Bihu, the Kāti Bihu is also related to the agrarian economy. It falls in the conjoining day of the month Āśvina and Kārttika, i.e. in the Saṃkrānti day, when the paddy crops are yet to mature and the granaries are almost empty.

Hence it is called ‘Kaṅgālī’, which means poor. This Bihu can be considered the thanks giving occasion to God and in hope for the better crops for the next year. This is the period of time in the year when the paddy grows in the fields and cultivators work hard and enthusiastically wait for a good harvest.

The Māgh Bihu is celebrated on the last day of the month Pauṣa and lasts for three days. It is a harvesting festival and is celebrated after the annual harvest; the name ‘Bhogālī’ itself implies feasts and food. Feasting forms the main feature of this Bihu.

Likewise the Skanda Purāṇa[1] mentions about the festival Saṃkrānti which was observed in the month of Māgha. This is the festival, where new rice was to be offered to the God.

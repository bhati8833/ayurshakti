6. Ornamental and Barter Metals

Silver and gold were the principal metals in the Vedic era for producing ornaments and the uses as the medium of financial transactions. In the previous chapter we have provided numerious examples of the use of hiranya or gold in the age of the Rgveda.

We have mentioned different kinds of golden ornaments used in the Rgvedic era. We need not repeat those names; the items continued to be used in the post-Rgvedic period. S.Bra 5.5.5.16 subtly explains the function of Satamana: 'with gold they do nothing, and yet it is an object of respect'. It may be noted that binary series of weights was found in the Harappan town of Chanhu-daro. The coins sana and Karshapana became popular long. after the age of Samhita or Brahmana literature. Manusamhita mentioned that sana and other coins could be made of not only gold or silver, but also copper or black metal (iron or lead) We have already referred to the bronze (Atharvaveda.10.10.5) and lead (Shatapatha Brahama 12.7.2.10-12) pieces being used as media of exchange Atharvaveda. 5.28.1 mentions in one breath dirghayutvaya satasaradaya harite trini rajate trini ayasi trini  'Three pieces (coins) each of gold, silver and ayas (bronze?) for worship to gain long life'.

7. Iron Implements

India entered the iron age after the Rgvedic era. Many metallic implements, probably made of copper or bronze, were mentioned in our previous chapter on the Rgveda. Similar tools continued to be made using the newly discovered metal, iron. We need not mention them again in this chapter. Just by the way of illustration, it may be noted that Ksura or razor known in the Rgveda continued to be popular thereafter (Atharvaveda.12.5.20,55; Tai. Sam. 2.1.5.7; s. Bra 2.6.4.5; 3.1.2.7; 3.6.2.9 etc). # The * sharpedge of the razor' ksurasya dhara nisita  (Katha Upanisad, 1.3.14, B.Up.3.3.2) became a popular expression. Swadhiti in Tai.Sam. 6.3.3.2, Va.Sam. 2.15 and 5.43 meant an axe or a sword as in Rigveda 2.39.7, 3.2.10 etc. The names of several new iron instruments appeared in the later Vedic literature; possibly there had been no prototypes in copper or bronze. Parigha was an iron bar to keep door(s) closed from inside (Cha.Up.2.24.6, 10 and 15). It also represented an iron cudgel used in warfare. Parinaha was an iron chest or sinduka for storing valuable items (Atharvaveda.19.48.1). Two-armed iron tongue was known as parisas (Shatapatha Brahama 14.1.3.1; 14.2.1.16; 14.2.2.54 14.3.1); this resembles modern sandasi and was used to lift hot crucibles and vessels from a furnace. Prasasa was an axe or some other instrument for cutting (Ai.Bra. 2.6.5, Nir.5.11). Sasa stood for a sword or knife (Ai.Bra. 7.17.15, San.Sr.15.25.1, s.Bra. 3.8.1.4-5, 13.2.3.16). A stick of iron was known as salvaka (Va. Sam. 24.35, Ai.Bra. 3.26.3).

5. On Lead

On account of its low melting point (327°C and low latent heat of fusion (6 cal/gm), lead has sometimes been confused with tin. This is attested by its misleading equations with trapu. As we have quoted earlier (S.YV or Va. Sam 18.13, Cha.Up 4.17.7) tin (trapu) and lead (sisa) have been mentioned as different metals. Monnier-Williams and other scholars have failed to discover the etymological origin of sisa or sisaka signifying lead. There are many other references to the use of this metal (Tai. Bra 2.6.42, 3.12.6.5; Mai.Sam.2.4.2, 3.11.9; S.Bra, 5.1.2.14; 5.4.1.9; 12.7.1.7 etc.). In S.3 ra 5.4.1.10, the softness of lead metal has been emphasized. S.YV or Va.Sam. 19.80 mentioned the use of heavy pieces of lead in the art of weaving: Like other metal pieces, lead has sisena tantra urjasutrena  In the also been used as a medium of barter (S.Bra 12.7.2.10-12). Atharva Veda, lead powder was reported to have medicinal value in curing tuberculosis of human beings and cows (Atharvaveda.12.2.1, when used. in small doses. That it acts as a poison when administered in larger doses was also understood (Atharvaveda.1.16.2 and 4).

9. Mani in the Vedic Literature

Minerals and gems abounded in the sites of the Harappan civilization and later in Taxila and the mahajanapada sites during the Historical Period. Quite strangely however, the Vedic literatures do not mention or name any significant number of minerals or inorganic gems.. Gold and silver were of course mentioned as mani or ratna Lohamani in Cha. Up. 6.1.5 meant red (copper) mani or bead, and not ruby as some scholars have surmised. The word mani meant "anything which decorates'. In the Atharva Veda, many plant products such as stated to be jangida (Atharvaveda 2.4.1-6), udumbara, darbha etc. were used as decorative mani to ward off evils. Gems of organic origin such as ivory, conch-shell, pearl are not usually classified as minerals (which are by definition inorganic); these were used in the Vedic ages as mani, or gems. Ka. s.s. 22.2.22.28 informs that elephants were reared mostly in the eastern part of India, and we have found ivory specimens underneath the early archaeological sites in the Ganga-Yamuna valley. Atharvaveda 3.22 refers to the use of ivory. Conch shell or sankha was used to issue proclaiming samkha dhyayamanasya  (ninadita ) (3.UP.2.4.8, sound samkha  4.5.9). Krisan or pearl was possibly conch-pearl. The Rgvedic poet has described krisan decorating the chariot of Savita (Rigveda 1.35.4) and the horses (Rigveda 10.68.1). Sadvisma Brahmana 5.6.13.3 called pearl a samudramani. The Atharva Veda 4.10.1-7 discusses

III-18 the origin of sankha and Krsana, as well as their uses as mani and supernatural aids' to ensure long life: (4.10.1) samo hiranyajah samkhah krsanah patuma ahasa divi jatah samudrajah sindhutasyabhrtah | sa no hiranyajah samkha ayuspratarano manih  'devanam asthi krsanam babhuva- dirghayutvaya satasaradaya karsanastvabhi raksatu ||  4.10.7) (4.10.4) varcase balaya  We find in this passage a mixture of scientific observation and mystical belief. The shell and pearl are said to grow in the sea under atmospheric influences on the one hand; on the other, it is also proclaimed that pearl is god's bone. which when worn, ensures a long life.

## 41. Part 24 - Agriculture and its Related Concepts in the Proposed Purāṇas

Agriculture and Animal husbandry in the Puranas

This page relates ‘Agriculture and its Related Concepts in the Proposed Puranas’ of the study on agriculture and animal husbandry in the Puranas—a category of Sanskrit literature describing the culture and heritage of ancient Indian society. Besides detailing animal care, the six chapters of this essay also documents various Puranic agricultural science: ploughing, sowing, soil-types/fertility tests, transplantation, manuring, irrigation/water-management, tanks, rainfall–Sun dependence, horticulture, plus crop lists (e.g., Vrihi, Yava, Godhuma) and festivals/rites.

Part 24 - Agriculture and its Related Concepts in the Proposed Purāṇas

The Viṣṇu Purāṇa mentions the four branches of knowledge-logical, scriptural, practical and political. Agriculture, commerce and tending of cattle; the knowledge of these three professions constitutes practical Science. Agriculture is the subsistence of farmers.[1] In the Tretā age, there grew the plants in agricultural produce.[2]

In the Agni Purāṇa the Goddess Earth is prayed as the possessor of all seeds, all gems and all cereals.[3] The Agni Purāṇa has described the synonymous words of agricultural products and its related terms in the chapter 366. The term kaṇiśa and śasyamañjarī are used in the sense of the ear of paddy etc. cereals[4], dhānya, vrīhi and stambakari in the sense of common cereals,[5] māṣa etc. (mudga, rājamāṣa, kulattha, canaka, tila, kākaṇḍa and cīvara) are used in the sense of śamī cereal (dhānya), barley (cereal bearing tūṇḍa) etc. in the sense of śūka cereal, and nīvāra is used in the sense of tṛṇadhānya.[6] Yugya and prāsaṃgya are used in the sense of bull being yoked, śākaṭa in the sense of bull pulling the cart.[7] Mṛt and mṛttikā are used in the sense of clay and mṛtsā and mṛtsnā are used in the sense of fertile soil.[8]

Among the duties of a king the foremost is that, a good king should maintain or protect agriculture.[9] The provinces should be largely inhabited by the Śūdras.[10] The inhabitants of the provinces should live by agriculture[11] and abound in cattle.[12] The country should be safe against all foreign invasions abounding in grains.[13] In reference to the ways of protecting the fort and the city of a king, Matsya said in the Matsya Purāṇa that within the fortress, agricultural products such as all kinds of grain, barley, wheat, all kinds of pulse such as aconite leaved kidney been, green grain, gram, sesamum and grain-dust,etc. should be kept in stock in abundance.[14]

Auspiciousness of cereals and paddies are mentioned in the Agni Purāṇa. It is stated that cereals and paddies, except those of the black species are auspicious and ensure the success of one’s mission, when looked at by a man at the time of leaving house.[15] Other grains such as barley, wheat, sesamum, green gram and black gram, when they are consecrated by the offerings, they are dear to Viṣṇu.[16] While enumerating the sixty different Samvatsaras, the Agni Purāṇa states that a good harvest is usually reaped and gathered in and the earth overflows in joy in the year named as Śukra.[17] Corn grows abundant in the year named as Vikrama.[18] The year Pramāthī is characterized by a moderate rain-fall.[19] The year Pārthiva is marked by the abundance of grain and food-stuff, while the year named Jaya is characterized by excessive rainfall.[20] God Indra (rain-god) is considered the presiding deity of all the cereals.[21] Various sorts of paddy grow in a year belonging to the class of Ananam.[22] Again the Dundubhi class is marked by abundance of paddy.[23] Rainfall becomes abundant in a year of the Piṅgala class. Again there is a moderate rainfall in a year of the Durmati class.[24]

The Purāṇas describe the quantity of food grains and articles that are to be collected when natural phenomena such as the showers of meteors, earthquakes, falls or thunderbolts, hurricanes, eclipses and the appearance of the meteoric light take place in the different months of the year.[25] The food-stuff such as rice, wheat and barley stocked in the month of Jyeṣṭha under the mysterious influence of the above mentioned phenomena would bring in six times their value in the course of eight months.[26] Rice purchased in the month of Aśvina and rice bought and stocked in the month of Kārttika would sell at an advantage in the month of Māgha.[27]

Reference of the use of forced labour in agriculture is met with in the Bhāgavata Purāṇa. Bharata, being made to engage in work by the instance of others, by viṣṭi(forced labour) or by wages, he ate whatever was obtained by alms or by chance, much or little, sweet or foul, but never for satisfaction of senses.[28] When he wished to have food(for his livelihood) in return(as wages) for work for others, he was asked even by his brothers to work in the fields and he did it, but he knew not whether it was level or uneven, whether he did more or less(in proportion to the wages).He swallowed, as if it were nectar, whether it was broken rice or other grain;oil-cake, husk, worm-eaten grains and charred rice sticking to the bottom of pot.[29]

Part 23 - Oil Seeds (according to the Purāṇas)

Ikṣu (Sugarcane)[1] and oil-seeds also come under agricultural products. In the Purāṇas various types of oil-seeds are mentioned to be used in various rituals.

The most popular oil-seeds mentioned in ritual context are—

tila (sesame)[2],

sarṣapa (mustard)[3],

rājikā (a mustard-type seed)[4],

eraṇḍa (castor-seeds)[5] and

siddhārthaka (white mustard)[6] etc.

The sesame seeds are the most common of oil-seeds. They are considered deva-dhānya[7] i.e. divine grain but not ordinary grain. Hence at all rituals it seems to be important.

Moreover, [the following] the oil-seeds are also mentioned in the Purāṇas.—

kṣauma (linseeds)[8], and

akṣa (the seed of the bead of rosaries)[9]

Part 27 - Agricultural Products Used as Medicines

The Garuḍa Purāṇa[1] states the medicinal value of various agricultural products. Rakta śāli pacifies thirst and reduces fat. Moreover, it removes the three faults i.e. vāta, pitta and kapha.[2] The mahāśāli increases sexual desire (vṛṣyaṃ).[3] The kalabha cures bile-troubles and phlegmatic humour.[4] The ṣaṣṭika (variety of rice) cures from the tridoṣa (vāta-pitta-kapha).[5] The śyāmāka is the cure for bile and phlegmatic disorder.[6] Priyaṅgu, nīvāra and koradūṣa cure the same.[7] Yava is also the remover of bile and phlegm.[8] Godhūma is promoter of sexual desire and destroyer of vāta (gastric disorder).[9] The mudga cures cough, bile and dripping eyes (asra).[10] The māṣa gives great strength, promotes sexual desire and also removes bile and phlegm.[11] Rājamāṣa does not promote sexual desire, but it removes gas trouble.[12] The kulattha cures from restrained breathing, hiccups, phlegm (kapha) and gases.[13] The makuṣṭhaka drives away fever and raktapitta (derangement of blood produced by bile).[14] Caṇaka and āḍhakī destroys the pitta

and kapha.[15] Atasī generates pitta and destroys kapha and vāyu.[16] The masūra is destroyer of bile and phlegm.[17] Besides, the soup from yava, pepper and kulattha is stated as good for throat and it also cures the gas-trouble.[18] The mudga and āmalaka is a cure for phlegm and bile.[19]

To remove the weakness of a person suffering from fever, a gruel (maṇḍa) of roasted puffed rice (lājaḥ) and boiled water is recommended.[20] For a convalescent patient, a diet of old and matured grains of ṣaṣṭika, nīvāra, raktaśāli, pramodakā should be given.[21] Another diet chart prescribed by the Agni Purāṇa for a patient with fever includes preparations of yava in any shape as well as the pulses mudga, masūra, caṇakā, kulattha cooked with kuṣṭha and fruits and vegetables such as pomegranates, paṭolas, nimbas, parpaṭas, karkoṭakas, kaṭolakas may also be used safely.[22] In diarrhoea (atisāra), the Agni Purāṇa recommends a diet composed of saktu, godhūma, lāja (roasted puffed rice), yava, śāli, masūra, caṇaka and mudga treated with the drug kuṣṭha.[23] Again old śāli rice is also recommended in diarrhoea.[24] For the patient of chronic dysentery, grains of old and matured śāli rice should form the staple food.[25] Wheat, barley, śāli rice, mudga pulse and brahmarkṣa are the various diets given in the Agni Purāṇa for a man suffering from abdominal dropsy.[26] Another diet chart consists of a soup of masūra, mudga, old śāli rice, nimba and parpaṭa along with the soup of animals.[27]

Apūpa, kuṣṭha, kulmāṣa and yava are favourable to a man suffering from an attack of Gonorrhea, during which his diet should consist of mudga pulse, kulattha, preparations of barley and matured śāli rice.[28] A leper should largely drink of a potion containing the washings of mudga or masūra pulse.[29]

A man suffering from Erysipelas(bacterial infection which affects the skin) should live on a diet composed of mudga, āḍhaka, masūra, prepared with lemon juice, saindhava salt, clarified butter, juice of grapes, old wheat, barley and śāli rice.[30] For śotha or swelling, dry radish and roots of kulattha boiled in the juice of jāṅgala are prescribed. And the patient should live on a diet composed of preparations of barley, wheat and matured śāli rice; treated with uśīra.[31] For a patient with breathing trouble or bronchitis, kulattha and mudga pulse prepared with bird’s flesh(viṣkiras), cooked with curd, pomegranate juice, juice of matuluṅga, honey, lime and grapes and wheat, barley or boiled śāli rice are prescribed.[32]

For a patient of rheumatism a diet of red ṣaṣṭika, barley, wheat, mudga, kākamācī (niteseed), vāstuka,honey and distilled water is prescribed.[33] Śāli rice, āmalaka, date fruits, grapes and jujubes are also favourable for rheumatic patient.[34] Besides, the diet for a patient suffering from piles should consist of preparations of barley, śāli rice, and suvarca salt.[35] Fried paddy, powdered barley, honey, śikhi and wine are good medicines for cold.[36] Boiled rice made of śāli paddy or a mixture of milk and water made lukewarm should be deemed as the best layer of thirst.[37]

In ūrustambha (rheumatism of the thigh),various preparations made of barley, dry radish, vegetables of paṭola and vetra are prescribed.[38] The diet of a consumptive patient, should consist of oil pressed out of sesamum seeds, mudga, barley, wheat, boiled rice made from grains matured in the granary, at least for a year and the juice of jāṅgala are prescribed.[39]

## 44. Part 1 - Meaning of the word Ākhyāna

Agriculture and Animal husbandry in the Puranas

This page relates ‘Meaning of the word Akhyana’ of the study on agriculture and animal husbandry in the Puranas—a category of Sanskrit literature describing the culture and heritage of ancient Indian society. Besides detailing animal care, the six chapters of this essay also documents various Puranic agricultural science: ploughing, sowing, soil-types/fertility tests, transplantation, manuring, irrigation/water-management, tanks, rainfall–Sun dependence, horticulture, plus crop lists (e.g., Vrihi, Yava, Godhuma) and festivals/rites.

Part 1 - Meaning of the word Ākhyāna

The word ākhyāna in Sanskrit is used to indicate myth, story or tale. It is derived as—

āṅ khyā lyuṭ /kathanam iti.[1]

V.S. Apte in his The Practical Sanskrit-English Dictionary defines the word ākhyāna as a tale, a story; especially a legendary story and a legend.[2] M. Monier Williams in his A Sanskrit-English Dictionary too defines ākhyāna as a tale, story, and legend.[3] A.A. Macdonell opines that a myth actually arises when the imagination interprets a natural event as the action of a personified being resembling the human agent. Thus the observation that the moon follows the sun without overtaking it, would have been transformed into a myth by describing the former as a maiden following a man by when she is rejected. Such an original myth enters on the further stage of poetical embellishment, as soon as it becomes the property of people endowed with creative imagination.[4]

The Encyclopaedia Britannica defines the word myth as traditional story of ostensibly historical events that serves to unfold part of the worldview of a people or explain a practice, belief, or a natural phenomenon. Myths relate the events, conditions and deeds of gods or superhuman beings that are outside ordinary human life and yet basic to it. These events are set in a time altogether different from historical time, often at the beginning of creation or at an early stage of prehistory.[5]

According to The Encyclopaedia of Religion and Ethics a myth is usually, directly or indirectly, in narrative form; its difference from ordinary tales seems to lie partly in the fact that it is believed to be substantially true, at least by those among whom it is first repeated;it thus differs from a parable or allegory, as well as from a fiction or romance. Moreover, most myths, if not all, are etiological; this is to say, they grew up or were invented to explain certain phenomena, beliefs or customs.[6] Sukumari Bhattacharji in her Literature in the Vedic Age states that a myth is a statement of the state of affairs in illo tempore, i.e. something which happened at the beginning of time with cosmic or minor etiological creations subsumed under it. The concern and obligation of the socially responsible man is to preserve creation by re-enacting through prescribed sacraments that which the gods performed for creation and preservation.[7]

The myths are the stories which were handed down from olden times. They are the stories with a veiled meaning. They were originated in ancient times. In the Purāṇas we come across various myths viz., the myth behind the creation of Agriculture, the myth of Vena and Pṛthu, the Indra-Vṛtra myth, the myth of Indra and Ahalyā, the myth of the king Kuru, the myth of Viśvāmitra etc. which are directly or indirectly connected with agriculture. An attempt has been made in this chapter to discuss some of the important myths in the Purāṇas, which are related to agriculture.

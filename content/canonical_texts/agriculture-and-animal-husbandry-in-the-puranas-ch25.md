## 105. Part 17 - Classification of Soils

Agriculture and Animal husbandry in the Puranas

Besides detailing animal care, the six chapters of this essay also documents various Puranic agricultural science: ploughing, sowing, soil-types/fertility tests, transplantation, manuring, irrigation/water-management, tanks, rainfall–Sun dependence, horticulture, plus crop lists (e.g., Vrihi, Yava, Godhuma) and festivals/rites.

Part 17 - Classification of Soils

[Full title: Agriculture in the Modern Age of 20-21st Century (3) Classification of Soils]

The investigations of JW Leather (the first Imperial Chemist of Agricultural Research Institute, Pusa) in 1898, led to a classification of Indian soils into four major types:

(i) Indo-Gangetic alluvium;
(ii) black cotton or regur soils;
(iii) red soils lying on metamorphic rocks; and
(iv) laterite soils.[1]

In addition to the four principal groups of soils described above, mention may be made of four more groups.[2] They are:

(i) Forest and Hill Soils
(ii) Desert Soils
(iii) Saline and Alkaline Soils
(iv) Peaty and Marshy Soils

Various types of grounds are mentioned in the Purāṇas long ago. They are-white coloured ground, blood coloured ground, black clay and yellow coloured ground.[3] Again golden soil, rocky soil, blue soil, white soil and yellowish red soil are also mentioned.[4]

Part 18 - Sowing of Seeds

[Full title: Agriculture in the Modern Age of 20-21st Century (4) Sowing of Seeds]

Sowing is one of the most important farm procedures. The germination of seeds, growth as well as the plant propagation depends on the time of sowing the seeds and the way in which it is done.

The seeds which are proposed for sowing should assure the following conditions:—

(1) They should belong to the proper variety or strain of the crop which is proposed to be grown;
(2) They should be clean and free from obvious mixtures of other seeds;
(3) They should be mature, well developed and plump in size;
(4) They should be free from obvious signs of age or bad storage such as mouldiness, insect attacks, bad smell, etc.;
(5) Above all they should have a high germinating capacity.[1]

The seeds of certain crops are subjected to some kinds of treatment before sowing, for different purposes, some of which are of a special character while others are ordinary.

These purposes comprise:

(1) convenience in sowing,
(2) the control of disease,
(3) protecting against white-ants,
(4) hastening or facilitating germination,
(5) inducing earliness,
(6) increasing the yield, and
(7) inducing variation in the progeny.[2]

## 107. Part 20 - Plant-Protection Practices

Agriculture and Animal husbandry in the Puranas

This page relates ‘Plant-Protection Practices’ of the study on agriculture and animal husbandry in the Puranas—a category of Sanskrit literature describing the culture and heritage of ancient Indian society. Besides detailing animal care, the six chapters of this essay also documents various Puranic agricultural science: ploughing, sowing, soil-types/fertility tests, transplantation, manuring, irrigation/water-management, tanks, rainfall–Sun dependence, horticulture, plus crop lists (e.g., Vrihi, Yava, Godhuma) and festivals/rites.

Part 20 - Plant-Protection Practices

[Full title: Agriculture in the Modern Age of 20-21st Century (6) Plant-Protection Practices]

Agricultural scientists are turning away their concentration to the traditional or indigenous technology and searching possibilities of using them wherever potential. Old traditional technologies were scientific and almost ecological as all the practices of protection of plants were based on organic materials both of plant and animal origin.[1] We find in the Purāṇas that plants were protected by using honey, ghee, milk and milk products, cow dung and urine etc.[2] The Agni Purāṇa states that in case where barrenness would be apprehended, the leaves and branches should be sprinkled over with a solution of powdered viḍaṅga, clarified butter, pulverized mudga, māṣa and kulattha pulse.[3]

Milk and ghee have been used for centuries. Following the traditional techniques today the farmers use cow dung for different purposes such as seed dressing, plastering cut ends of vegetative propagating units such as sugarcane sets, dressing wounds and applying to soil.[4] Indigenous system of plant protection is still followed by the farmers of different parts of India.

The tribal farmers in the tribal region of Rajasthan use the following methods[5] of plant protection—

1) In paddy, a solution of 4 L of cow urine and 10 g asafetida in 10 L of water is sprayed to repel sucking pests.

2) To control paddy blast and bacterial blight, farmers commonly spray a solution of cow dung prepared by mixing 3kg cow dung in 3 L of water.

3) Several farmers lay fresh cow dung near the lapel area of chili plant to stop it from fungal diseases, namely, damping-off and die-back.

4) In case of soil borne diseases such as root rot and collar rot, and termite attack, castor oil, karañja(Pongamia glabra) or neem cakes are used as control measures.

In the Semi-arid region of Rajasthan the following methods[6] of plant protection are used by farming communities:

1) Control of termites:

Termites attack and cracking of epidermis in fruits can be avoided by using a paste on the main trunk with a mixture of mustard oil(1 L) and turmeric powder(200g).Mustard oil attracts ants which remove termites while turmeric powder helps in healing the cracks. In another practice, aak(Calotropis gigantea) leaves are chopped, filled in gunny bags, and kept in irrigation channel. The exudates from these leaves have been reported to kill termites and other soil borne pathogens.

2) Control of thrips, aphids, whitefly, and other pests:

(a) To prevent thrips and aphids, cow dung ash is applied on foliage of vegetables, e.g., chili, onion, garlic and cucurbits.

(b) To trap insects like whitefly and thrips, spraying of buttermilk over cucurbits and chili plant is a common practice in some districts of Rajasthan.

In Arunachal Pradesh following traditional or indigenous methods[7] of plant protection are used:

1) Control of ginger rot and yellowing:

The tribal farmers sow ginger after treating with a solution of cow dung. About 10-15kg cow dung and asafetida (40-80 g) are mixed in 8-10 liters of water. This is enough to treat ginger rhizomes required for one hectare. During vegetative growth, for protecting the ginger plants from rotting, this practice is useful. To prevent yellowing the farmers grow papaya as an intercrop for providing shade to ginger. Thus, the leaves of ginger do not turn yellow and high yields are obtained.

2) Control of storage pests:

Farmers use different simple practices for effective control of storage pests; viz., fuel wood ash 250 g for 250 kg of pulses; dry neem leaves 2.5 kg for 100 kg of wheat; and five to six dry chilies placed in one kg seed of green gram (mudga) and black gram(māṣa).

## 108. Part 21 - Irrigation (in modern Indian Agriculture)

Agriculture and Animal husbandry in the Puranas

This page relates ‘Irrigation (in modern Indian Agriculture)’ of the study on agriculture and animal husbandry in the Puranas—a category of Sanskrit literature describing the culture and heritage of ancient Indian society. Besides detailing animal care, the six chapters of this essay also documents various Puranic agricultural science: ploughing, sowing, soil-types/fertility tests, transplantation, manuring, irrigation/water-management, tanks, rainfall–Sun dependence, horticulture, plus crop lists (e.g., Vrihi, Yava, Godhuma) and festivals/rites.

Part 21 - Irrigation (in modern Indian Agriculture)

[Full title: Agriculture in the Modern Age of 20-21st Century (7) Irrigation]

When the primitive man learned the process of agriculture, the only source of getting water for the crops was rain. The Matsya Purāṇa states that agricultural prospects flourish by rain which again depends on fixed time.[1] The Agni Purāṇa mentions that the god Parjanya, Arka and Indu,should be worshipped to guard against any disturbance that affects the annual rainfall of a country.[2] But when there were no rains, farmers drew off water from rivers and natural lakes and in this way irrigation was invented. It is also suggested in the Purāṇas that a king should not depend upon the atmospheric causes for its water–supply. Therefore, the fort of a king should be situated in a country watered by large rivers.[3]

Later on emphasis was given to modern irrigation system by constructing dames and lakes. The canals were dug and water stored in reservoirs was carried to the fields for irrigation. The system of irrigation has been developed from time to time. By 1950, a comprehensive plan was drawn up in accordance with the availability of men and materials and the need for maximum utilization of water resources. Large-scale projects were proposed. These had necessarily to be multipurpose in character. New methods of construction, transport and haulage of material, power generation, and flood control which modern technology has evolved, have made the planning of such projects possible. As many as 19 dams have been taken up for construction. These include a number of large dams like those of Bhakra-Nangal, Damodar Valley, Hirakud and Tungabhadra.[4]

The following important irrigation-power projects were undertaken during the colonial rule and post-independence era:

1) Ganges Canal—Though the surveys began in 1836, actual work could be started only in 1842. The canal was first opened in 1854.[5]

2) Sarda Canal Project—For irrigation in the United Provinces of Agra and Awadh.

3) Bhakra-Nangal Project—On Sutlej River in Bilaspur, Himachal Pradesh.

4) Tungabhadra Project—On Tungabhadra River in Bellary district, Mysore.

5) The Kakrapar barrage—On the River Tapti at Kakrapar, some 50 miles east of Surat in Bombay State.[6]

6) The Nagarjunasagar Project—On the Krishna River in Nalgonda district of Andhra Pradesh.

7) The Matatila dam—On the River Batwa near Jhansi in Uttar Pradesh.

8) The Panchet hill dam—On the River Damodar near Dhanbad, Bihar.

9) The Hirakud dam—On the River Mahanadi, Orissa.

10) The Durgapur barrage—On Damodar River, Bihar.

11) The Chambal Project—Which includes Gandhi Sagar Dam and Rana Pratap Sagar Dam on the River Chambal.

The Rajasthan Canal Project now known as Indira Gandhi Nahar Pariyojana, one of the largest canal systems of the world, is a gigantic human effort to transform a part of the vast Thar Desert into a land of prosperity.[7]

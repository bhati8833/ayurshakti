# Chapter 9 - The Four Basic Factors of Therapeusis (cikitsa-chatuspada)

## TL;DR — Executive Clinical Summary

**Sutrasthana Chapter 9** — the first of the two chapters on the *chatuspada*, the four legs of therapeusis — names the four basic factors of treatment: the **physician** (*bhisaj*), the **drugs** (*dravya*), the **attendant** (*upasthatri*) and the **patient** (*rogin*). Each factor is defined by a tetrad of desiderata, giving sixteen qualities in all. The chapter then argues at length that the physician is the foremost of the four — the knower of disease and drugs, the instructor of attendant and patient — warns graphically against the quack, and closes with the physician's professional conduct: friendship to all, compassion for the ailing, devotion to the curable and resignation towards the dying. The chapter also gives the classical definitions of disease, health and therapeusis itself.

---

## Introduction

This minor chapter (and the major chapter that follows in Chapter 10) answers a foundational question of medicine: **what makes treatment succeed?** The answer is not the drug alone, but a team of four — physician, medicine, nurse and patient — each contributing its own quality. Therapy, in this view, is a combined operation of all four; failure can be traced to any one of them. The chapter is the classical source of the *cikitsa-chatuspada* doctrine, still taught in every Ayurvedic college, and it doubles as an early treatise on the professional ethics of the physician.

## The Four Basic Factors of Treatment

> Verses 1–3. The team is named.

**Verses 1–2** open the chapter in the usual form — "We shall now expound the minor chapter entitled 'The Four Basic Factors of Therapeusis,'" declared by the worshipful Atreya.

**Verse 3** states the doctrine: the physician, the drugs, the attendant and the patient constitute the four basic factors of treatment (*cikitsa*). Possessed of the required qualities, these four lead to the **earliest cure** of disease.

## Definitions of Disease, Health and Therapeusis

> Verses 4–5. The conceptual frame.

**Verse 4** gives the two definitions on which the whole of therapeutics rests: the **discordance of the body-elements is disease**, and their **concordance is normal health**. Freedom from disease is characterised by a sense of ease; disease is always of the nature of pain. **Verse 5** defines therapeusis: the combined operation of the four factors — the physician and the other three, of commendable qualities — with the object of bringing about the concordance of the body-elements when pathological changes have occurred in them.

## The Sixteen Desiderata

> Verses 6–9. Four tetrads.

**Verse 6 — the physician**: clear grasp of theoretical knowledge, wide practical experience, skill, and purity of body and mind.

**Verse 7 — the drugs**: abundance, applicability, usability in multifarious modes, and richness of quality.

**Verse 8 — the attendant (nurse)**: knowledge of nursing, skill, affection for the patient, and cleanliness.

**Verse 9 — the patient**: recollection, obedience to instructions, courage, and ability to describe his ailments.

These four tetrads — sixteen qualities in all — are the complete specification of the therapeutic team.

## The Physician Is Foremost

> Verses 10–14½. The chief of the four.

**Verse 10** awards the chief place to the physician: he is at once the **knower of disease and drugs**, the instructor of the attendant and patient, and the prescriber of medicine and regimen. **Verses 11–12** argue by analogy: as the utensils, fuel and fire are to a cook in cooking — as the terrain, army and weapon are to a conqueror in obtaining victory — so are the other three factors in relation to the physician in accomplishing the cure. **Verse 13** completes the analogy: as the clay, rod, string and wheel are ineffective in the absence of the potter, so the three other factors of treatment, in the absence of the physician, fail to accomplish the cure. **Verses 14–14½** give the empirical proof: the other three factors being given, serious diseases demanding attention and treatment are sometimes seen to vanish "like a city of illusion" and at other times to be aggravated — the difference lies in the physician, wise in the first instance and ignorant in the second.

## The Risks of Treatment by a Quack

> Verses 15–18. The strongest language in the chapter.

**Verses 15–16** condemn the quack in the chapter's most forceful passage: it is better for the quack to offer himself up in the fire than to enforce his treatment on the patient; like a blind man groping about with his hands in fearful uncertainty, or a rudderless canoe left to the mercy of the winds, the ignorant physician proceeds in his work full of uncertainty and fear. **Verse 17** describes the mechanism of quack harm: emboldened by meeting with providential success in the treatment of a patient destined to survive, the pretentious quack hastens to death a hundred others whose tenure of life is not so definite. **Verse 18** gives the standard: the physician who possesses the fourfold accomplishment of **theoretical knowledge, clear interpretation, right application and practical experience** is to be regarded as the redeemer of life.

## The Complete Physician

> Verses 19–23. Knowledge, understanding and the name of physician.

**Verse 19** specifies the fourfold knowledge that qualifies the best of physicians — **etiology, symptomatology, therapeutics and prophylaxis** — and declares such a man worthy of honour by the king and fit to be the royal physician. **Verse 20** locates the true seat of medicine: weapons, learning and water are wholly dependent for their merits and demerits on their holder; hence it is the understanding that should first of all be rendered immaculate and worthy of holding the knowledge of medicine. **Verse 21** lists the six qualities of the healer — knowledge, imagination, comprehension, memory, promptness and action — for him who possesses these, there is no curable disease that cannot be cured. **Verse 22** lowers the threshold for the mere name: knowledge, intelligence, experience, practice, accomplishment and guidance — any single one of these suffices to give its possessor the name of physician. **Verse 23** reserves the title of "good physician" for one who has all these qualities, making him a benefactor of humanity.

## The Duty and Conduct of the Physician

> Verses 24–26. Vision, self-culture and the fourfold attitude.

**Verse 24** states the physician's working instrument: the science is the light for elucidation; the vision is one's own intellect; the physician, well endowed with both, errs not in treatment. **Verse 25** draws the practical conclusion: inasmuch as the other three factors of treatment are dependent on himself, the physician must ever strive to enrich his qualities. **Verse 26** closes with the celebrated fourfold nature of the physician's profession — **friendship towards all, compassion for the ailing, devotion to curable patients, and a sense of resignation towards the dying**.

## Key Points for Study

- **Four factors, sixteen qualities**: physician (knowledge, experience, skill, purity), drugs (abundance, applicability, multifarious modes, quality), attendant (nursing knowledge, skill, affection, cleanliness), patient (recollection, obedience, courage, ability to describe) — the complete specification of the therapeutic team.
- **The definitions**: discordance of body-elements is disease; concordance is health; therapeusis is the combined operation of the four factors to restore concordance.
- **The physician is the chief**: the other three factors are instruments — like fuel to the cook, weapon to the conqueror, wheel to the potter; the cure stands or falls with the physician.
- **The quack is a public danger**: the ignorant physician, after providential success, hastens a hundred others to death; the standard is fourfold accomplishment — theoretical knowledge, clear interpretation, right application and practical experience.
- **Ethics before skill**: the chapter's last word is the physician's fourfold conduct — friendship, compassion, devotion to the curable and resignation towards the dying.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the four factors of treatment in Ayurveda (cikitsa-chatuspada)?

The four basic factors of treatment are the physician (bhisaj), the drugs (dravya), the attendant or nurse (upasthatri) and the patient (rogin). Possessed of their required qualities, these four lead to the earliest cure of disease; therapy is defined as their combined operation to restore the concordance of the body-elements.

### What are the sixteen qualities (desiderata) of the four factors?

Each factor has four qualities: the physician — theoretical knowledge, practical experience, skill and purity of body and mind; the drugs — abundance, applicability, usability in multifarious modes and richness of quality; the attendant — knowledge of nursing, skill, affection for the patient and cleanliness; the patient — recollection, obedience to instructions, courage and the ability to describe his ailments.

### Why is the physician the foremost factor in treatment?

The physician alone is the knower of disease and drugs, the instructor of the attendant and patient, and the prescriber of medicine and regimen. Charaka argues by analogy — the other three factors are like fuel and fire to the cook, like terrain and weapon to the conqueror, like clay and wheel to the potter: effective only in the hands of the master.

### What are the qualifications of a good physician in Ayurveda?

The complete physician has the fourfold accomplishment of theoretical knowledge, clear interpretation, right application and practical experience; the fourfold knowledge of etiology, symptomatology, therapeutics and prophylaxis; and the six qualities of knowledge, imagination, comprehension, memory, promptness and action. His conduct is fourfold: friendship towards all, compassion for the ailing, devotion to the curable and resignation towards the dying.

### What happens when treatment is given by a quack?

Charaka is emphatic: it is better for the quack to offer himself up in the fire than to enforce his treatment on the patient. Emboldened by providential success in a patient destined to survive, the pretentious quack hastens to death a hundred others whose tenure of life is not so definite.

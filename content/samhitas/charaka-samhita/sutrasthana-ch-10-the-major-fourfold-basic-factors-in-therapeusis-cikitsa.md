# Chapter 10 - The Major Fourfold Basic Factors in Therapeusis (cikitsa)

## TL;DR — Executive Clinical Summary

**The Major Fourfold Basic Factors in Therapeusis (cikitsa)** — Sutrasthana Chapter 10 of the Charaka Samhita is the sequel to the doctrine of the four pillars of treatment (cikitsā-catuṣpāda). Here the sage Maitreya raises the sharpest objection in classical Indian medicine: *"treatment is in no way better than no treatment,"* since patients die despite skilled care and recover without any. Atreya's reply establishes the enduring principle that **treatment acts only within the limits of curability** — the physician's task is first to determine, by differential diagnosis, whether a disease is curable (sadhya), formidable, palliable, or irremediable (asadhya), and to act accordingly. The chapter gives the characteristic signs of each class, and warns that the physician who undertakes irremediable cases loses income, learning, fame and social standing.

---

## Introduction

The previous chapter established the four factors of therapeusis — the physician, the drugs, the attendant and the patient — each adorned with four qualities, making therapeusis "four-pillared and sixteen-faceted." Chapter 10 takes the argument one step further. If treatment depends on four factors, why do some patients die even when all four are perfect? And why do others recover with no treatment at all?

The exchange between Maitreya and Atreya recorded here is one of the most quoted philosophical passages of the Charaka Samhita, because it settles the *epistemological* status of medicine: therapy is neither omnipotent nor useless, but effective within a defined domain. The chapter then builds the clinical instrument that this insight demands — a formal classification of diseases by curability, with observable signs for each class — which remains the backbone of Ayurvedic prognosis (roga-parijnana) and of the ethical rule that a physician must decline cases beyond the reach of art.

## The Four Pillars, Recalled

3. "The physicians speak of therapeusis as being four-pillared and sixteen-faceted; the same was spoken of in the previous chapter as having sixteen attributes. Now this therapeusis, applied skilfully, is sufficient for the restoration of health." Thus said the worshipful Punarvasu, the son of Atri.

The declaration opens with a summary of the doctrine of [the four factors of treatment](/samhitas/charaka-samhita/sutrasthana-ch-09-the-four-basic-factors-of-therapeusis-cikitsa-chatuspada): the physician (bhishak), the drugs (dravya), the attendant (upasthata) and the patient (rogi). Skilful application of the complete apparatus is declared sufficient for restoration of health. The word "skilfully" carries the whole weight of the chapter — it implies a physician who knows *when* treatment applies, and to *whom*.

## Maitreya's Proposition — "Treatment Is No Better than No Treatment"

4-(1). "No," said Maitreya. "But why? Because there are seen some patients possessed of means, having attendants, self-controlled and treated by expert physicians, recovering, while some others, though enjoying similar amenities, dying nevertheless. Thus treatment becomes a thing of no consequence."

4-(2). "It is comparable to a few drops of water sprinkled in a pit or lake, or to a handful of dust scattered on a flowing river or on a dust-heap."

4-(3). "Conversely, we see others, devoid of means, with no attendants, no self-control, and treated by unskilled physicians, regaining health; and yet others, similarly circumstanced, dying all the same."

4. "Thus, recovery follows treatment, death too follows treatment. Similarly, recovery follows in the absence of treatment, and in the absence of treatment, death too follows. Hence it is to be concluded that treatment is in no way better than no treatment."

Maitreya's argument is a genuine logical puzzle, and Charaka preserves it in full rather than caricaturing it. Observed outcomes — recovery and death — occur in both the treated and the untreated groups. If the outcome is the same with and without the intervention, the intervention is causally inert. This is, in modern terms, the problem of **confounding**: sicker patients may be treated more aggressively, and patients destined to recover may recover regardless.

## Atreya's Decision — The Domain of Treatment

5-(1). "Maitreya! you think wrongly," said Atreya; "and how? Because what has been stated by you, that patients though given the systematic treatment possessed of all the sixteen qualities die, is not sound. Treatment does not become inconsequential in regard to diseases amenable to treatment."

5-(2). "And, again, as regards those who recover without the aid of any treatment, even in their case, there is a special reason for giving them the complete course of treatment."

5-(3). "Just as a man, by lifting another who has fallen although the latter is able to rise by himself, gives him support in consequence of which he rises sooner and without difficulty, in like manner do patients, receiving the aid of a complete treatment, recover more easily and without difficulty."

5-(4). "As regards those patients who die in spite of full treatment, not all of them could be expected to recover by the blessings of treatment. For not all diseases are amenable to treatment and yet, of those diseases as are amenable to treatment, cure cannot be accomplished except by treatment. However, the whole pharmacopeia will fail in curing irremediable diseases; and no physician, however clever, is able to save a dying patient."

5. "Hence, only those that act after investigation are considered wise. As a bowman who is a good marksman and given to constant practice, taking up a bow and releasing an arrow, does not fail in hitting a big target that is not too far off and achieves his purpose, so does a physician of accomplishment and means, who starts treating a curable disease after full investigation, without fail bestow health on the patient. Hence it cannot be said that treatment is no better than non-treatment."

Atreya's refutation has three movements, each of which anticipates a modern statistical principle:

- **Outcomes are conditional, not universal.** Treatment acts "in regard to diseases amenable to treatment." The observed deaths do not falsify therapy; they define its limits.
- **The treated recover *sooner* and *without difficulty*.** Even where recovery would occur naturally, treatment changes the *speed and ease* of the course — the analogy of the man who lifts another who could rise by himself.
- **The appropriate comparison is within the curable class.** The bowman does not miss a target that is "not too far off." The physician's skill is to choose the targets — the curable cases — where the arrow of treatment will strike.

The closing simile is the chapter's signature: medicine is marksmanship, and marksmanship begins with the correct choice of target.

## The Physician's Art — Treatment by Opposites

6. "This much is evident to us all, viz., we treat a disease-ridden man with disease-removing measures and the depleted man with impletion. We nourish the emaciated and the feeble; we starve the corpulent and the fatty, and treat the man afflicted by heat with cooling measures, and with hot things him who is afflicted with cold. We replenish body-elements that have suffered decrease, and deplenish those that have undergone increase. By treating disorders properly with what are antagonistic to their causative factors, we restore the patient to the normal condition. In our hands, administered in this manner, the pharmacopeia shows itself to the best of its excellence."

Verse 6 states the operational principle of Ayurvedic therapeutics: **treatment by antagonism (viruddha-upacara)** — counter the morbid factor with its opposite in quality, direction and potency. This is the classic statement of samanya-vishesha applied to therapy: increase what has decreased, decrease what has increased, cool what is hot, warm what is cold.

7. "The physician who knows the differential diagnosis between the curable and the incurable among diseases and begins treatment with full knowledge of the case and in time, obtains success in his effort with certainty."

## Loss of Reputation by Taking Up Incurable Cases

8. "But the physician who undertakes to treat incurable diseases will invariably suffer loss of income, will tarnish his learning and fame, and earn for himself disrepute and taboo in society."

Charaka is blunt about the professional consequences of treating beyond the reach of art. The passage is not merely prudential — it encodes the ethical doctrine that the physician's reputation rests on *honest prognosis*. The doctor who promises cure where none is possible trades long-term trust for short-term gain, and loses both.

## The Fourfold Division of Diseases

9. "The curable diseases are of two kinds: those that are easily cured and those that are cured with difficulty. The incurable diseases also fall into two categories: those that are palliable and those that are absolutely irremediable."

10. "The curables are, again, classed into three fresh categories by reason of their requiring mild, moderate or strong treatment. The incurable ones, being inexorable, admit of no such classification."

The taxonomy of prognosis in a single verse:

| Class | Sub-classes | Treatment stance |
|---|---|---|
| Curable (sadhya) | easily cured · cured with difficulty | treat confidently; strength of treatment graded mild, moderate or strong |
| Incurable (asadhya) | palliable (yapya) · absolutely irremediable | palliate and comfort only; never promise cure |

## Easily Curable Diseases

11-13. The characteristics of an easily curable disease are: the causes, premonitory symptoms and symptoms are mild; the morbific factor is homologatory neither to the affected body-element nor to the habitus of the patient, nor to the traits of the prevailing season; the place of disease is not inaccessible to treatment; the course of disease is localised in one system, is recent, has no complication and is born of the predominant morbidity of only a single humor; the body is in a condition to withstand all treatment and the fourfold requisites of treatment are at hand. These are the circumstances in which a disease is easily curable.

The list reads like a modern prognostic checklist. Favorable signs: mild etiology, mild premonitory and manifest symptoms; the disease-causing factor is *antagonistic* (viruddha) to the patient's constitution, body-elements and season — meaning the body's natural resistance works *with* the treatment; accessible site; single-system involvement; recent onset; uncomplicated; single-dosha origin; a body strong enough to bear therapy; and the fourfold equipment available.

## Formidable Diseases

14-16. The formidable diseases are those wherein the causes, premonitory symptoms and symptoms are of moderate strength; when any one of the triad, viz., season, habitus and susceptibility of body-elements, is homologatory to the morbific factor; the ailments of a gravida, of the aged and of children; those that are not much aggravated by complication; those requiring operative, caustic and cauterizing procedures; those that have gone beyond the incipient stage; those that are located in a part difficult of access; those whose course is localized in one system; those in which the full complement of the fourfold medical equipment is not available; those which have spread through two body-systems but have not become very chronic; and those due to the predominant morbidity of only two humors.

The formidable (kricchra-sadhya) class is a study in escalation: moderate severity; even one factor of season, habitus or tissue-susceptibility aligning with the disease; vulnerable populations (the pregnant, the aged, the child); the need for surgery, cautery or caustics; deep-seated or inaccessible location; incomplete therapeutic resources; two doshas involved; two systems affected. Each item adds one degree of difficulty to the cure.

## Palliable and Irremediable Diseases

17-20. The diseases of the following description are to be regarded as incurable but palliable: those in which the patient, being still possessed of a part of life-span, needs to be kept going by means of strict regimen; those which admit of slight relief, but which get readily aggravated from slight causes; those which are deep-seated; those which affect many body-elements; those which have lodged themselves in vital parts and joints; those which are constantly relapsing; those which are long-standing; and those which are born of the discordance of only two humors.

The palliable (yapya) class marks the borderland of medicine: the disease cannot be eradicated, but the patient can be sustained — "kept going by means of strict regimen." Charaka thus formally recognizes chronic disease management: the goal shifts from cure to maintenance, comfort and the prolongation of life.

The following diseases are both incurable and immitigable: those which are similar to the above, excepting those wherein some expectation of life and the possibility of some relief still remain; those which are born of the discordance of all the three humors; those which have gone beyond the stage of treatment; those which have spread to all the systems of the body; those which give rise to sudden and extreme excitement, restlessness and stupefaction; those which destroy the sense-faculties; those which, afflicting weakened constitutions, get greatly aggravated; and those which are accompanied with the fatal prognostic signs.

The irremediable (asadhya) class is defined by terminal features: involvement of all three doshas; spread through all systems; loss of sense-faculties; sudden agitation, restlessness and stupefaction; fatal prognostic signs (the arishta-lakshanas of the Indriyasthana); and a body too weak to mount any response. Where the palliable patient retains some life-expectancy and some possibility of relief, the irremediable patient retains neither.

## The Advantage of Differential Diagnosis

21. "A wise physician should first examine the signs and symptoms of the disease and only then commence treatment of curable ones."

22. "One who knows the differential diagnosis between curable and incurable diseases, as also the right mode of application, will not fall into such erroneous mode of thinking as Maitreya and others did."

The chapter closes by converting the philosophical debate into a clinical discipline. The physician who can classify disease by curability — and who knows the right mode of application for each class — is immunized against two errors at once: the nihilism of Maitreya ("treatment is useless") and the hubris of the quack ("treatment can cure anything").

23-24. Here in this Major chapter on the "Fourfold Basic Factors of Therapeusis" have been laid down: treatment, its composition and character, the result proceeding from treatment, the difference of opinion between Atreya and Maitreya, the decision concerning this doctrinal difference, and the fourfold classification of curability and incurability of diseases, together with the characteristics of each class.

10. Thus, in the Section on General Principles, in the treatise compiled by Agnivesha and revised by Caraka, the tenth Major chapter entitled "The Fourfold Basic Factors in Therapeusis (cikitsā)" is completed.

## Key Points for Study

- **The skeptical challenge**: Maitreya's argument that recovery and death occur with and without treatment — the classical formulation of the confounding problem in therapeutics.
- **Atreya's threefold reply**: treatment acts within the class of curable diseases; it speeds and eases recovery even where recovery is natural; the bowman chooses the target within range.
- **Treatment by opposites (viruddha-upacara)**: cool the heated, warm the chilled, replenish the depleted, deplenish the excessive — restore equilibrium.
- **The fourfold prognosis**: easily curable, formidable, palliable, irremediable — each with its own therapeutic stance and ethical obligations.
- **The ethical rule**: the physician who takes up irremediable cases loses income, learning, fame and social standing; honest prognosis is the foundation of medical trust.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why does Charaka Samhita Chapter 10 say treatment is "no better than no treatment"?

That is Maitreya's skeptical proposition, raised and then refuted. He observes that patients die despite full treatment and recover without it. Atreya answers that treatment acts within the domain of curable diseases — the dying were never within the bowman's range — and that even where recovery would occur naturally, complete treatment makes it sooner and easier. The chapter's conclusion is the opposite of Maitreya's: differential diagnosis, not nihilism, is the physician's safeguard.

### What are the fourfold basic factors of therapeusis (cikitsa)?

The physician (bhishak), the drugs (dravya), the attendant (upasthata) and the patient (rogi) — each with four qualities, making therapeusis "four-pillared and sixteen-faceted." Chapter 9 establishes the doctrine; Chapter 10 defends its efficacy against Maitreya's objection and adds the classification of curability that governs how the four factors are deployed.

### How does Ayurveda classify curable and incurable diseases?

Curable (sadhya) diseases are of two kinds — easily cured and cured with difficulty. Incurable (asadhya) diseases are also of two kinds — palliable (yapya), which can be managed by strict regimen and comfort, and absolutely irremediable, marked by all three doshas in discordance, spread through all systems, loss of sense-faculties and fatal prognostic signs.

### What makes a disease easily curable in Ayurveda?

Mild causes, premonitory symptoms and symptoms; a morbific factor antagonistic to the body-elements, habitus and season (so the body's resistance works with treatment); an accessible site; single-system, recent, uncomplicated disease of single-dosha origin; a body able to bear treatment; and the full fourfold therapeutic equipment at hand.

### Why does Charaka warn physicians against treating incurable cases?

Verse 8 states the professional and ethical consequence: the physician who undertakes irremediable diseases suffers loss of income, tarnishes his learning and fame, and earns disrepute and taboo in society. The deeper principle is that honest prognosis protects the trust on which the physician-patient relationship rests — the physician must decline what art cannot accomplish.

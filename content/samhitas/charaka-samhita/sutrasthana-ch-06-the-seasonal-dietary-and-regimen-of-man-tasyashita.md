# Chapter 6 - The Seasonal Dietary and Regimen of Man (tasyashita)

## TL;DR — Executive Clinical Summary

**Sutrasthana Chapter 6** is the classical source of **ritucharya**, the seasonal dietary and regimen of man (*tasyashita*). It divides the year into six seasons under the two solar courses — the northern course (*uttarayana*), a period of absorption dominated by fire, and the southern course (*dakshinayana*), a period of liberation dominated by water — and shows how bodily strength waxes and wanes with them. For each season it then prescribes a complete code: food, drink, exercise, oiling, bathing, sleep, residence and even sexual conduct. The chapter closes with the doctrine of **homologation** — the deliberate, habitual adaptation of diet and behaviour to one's climate and to the diseases prevalent there.

---

## Introduction

Having established in Chapter 5 how much one should eat, the Sages now address **when and what** one should eat across the turning year. The teaching rests on a single physiological observation: the digestive fire, the humours and bodily strength all fluctuate with the sun's course, the moon's radiance and the winds. Health, therefore, is a matter of **matching regimen to season** — eating and living in a way that counteracts the seasonal tendency of each humour, rather than feeding it.

The chapter is addressed by **Atreya Punarvasu** through the compilation of **Agnivesha**, revised by **Charaka**. Its seasonal code remains the basis of Ayurvedic preventive practice to this day.

## The Two Solar Courses and the Six Seasons

> Verses 1–8. The frame of the doctrine.

**Verse 3** opens with the principle of **seasonal homologation**: the man who knows the correspondence between season, behaviour and diet promotes his vigour and complexion. **Verses 4–5** set the astronomical frame: the year divides into six parts — from the dewy season to summer is the sun's northern course and period of **absorption**; from the rains to winter is the southern course and period of **liberation**. In liberation the moon, unabated, saturates the earth with cool radiance, so this period is dominated by the **water element**; in absorption the sun and drying winds dominate, so the period is dominated by the **fire element**. Sun, wind and moon — governed by time and their own natures — are the causative factors of periods, seasons, tastes, humours and bodily strength.

**Verse 6** describes the weakening effect of absorption: the sun's hot rays and the piercing dry winds dehydrate the earth, promote the three dry tastes (bitter, astringent, pungent), and lead to the gradual waning of strength. **Verse 7** describes the strengthening effect of release: with the sun turned south and shorn of glory, the moon undiminished and the summer heat quenched by rain, the tastes sour, salt and sweet increase in order, and the strength of man waxes.

**Verse 8** plots the strength curve across the year: vigour is lowest at the beginning of liberation and the end of absorption; moderate in the middle of each period; and **highest at the end of liberation and the beginning of absorption** — that is, roughly from autumn into winter, when the accumulated nourishment of the southern course meets the body's need for heavy food.

## The Regimen of Winter (Hemanta)

> Verses 9–18. The season of strong digestion.

**Verse 9** states the key physiology of winter: the gastric fire, hemmed in by cold air all around, becomes greatly enhanced in strong men and capable of digesting heavy food, both in measure and quality. **Verse 10** warns of the consequence of failing to feed it: an under-fuelled fire consumes the body fluids, and cold-natured vata is provoked.

The prescriptions follow logically (**verses 11–15**): unctuous, acid and salt preparations of the flesh of fatty aquatic and wetland animals; spit-roasted flesh of the burrowing and tearer groups followed by *madira* and *seedhu* wines with honey; habitual milk, sugarcane preparations, animal fat, oil, new rice and warm water to protect the life-span; inunction, oil massage, oil shampoo, hot-house sudation, sun-baths and warm apartments. **Verse 15** extends the warmth to furniture: well-covered vehicles, beds and seats, with thick quilts, deer- or tiger-skin, silk and variegated blankets.

**Verses 16–17** add the personal and social note: wear warm thick clothing, anoint the body with thick eagle-wood paste, and — the text is explicit — winter is the season in which one may indulge in sexual enjoyment to one's heart's content. **Verse 18** gives the avoidance list: light and vata-provoking foods and drinks, draughts, restricted diet and diluted demulcent drinks.

## The Regimen of the Dewy Season (Shishira)

> Verses 19–21. Winter, with added dryness and cold.

**Verses 19–19½** observe that winter and the dewy season are similar in nature, with one distinction: the dewy season adds the dryness of the sun's absorbing period and the cold born of clouds, wind and rain. **Verses 20–21** therefore repeat the entire winter regimen, with one rule tightened — residence in draughtless, warm apartments is to be observed even more stringently — and with pungent, bitter, astringent, vata-provoking, light and cold foods and drinks added to the avoidance list.

## The Regimen of Spring (Vasanta)

> Verses 22–26. Draining the liquefied kapha.

**Verse 22** names the seasonal pathology of spring: the kapha accumulated through winter is liquefied by the hot rays of the sun, disturbs the body heat and gives rise to many diseases. **Verse 23** therefore prescribes purification — emesis and similar procedures — and the avoidance of heavy, acid, unctuous and sweet articles and of day-sleep.

**Verses 24–26** complete the code: physical exercise, dry massage, smoking, gargles, collyrium and frequent ablutions with genially warm water; anointing with sandalwood paste; a meal of barley or wheat with the flesh of wapiti, rabbit, Indian antelope, grey quail and grey partridge; and wholesome *seedhu* or honey-wine — the season of vigour, youth and the woods.

## The Regimen of Summer (Grishma)

> Verses 27–32. Cooling and conserving.

**Verse 27** opens with the image of the sun drinking up the unctuous element of the earth; the summer diet must therefore be sweet, cool, liquid and unctuous. **Verse 28** specifies cold demulcent drinks mixed with sugar, the flesh of *jangala* (arid-zone) animals and birds, ghee, milk and shali rice, to escape the enervating effects of the season.

**Verse 29** adds the restraints: little or no wine — or wine diluted liberally with water — and the avoidance of salt, sour, pungent and hot foods and of exercise. **Verses 30–31** paint the ideal summer day: sandal paste on the body, sleep by day in a cool apartment, and by night on a terrace cooled by the moon and open to the breezes, adorned with pearls and gems, ministered to by fans and the cool caresses of sandal-watered hands. **Verse 32** counsels abstinence from sexual intercourse and the coolness of woods, waters and flowers.

## The Regimen of the Rains (Varsha)

> Verses 33–40. The season of moderation.

**Verses 33–34½** describe the precarious state of the rains: the body is impoverished by the summer sun, the digestive power is weak, and the humours — aided by moist-hot exhalations from the earth, the rainfall and the tendency of water to acidity — are readily provoked. The general rule is **moderation**.

**Verses 35–36** list the avoidances: watery demulcent drink, day-sleep, frosts, river-water, exercise, sun and sexual intercourse; and the prescription: well-seasoned foods and drinks combined with honey. **Verse 37** allows an exception — on very cold, stormy, rainy days, take unctuous articles with pronounced acid and salt tastes to alleviate vata. **Verses 38–39** guard the fire with old barley, wheat and shali rice with the flesh and well-seasoned soups of *jangala* animals, and a small measure of decocted honey-wine or boiled-and-cooled rain, well or lake water with honey. **Verse 40** prescribes friction-massage, dry massage, bathing, perfumes and garlands, light clean clothing and residence in a rain-house free from damp.

## The Regimen of Autumn (Sharad)

> Verses 41–48. Pacifying the provoked pitta.

**Verse 41** explains the autumnal danger: the body habituated to the cold of the rains is suddenly heated by the autumn sun, and the accumulated pitta is generally provoked. **Verse 42** therefore prescribes sweet, light, cooling and slightly bitter foods and drinks that are curative of pitta, taken in due dose and only when truly hungry.

**Verses 43–45** give the detail: at the beginning of autumn, the flesh of grey quail, grey partridge, black buck, wild sheep, wapiti and hare with shali rice, barley and wheat; then, once the rain clouds have disappeared, a potion of ghee medicated with bitter articles, purgation and depletion of blood, and avoidance of the sun. Avoid animal fats, oils, frosts, the flesh of aquatic and wetland animals, alkalis, curds, day-sleep and easterly winds.

**Verses 46–47** introduce the famous **Hamsodaka** — the celestial water of autumn: heated by the sun by day, cooled by the moon by night, ripened and purified by time and detoxicated by the radiations of the star Agastya (Canopus), clear and pure, beloved of swans. Water for bathing, drinking or immersion in this season is as beneficial to the body as ambrosia. **Verse 48** adds garlands of autumnal flowers, spotless garments and moonlight in the early part of the night.

## The Principle of Homologation

> Verses 49–50. The closing doctrine.

**Verse 49** defines the concept on which the whole chapter rests: that which becomes homological by habitual use is called **acquired homologation** — the body's capacity to adapt to a regimen through consistent practice. **Verse 50** draws the clinical conclusion: the knowers of homologation consider it desirable to acquire adaptation to food and behaviour that are **antagonistic to the characteristics of one's country and to the causative factors of the diseases prevalent there** — the classical formula for climate- and region-specific preventive medicine.

## Key Points for Study

- **Two solar courses, one principle**: the entire chapter follows from the observation that the sun's course controls moisture, taste and digestive strength. Regimen is not a list of rules but a response to physiology.
- **Strength curve**: vigour is lowest at the transitions of the sun's course, highest from the end of the southern course into winter — which is why winter allows the heaviest food and the fullest sexual life, and summer demands the lightest.
- **Seasonal humour logic**: kapha accumulates in winter and must be drained in spring (emesis, dry massage, exercise); pitta accumulates in the rains and must be pacified in autumn (cooling bitters, purgation); vata is provoked by the cold dryness of winter and the dewy season and by the winds of the rains — each season's code counteracts its own humour.
- **Homologation is the takeaway**: the chapter's deepest teaching is adaptability — deliberate, gradual, habitual adaptation to one's place and its diseases, the classical ancestor of personalised and climate-sensitive preventive medicine.
- **Practical continuity**: modern *ritucharya* protocols in Ayurvedic clinics are this chapter, annotated with current food availability.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is ritucharya?

Ritucharya is the Ayurvedic seasonal regimen — the systematic adaptation of diet, exercise, sleep, hygiene and lifestyle to each of the six seasons, as taught in Sutrasthana Chapter 6 of the Charaka Samhita. The word combines *ritu* (season) and *achara* (conduct).

### What are the six seasons and their order?

The year divides into six seasons: winter (*hemanta*), the dewy season (*shishira*), spring (*vasanta*), summer (*grishma*), the rains (*varsha*) and autumn (*sharad*). The first three belong to the sun's northern course (*uttarayana*, the period of absorption), the last three to the southern course (*dakshinayana*, the period of liberation).

### Why is digestion strong in winter and weak in summer?

The text teaches that the gastric fire is hemmed in by cold in winter and becomes greatly enhanced, capable of digesting heavy food; in summer the sun dries the body's unctuous element, so the diet must be sweet, cool and light. The strength of the digestive fire follows the sun's course — strong in the cold season, weak in the hot.

### What is Hamsodaka?

Hamsodaka is the "swan-loved" celestial water of autumn — water heated by the sun by day, cooled by the moon by night, purified by time and detoxicated by the star Canopus (Agastya), clear and pure. Its use for bathing, drinking and immersion is said to be as beneficial as ambrosia.

### Can I practise ritucharya in a modern climate?

Yes — that is exactly what the chapter intends. The closing doctrine of homologation instructs adaptation to one's own country and its prevalent diseases. The seasonal principles (heavy warm food in winter, cooling light food in summer, draining kapha in spring) can be translated into any climate by substituting locally available foods for the classical ones.

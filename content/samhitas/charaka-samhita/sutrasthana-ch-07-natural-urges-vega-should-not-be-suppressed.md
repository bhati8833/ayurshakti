# Chapter 7 - Natural urges (vega) should not be suppressed

## TL;DR — Executive Clinical Summary

**Sutrasthana Chapter 7** teaches the management of the natural urges (*vega*) — the thirteen bodily urges that should never be suppressed, the diseases each suppression causes, and the specific remedy for each. It then draws the crucial distinction: while bodily urges are to be released, the **evil impulses of mind, speech and body** (greed, anger, harsh speech, injury to others) are to be restrained. The chapter continues with the classical science of physical exercise (*vyayama*) — its definition, virtues, the signs of its full measure, the evils of excess and those who should not exercise — and closes with the doctrine of **gradual change** in habits, the role of constitution (*prakriti*), the seasonal purificatory regimen, the prevention of exogenous diseases through right conduct, and the famous rules for taking curds.

---

## Introduction

The teaching of Chapter 6 was that regimen must follow the season. The teaching of Chapter 7 is that regimen must follow the **body's own signals** — and the conscience. Some urges arise from the body and must be honoured; others arise from the passions and must be mastered. Getting these two directions wrong is, in the classical view, a major cause of disease: suppressing a bodily urge provokes its humour and its organ; indulging a mental impulse corrupts conduct. This chapter is thus the classical foundation for both preventive medicine and what we would today call self-regulation and habit psychology.

## The Thirteen Natural Urges

> Verses 1–4. The scope of the chapter.

**Verses 1–2** open the chapter in the usual form — "We shall now expound the chapter entitled 'Natural urges (vega) should not be suppressed,'" declared by the worshipful Atreya.

**Verses 3–4** state the rule that gives the chapter its name: a wise person should not suppress the natural urges of **urine, faeces, semen, flatus, vomiting, sneezing, eructation, yawning, hunger, thirst, tears, sleep and deep breathing after exertion**. These thirteen are treated as physiological necessities, each with its own pathology of suppression and its own remedy.

## Diseases Born of Suppression — and Their Remedies

> Verses 5–24. The paired pathology and therapy of each urge.

**Verse 5** frames the teaching as therapeutic: the diseases born of suppression are described one by one "for the purpose of treatment."

### Suppression of the Urge for Micturition (verses 6–7)

Pain in the region of the bladder and genitals, dysuria, headache, flexure of the body, and retention of urine with distension of the lower abdomen. The remedy: sudation, immersion bath, inunction, an oppressive dose of ghee, and the **triple enema** — two kinds of rectal enema and one urethral douche.

### Suppression of the Urge for Defecation (verses 8–9)

Intestinal colic, headache, retention of flatus and faeces, cramps in the calf-muscles and distension of the abdomen. The remedy: sudation, inunction, immersion bath, suppositories, enemata and carminative foods and drinks.

### Suppression of the Urge for Ejaculation (verses 10–11)

Pain in the phallus and testes, body-ache, cardiac pain and retention of urine. The remedy: inunction, immersion bath, *madira* wine, the flesh of the cock, shali rice, milk, evacuative enema and sexual intercourse.

### Suppression of the Urge for Flatus (verses 12–13)

Retention of faeces, urine and flatus, distension of the abdomen, pain, exhaustion and other vata disorders of the abdomen. The remedy: oleation and sudation, suppositories, carminative foods and drinks and enemata.

### Suppression of the Urge for Vomiting (verses 14–15)

Pruritus, wheals, anorexia, freckles, anaemia, fever, dermatosis, nausea and acute spreading affections. The remedy: emesis after taking food, smoking, starvation, depletion of blood, dry foods and drinks, physical exercise and purgation.

### Suppression of the Urge for Sneezing (verses 16–17)

Stiffness of the neck, headache, facial paralysis, hemicrania and asthenia of the sense-organs. The remedy: inunction, sudation of the upper supra-clavicular parts of the body, smoking, nasal medication, a vata-alleviating diet and a postprandial potion of ghee.

### Suppression of the Urge for Eructation (verse 18)

Hiccup, dyspnoea, anorexia, tremor and impediment to the functions of the heart and lungs. The remedy is the same as that prescribed for hiccup.

### Suppression of the Urge for Yawning (verse 19)

Flexures of the body, spasm, contractions, numbness, tremor and shaking. The remedy: all measures that alleviate vata.

### Suppression of the Urge for Hunger (verse 20)

Emaciation, weakness, discoloration, body-ache, anorexia and giddiness. The remedy: unctuous, hot and light diet.

### Suppression of the Urge for Thirst (verse 21)

Parching of the throat and mouth, deafness, fatigue, depression and cardiac pain. The remedy: cooling and demulcent drinks.

### Suppression of the Urge for Tears (verse 22)

Coryza, eye-diseases, cardiac disorders, anorexia and giddiness. The remedy: sleep, wine and pleasing conversation.

### Suppression of the Urge for Sleep (verse 23)

Yawning, body-ache, torpor, diseases of the head and heaviness of the eyes. The remedy: sleep and massage.

### Suppression of the Urge for Deep Breathing after Exertion (verse 24)

*Gulma* (abdominal tumour-like condition), cardiac disorder and stupefaction. The remedy: rest and measures that alleviate vata.

### The Condemnation of Restraint (verse 25)

The conclusion is stated plainly: one who wishes to avoid the diseases listed above should not suppress these urges.

## The Urges That Should Be Restrained

> Verses 26–30. The discipline of mind, speech and body.

The chapter now turns the instruction on its head. **Verse 26** declares that those who desire welfare in this and the next world should, on the other hand, suppress the evil impulses of mind, speech and body. **Verse 27** names the mental impulses: greed, grief, fear, anger, vanity, impudence, jealousy, excessive attachment and malice. **Verse 28** names the speech impulses: harsh, extravagant, insinuating, untrue and untimely speech. **Verse 29** names the bodily impulses: all activities injurious to others — adultery, theft, inflicting pain. **Verse 30** gives the reward: free from these sins of mind, speech and body, the happy man of righteous nature enjoys and acquires spiritual merit, wealth and sense-pleasures.

## Physical Exercise — the Science of Vyayama

> Verses 31–35. Definition, virtues, measure and limits.

**Verse 31** defines the subject: that activity of the body which is meant to increase its firmness and strength is regarded as physical exercise, and it should be practised in the right measure. **Verse 32** lists the six virtues of exercise: lightness, capacity for work, firmness, tolerance to hardship, subsidence of humoral discordance and stimulation of the gastric fire.

**Verse 33** lists the evils of over-exercise: fatigue, exhaustion, wasting, thirst, hemothermia, dyspnoea, asthma, cough, fever and vomiting. **Verse 33-(1)** gives the classical gauge of the full measure — perspiration, increased respiration, lightness of the limbs and a feeling of oppression in the cardiac region indicate that the exercise has reached its correct limit. **Verse 34** warns against excess in any pleasure: the wise man should not over-indulge in physical exercise, laughter, speaking, walking, the sex-act or waking at night, even if accustomed to them — **verse 35** adds that the person who does comes to a violent end, like a lion trying to drag the body of an elephant. **Verse 35-(1–2)** lists those who should avoid exercise altogether: the emaciated by excessive sex, load-carrying and way-faring; those afflicted with anger, grief, fear and toil; the very young and the very old; those of vata habitus; the loud and much-talking; and those afflicted with hunger and thirst.

## Withdrawing from Habits — the Doctrine of Gradual Change

> Verses 36–38. The psychology of habit.

**Verse 36** states the principle: by degrees the wise man frees himself from unwholesome habits and, also by degrees, develops wholesome ones. **Verse 37** gives the classical method — quarter steps of decrease for the bad habit and of increase for the good, applied at orderly intervals of one, two and three days. **Verse 38** gives the reason: by gradual withdrawal, addictions do not revert, and wholesome habits, gradually acquired, become firmly implanted — an anticipation, millennia old, of modern habit-formation research.

## Constitution — the Equibalanced and the Predominant

> Verses 39–41. Prakriti and its therapeutic consequence.

**Verse 39** observes that from the moment of conception some men are equibalanced in the three humours — vata, pitta and kapha — while some have a predominance of one. **Verse 40** draws the clinical consequence: the equibalanced alone enjoy perfect health, while the others are ever liable to disease, and their body-habitus is named according to the continual predominance of a particular humour. **Verse 41** prescribes accordingly: for the predominant types, the use of things antagonistic in quality to the predominant humour is beneficial; for the equibalanced, the balanced use of articles of all tastes is recommended as homologatory.

## The Excretory Passages and Their Disorders

> Verses 42–44. The emunctories in health and disease.

**Verse 42** describes the bodily architecture of elimination: two excretory orifices in the lower part of the trunk, seven cavities in the head, and innumerable openings of the sweat-glands all over the body; the emunctories are impaired by morbid or excessive formation of excretory products. **Verse 43** gives the diagnostic signs: excessive or scanty formation of excretory products is known by the enlargement or contraction of the emunctory concerned, and by excessive discharge or suppression of the excrement. **Verse 44** instructs the physician: diagnosing these disorders by their characteristic signs and morbidity, treat the curable by remedies antagonistic to the disease and its causative factors, with due regard to dose and time.

## Warding Off Endogenous Diseases — the Seasonal Purifications

> Verses 45–50. Preventive elimination and rejuvenation.

**Verse 45** gives the motive for the whole chapter's discipline: these and other diseases occur in those who do not observe the rules of healthful living, so the healthy man should be diligent in observing them. **Verses 46–47** prescribe the classical preventive course — elimination of accumulated morbid matter in the months of **Caitra (spring), Shravana (rains) and Margashirsha (early winter)**, by the purificatory procedures of emesis, purgation, enemata and errhines, after preparatory oleation and sudation. **Verse 48** adds the follow-through: the physician skilled in climatology should then administer alterative and virilific remedies of tested efficacy, systematically and as indicated. **Verse 49** states the result: the body-elements being restored to normal, susceptibility to disease disappears, the body-elements are aggrandized and the pace of age is slackened — the classical formula linking seasonal cleansing (*panchakarma*) to rejuvenation (*rasayana*).

## Volitional Transgression and Exogenous Disease

> Verses 51–55. The moral aetiology of external diseases.

**Verses 51–52** name the cause of exogenous diseases: those proceeding from evil spirits, poison, air, fire, trauma and similar agents — and likewise the psychic disorders of jealousy, grief, fear, anger, vanity and hatred — are all due to **volitional transgression** (*prajnaparadha*), the failure of right judgement. **Verses 53–54** give the prevention: avoidance of volitional transgression, control of the senses, recollection, knowledge of clime, season and the self, and observance of the rules of good conduct — to be practised, much before the onset of disease, as prophylaxis. **Verse 55** states the general principle: knowledge of authoritative instruction and its right application are the two factors necessary for the prevention and cure of diseases.

## Whom to Avoid, Whom to Associate With

> Verses 56–59. The company of men.

**Verses 56–57** describe those to be shunned — the sinful of conduct, speech and disposition; backbiters; the quarrelsome, sarcastic and niggardly; the envious, the cheating, the scandal-mongering and fickle-minded; those with a foot in the enemy's camp; the unrepentant and apostates; "the scum of humanity." **Verses 58–59** describe those to be sought — the mature in understanding, learning, years, character, courage, memory and one-mindedness; the insightful, the healthy, the well-disposed to all creatures, the tranquil of heart, the commendable of character, the teachers of the right path, and those who hear and see only what is meritorious. **Verse 60** closes the social teaching: the wise man who seeks happiness here and hereafter should exercise the highest care in selecting what is wholesome in food, conduct and behaviour.

## The Rules for Taking Curds

> Verses 61–62. A famous and practical prescription.

**Verse 61** codifies the classical rules for curds — still a staple of Ayurvedic dietary advice today: curds should not be taken at night, nor without ghee and sugar, nor without green-gram soup, nor without honey, nor hot, nor without emblic myrobalans (amalaki). **Verse 62** warns of the consequence of violating these rules: the curds-lover who does would be affected with fever, hemothermia, acute spreading affections, dermatosis, anaemia, giddiness and severe jaundice.

## Key Points for Study

- **The thirteen urges are non-negotiable**: urine, faeces, semen, flatus, vomiting, sneezing, eructation, yawning, hunger, thirst, tears, sleep and deep breathing after exertion — suppressing any of them provokes disease in its organ and its humour, and each has its own paired remedy.
- **Bodily urges vs mental impulses — the two disciplines**: the chapter's central contrast is between releasing bodily urges and restraining the evil impulses of mind, speech and body. Health requires getting both right.
- **Vyayama is a measured science**: exercise is defined by its goal (firmness and strength), valued for six specific virtues, gauged by four objective signs (perspiration, respiration, lightness, cardiac oppression) and forbidden for a specific list of constitutions and conditions.
- **Habits change by degrees**: withdrawal from addiction and formation of good habits both proceed by quarter steps at one-, two- and three-day intervals — the classical graded-change protocol.
- **Constitution dictates diet**: the equibalanced take all tastes in balance; the predominant counteract their humour — the seed of personalised (prakriti-based) nutrition.
- **Seasonal purification prevents endogenous disease**: elimination in spring, rains and early winter, followed by alterative and virilific remedies, restores the body-elements and slackens the pace of age.
- **Curds have rules**: not at night; always with ghee and sugar, or green-gram soup, or honey, or amalaki; never hot — among the most quoted of all Ayurvedic dietary injunctions.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the thirteen natural urges in Ayurveda?

The thirteen natural urges (*vega*) listed in Charaka Samhita Sutrasthana Chapter 7 are those of urine, faeces, semen, flatus, vomiting, sneezing, eructation, yawning, hunger, thirst, tears, sleep and deep breathing after exertion. None of them should be suppressed; each suppression produces its own disease and has its own remedy.

### What diseases result from suppressing natural urges?

Each urge has its own pathology — for example, suppressing urine causes bladder and genital pain, dysuria and headache; suppressing vomiting causes pruritus, wheals, anorexia and fever; suppressing hunger causes emaciation and weakness; suppressing sleep causes yawning, torpor and heaviness of the eyes. The chapter prescribes a specific remedy for each, from triple enema for urinary retention to emesis and purgation for suppressed vomiting.

### What does Ayurveda say about exercise?

Ayurveda treats exercise (*vyayama*) as a measured therapy: it should increase firmness and strength, confers lightness, capacity for work, firmness, tolerance to hardship, subsidence of humoral discordance and stimulation of the gastric fire — but excess causes fatigue, exhaustion, wasting, thirst, fever and vomiting. The correct measure is indicated by perspiration, increased respiration, lightness of the limbs and a feeling of oppression in the cardiac region. Children, the aged, the vata-dominant and the weak should avoid it.

### Why should curds not be eaten at night in Ayurveda?

Charaka prescribes that curds should not be taken at night, nor without ghee and sugar, nor without green-gram soup, nor without honey, nor hot, nor without amalaki (emblic myrobalans). Violating these rules is said to cause fever, hemothermia, acute spreading affections, dermatosis, anaemia, giddiness and severe jaundice — the basis of the common Ayurvedic advice to avoid curds at night.

### What is volitional transgression (prajnaparadha)?

Volitional transgression is the failure of right judgement — the intellectual error that leads to the wrong use of the senses, mind and body. Charaka attributes exogenous diseases (from poison, air, fire, trauma and similar causes) and psychic disorders (jealousy, grief, fear, anger, vanity, hatred) to it, and prescribes its avoidance — with control of the senses, recollection and good conduct — as the course of prevention.

# Chapter 5 - Measure in eating (matrashita)

## TL;DR — Executive Clinical Summary

**Sutrasthana Chapter 5** is the classical Ayurvedic text on **measure in eating** (*matrashita*) and, in its second half, the foundational chapter on the **daily personal regimen** (*dinacharya*). Its central teaching is that the correct quantity of food is relative — determined by the strength of one's digestive fire (*agni*), not by appetite, habit or social custom. The chapter then walks through the daily observances that keep the head, senses and body strong: eye-salves and collyrium, medicated smoking in three varieties, the *Anu* oil nasal toilet, tooth-twig and tongue scraper, oil gargles, head and ear oleation, body inunction, massage, bathing, attire and even footwear and the umbrella. Modern wellness movements around oil pulling, tongue scraping and nasya all trace their lineage to this single chapter.

## Introduction

After the pharmacology of external applications in Chapter 3, the Sages turn to the two questions that govern everyday health: **how much should one eat, and how should one live from morning to night?** The answer occupies one of the longest and most practical chapters of the Sutrasthana. It is a chapter of rules — but rules grounded in physiology: the strength of the gastric fire decides the measure of food; the humoral rhythm of *vata* and *kapha* decides the timing of smoking and nasal medication; and the structure of the head and neck decides which route a medicine should take.

The chapter is addressed by **Atreya Punarvasu** to the assembly of sages, through the compilation of **Agnivesha** as revised by **Charaka**. What follows is a faithful rendering of its sequence, with notes where modern practice may find it useful.

## Measure in Eating (Matrashita)

> Verses 1–8. The doctrine of food measure.

The chapter opens with the declaration that one must eat **in measure**, and that the measure is fixed by the strength of one's gastric fire (**verse 3**). The proper measure is defined operationally: that quantity which is digested **in due time without impairing health** (**verse 4**).

The text then introduces a crucial distinction (**verses 5–6**). Some foods are *light* by nature — shali and shashtika rice, green gram, quail, partridge, antelope, rabbit and sambar — being dominated by the qualities of air and fire; they stimulate the digestive fire and are relatively harmless even in surfeit. Others are *heavy* — pastry, sugarcane juice, milk, til, black gram, and the flesh of aquatic and wetland animals — dominated by earth and water; they are non-stimulative of the fire and harmful in excess unless the fire is stoked by hard exercise. **Verse 7** refines the rule: heavy articles should be taken in one-third or one-half of one's full capacity, while even light articles should respect the strength of the fire. **Verse 8** states the reward: a measured diet promotes strength, complexion, health and life instead of merely avoiding harm.

## The Articles of Diet to Be Eschewed

> Verses 9–11. Foods that should not be habitual.

Pastry, flattened rice and similar heavy articles should not be taken on top of a meal, even when hungry (**verse 9**). Dried meat and vegetables, lotus rhizomes and stalks, and the flesh of emaciated animals are not for habitual use (**verse 10**). Coagulated milk, cream cheese, pork, beef, buffalo flesh, fish, curds, black gram and wild barley are likewise to be avoided as a daily fare (**verse 11**).

## The Articles of Diet to Be Practised

> Verse 12. The daily staple.

The recommended daily diet is remarkably simple: shashtika and shali rice, green gram, rock-salt, emblic myrobalan (amalaki), barley, rain water, milk, ghee, the flesh of *jangala* (arid-zone) animals and honey. **Verse 13** states the aim of the daily diet in a single sentence: it should maintain present well-being and serve as a prophylactic against future disease.

## Care of the Eyes: Collyrium

> Verses 14–19½. The use of eye-salves.

From this point the chapter becomes a manual of personal regimen. **Verse 15** prescribes a daily antimony collyrium for the eyes, with an extract of Indian berberry once every fifth or eighth night to drain the eyes. The rationale (**verse 16**) is physiological: the eye is of the element of light, and is therefore specially liable to be disturbed by *kapha*, the watery humour — so measures that pacify *kapha* keep the vision clear.

**Verses 16½–19½** add two practical rules. Strong collyrium is not for daytime use, because eyes weakened by drainage are impaired by sunshine — the draining collyrium belongs to the night. And just as tarnished gold is cleansed with oil, cloth and brush, so the use of eye-salves and drops makes the vision shine like the moon in a clear sky.

## Medicated Smoking: The Three Cigars

> Verses 20–55½. Smoking as a therapeutic procedure — not a habit.

The next section describes **medicated smoking** (*dhumapana*) with the care of a pharmaceutical text. **Verses 20–24½** list the ingredients of the ordinary smoke — fragrant piper, perfumed cherry, cardamoms, fragrant poon, shell, sandalwood, cinnamon, cuscus grass, liquorice, nardus, guggul, eagle-wood, sugar, the barks of the fig trees, lodh, sarja sal, nut grass, lichen, lotus, pine resin, olibanum and angelica — ground into a paste, plastered over a reed, dried and moulded into a cigar the thickness of the thumb and eight fingers' breadth long.

Three varieties are distinguished: the **habitual smoke** (*snehana*), the **unctuous cigar** made of animal fat, ghee and wax with the sweet group of drugs (**verse 25**), and the **errhine smoke** that draws phlegm from the head, made with white mussel-shell creeper, staff plant, the arsenics, eagle-wood and cinnamon leaf (**verse 26**).

**Verse 27** gives the astonishing list of complaints for which smoking is the classical remedy — heaviness of head, headache, rhinitis, hemicrania, ear and eye ache, cough, hiccup, dyspnoea, throat spasm, dentalgia, anorexia, rigidity of jaw and neck, pruritus, worms, facial pallor, alopecia, greying hair, stupor and hypersomnia — while strengthening the hair, cranial bones, sense organs and voice.

**Verses 33–36** fix the **eight proper times** for smoking: after the bath, after tongue-scraping, after sneezing, after tooth cleansing, after nasal toilet, after the eye-salve, and at the end of sleep — times when *vata* and *kapha* rise. Habitual smoking is taken twice a day, in three puffs, three times; the unctuous smoke once a day; the errhine smoke three or four times a day.

**Verse 37–38½** teach the diagnosis of correct and incorrect smoking: success clarifies the mind, throat and senses and lightens the head; excess or untimely smoking brings deafness, blindness, dumbness, hemothermia and giddiness. **Verses 39–40** give the remedies for over-smoking — ghee potions, nasal medication, eye applications and demulcent drinks, varied according to the humour provoked.

**Verses 41–45½** list the **contraindications** with the precision of a modern label: one who has been purged or enema-treated, the hemothermic, the poisoned, the grief-stricken, the gravid, the fatigued or intoxicated, those with chyme or pitta disorders, the sleepless, the fainting, the giddy, the thirsty, the emaciated, those with pectoral lesions, those who have just taken wine, milk, unctuous drink, honey or curds, and those with dehydration, anger, dry palate, head injury, *shankhaka* or *rohini*. Smoking in these states, the text warns, dreadfully aggravates the complaints.

**Verses 46–51½** describe the technique and the pipe: smoke through the nose for diseases of the head, nose and eye, through the mouth for throat disease; inhale by the nose and exhale by the mouth, never in reverse, lest the eyes be injured; keep the body erect, sit at ease, close one nostril and smoke thrice through the other. The pipe is straight, with three bulges and a jujube-sized mouth, its length varying with the kind of smoke — twenty-four fingers' breadth for the errhine, thirty-two for the unctuous, thirty-six for the habitual.

## Nasal Toilet: The Anu Oil

> Verses 56–70½. The annual course of *nasya*.

**Verse 56** prescribes a course of *Anu* oil every year, in the seasons of the first rains, autumn and spring, on cloud-free days. **Verses 57–62½** state the virtues: sight, smell and hearing remain unimpaired; hair does not grey or fall; rigidity of neck, headache, facial paralysis, trismus, rhinitis, hemicrania and head tremor are alleviated; the vessels, joints, sinews and tendons of the cranium gain strength; the face becomes cheerful and plump, the voice mellow and firm; and the effects of senility are retarded.

**Verses 63–67½** give the full method of preparing the oil — some twenty-nine herbs decocted in rain-water one hundred times the quantity of the oil, boiled down tenfold, combined with an equal measure of oil and boiled until only the oil remains, the process repeated ten times, and finished at the tenth boiling with an equal quantity of goat's milk. **Verses 68–70½** give the use: two *tolas*, after oleation and sudation of the head, dropped into the nostrils with a cotton swab, thrice every alternate day for seven days, in a warm draught-free room, with wholesome food and self-control. The oil, the text concludes, is curative of tridiscordance and invigorative of the senses.

## Oral Hygiene: Teeth, Tongue and Gargle

> Verses 71–80. The classical rules for the mouth.

**Verses 71–72½** prescribe the **tooth-twig**: green, crushed at one end, of astringent, pungent or bitter taste, used twice a day without injuring the gums. Tooth-cleansing dispels oral fetor and dysgeusia, removes impurities of tongue, teeth and mouth, and promptly induces appetite. **Verse 73** recommends the twigs of Indian beech, Indian oleander, mudar, Arabian jasmine, arjun and the spinous kino tree.

**Verses 74–75½** cover the **tongue scraper** — without a sharp edge, curved, made of gold, silver, copper, tin or brass — because the coating at the root of the tongue obstructs the respiratory passage and causes fetor oris. **Verse 76** recommends holding nutmeg, musk mallow, betel nut, cloves, cubeb pepper, fresh betel leaves, camphor and small cardamom in the mouth for cleanliness and fragrance of breath.

**Verses 77½–80** praise the **oil gargle**: it gives strength to the jaws and voice, plumpness to the face, sensitiveness of the palate and a keen appetite; the throat does not dry, the lips do not chap, and the teeth become firmly rooted, insensitive to acidity and able to chew the hardest foods.

## Anointing: Head, Ears and Body

> Verses 80½–89. Oleation of the head, ears and body.

**Verses 80½–83** on the head: daily oleation prevents headache, baldness and greying, keeps the hair firmly rooted, flowing and black, strengthens the cranial bones, tones the sense organs, beautifies the face and brings good sleep. **Verses 83½–84** on the ears: daily oil in the ears prevents vata-type ear disease, stiffness of the neck and jaws, hardness of hearing and deafness.

**Verses 84½–89** on the body: as a pitcher is strengthened by oil, a hide by soaking and an axle by lubrication, so daily inunction makes the body firm and the skin beautiful, quiets vata disorders and builds tolerance to hardship. The reasoning (**verse 87**) is precise: vata predominates in the sense of touch, and the sense of touch resides in the skin — therefore inunction is the greatest dermic tonic and ought to be practised daily. The limbs so accustomed are not seriously affected by external trauma or violent exertion, and one becomes smooth, plump, strong and good-looking, escaping the effects of age.

## Pedal Inunction and Massage

> Verses 90–93. The feet and the whole body.

**Verses 90–92** on the feet: pedal inunction soon removes roughness, stiffness, dryness, fatigue and numbness of the feet, brings comeliness and strength to the legs and brightness to the eyes, quiets vata discordance and prevents sciatica, fissures and strain of the leg vessels and muscles. **Verse 93** on massage: massaging the body removes body-fetor, heaviness, torpor, itching, dirt, inappetence and offensive sweat.

## Bathing and Cleanliness

> Verses 94–99. Bath, clothing, perfumes and jewels.

**Verse 94**: the bath is purifying, promotive of virility and longevity, eliminative of fatigue, sweat and dirt, conducive to bodily strength and vitalizing in the highest degree. **Verse 95**: clean apparel is attractive, conducive to reputation and longevity, avertive of ill luck and worthy of society. **Verse 96**: perfumes and garlands promote virility and longevity, disseminate fragrance, and promote cheer. **Verse 97**: jewels and ornaments bring prosperity, are auspicious, decorative, dispersive of worries and vitalizing. **Verse 98**: frequent ablution of the feet and excretory orifices promotes intelligence, purity and longevity. **Verse 99**: trimming the hair, beard and nails promotes plumpness, virility and longevity and enhances good looks.

## The Umbrella, the Staff and the Vigilant Life

> Verses 100–104. Protection and the care of the self.

**Verse 100** on footwear: wholesome for the eyes, pleasant to the tread, mitigative of foot discomfort, promotive of strength and easy gait. **Verse 101** on the umbrella: avertive of calamity, a shield against sun, wind, dust and rain. **Verse 102** on the staff: a prop against stumbling, a support that promotes longevity and dispels fear. **Verse 103** closes with the chapter's motto — as a city lord governs his city and a charioteer his chariot, so a wise man is ever vigilant in the care of his own body — and **verse 104** adds the ethical frame: earn one's livelihood by means not contrary to religion, and be devoted to peace and study.

## Key Points for Study

- **The measure is physiological, not arithmetic**: food quantity is judged by the strength of the digestive fire and by the body's response — digestion in due time without harm — not by fixed portions. This is the seed of individualised dietetics.
- **Heavy vs light foods**: lightness and heaviness are properties rooted in the elements (air/fire vs earth/water), and the text's practical rule — heavy in one-third to one-half measure — is a genuinely useful clinical heuristic.
- **The daily regimen is a system**: collyrium at night, smoking at eight set times, *Anu* oil in three seasons, tooth-twig twice daily, gargle, inunction — each item is dosed, timed and contraindicated with pharmaceutical care. Modern *dinacharya* practice is this chapter, abridged.
- **The head and senses are the focus**: roughly two-thirds of the regimen concerns the region above the clavicles — eyes, nose, throat, ears, teeth, hair — reflecting the classical view that the senses are the seat of both perception and longevity.
- **Smoking is medicine, not habit**: the text's smoking is a dosed, timed, contraindication-heavy procedure with an explicitly non-habitual frame — a caution worth noting before any modern reading.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does "matrashita" mean?

*Matrashita* (or *matrashitiya*) means "measure in eating" — the doctrine that the correct quantity of food is determined by the strength of one's digestive fire. The proper measure is defined as that quantity which is digested in due time without impairing health.

### Which foods does this chapter recommend for daily use?

The daily staple is simple: shashtika and shali rice, green gram, rock-salt, amalaki (emblic myrobalan), barley, rain water, milk, ghee, the flesh of arid-zone (*jangala*) animals and honey. Dried meats, pork, beef, buffalo, fish, curds, black gram and heavy pastries are to be avoided habitually.

### Is oil gargling (oil pulling) taught in the Charaka Samhita?

Yes. Verses 77½–80 of this chapter praise the oil gargle — strength to the jaws and voice, plumpness of face, keen appetite, firm teeth and moist throat. Modern oil pulling derives directly from this classical *kavala* / *gandusha* practice. See the [glossary entry](/glossary) for the distinction between the two.

### What is Anu oil and how is it used?

*Anu taila* is a classical nasal oil prepared from some twenty-nine herbs, boiled in rain-water and finished with goat's milk. It is used as a nasal toilet (*nasya*) — two tolas dropped into each nostril with a cotton swab after head oleation and sudation, thrice every alternate day for seven days, in the rainy, autumn and spring seasons.

### Is this chapter still relevant today?

Extremely. The chapter's food-measure doctrine is the ancestor of personalised nutrition; its tongue scraper, tooth-twig and oil gargle are everyday wellness practices; and its head and body oleation, massage and bathing rules remain standard in Ayurvedic practice. What modern readers should carry away is the method: dosed, timed and individually adjusted regimen, built on physiological reasoning.

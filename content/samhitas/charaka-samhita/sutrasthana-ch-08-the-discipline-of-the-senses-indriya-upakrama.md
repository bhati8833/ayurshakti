# Chapter 8 - The Discipline of the Senses (indriya-upakrama)

## TL;DR — Executive Clinical Summary

**Sutrasthana Chapter 8** explains how the senses, the mind and the understanding work together — and how their misuse produces disease. It sets out the **five pentads** of sensation (sense-faculties, sense-materials, sense-organs, sense-objects and sense-perceptions), the nature of the mind as the super-sensual faculty that directs the senses, and the three kinds of faulty contact — **over-contact, non-contact and mis-contact** — that impair understanding. Its practical core is the classical **code of right conduct** (*sadvritta*): the positive observances (hygiene, dress, oiling, bathing, speech, charity, company) and the long list of interdictions (from food rules to sexual, study and social conduct) that keep the senses and mind healthy. The reward promised is striking: the full measure of a hundred years of diseaseless life.

## Introduction

This chapter closes the second tetrad of chapters concerning healthful living (Chapters 5–8: measure in eating, seasonal regimen, natural urges and now the discipline of the senses). Where Chapter 7 taught the management of the urges, Chapter 8 teaches the management of **perception itself** — for the classical physician, disease enters through the five senses, and health is maintained by disciplining what the senses contact and how the mind reacts. The chapter is therefore at once epistemology (how perception works), preventive medicine (what contact damages the senses) and ethics (the full code of right conduct), all in one.

## The Five Pentads of Sensation

> Verses 3–14. The anatomy of perception.

**Verse 3** lays down the five pentads: five sense-faculties, five sense-materials, five sense-organs, five sense-objects and five sense-perceptions. **Verses 8–12** name each pentad in turn:

- **The five sense-faculties** (*pañcendriya*): sight, hearing, smell, taste and touch.
- **The five sense-materials** (*pañcendriya-dravya*): ether, air, light, water and earth.
- **The five sense-organs** (*pañcendriya-adhishthana*): the eyes, the ears, the nose, the tongue and the skin.
- **The five sense-objects** (*pañcendriya-artha*): sound, touch, shape, taste and smell.
- **The five sense-perceptions** (*pañcendriya-buddha*): the visual and other perceptions — fleeting, decisive results of the coordination of the senses, their objects, the mind and the soul.

**Verse 14** adds the principle of predominance: though each sense is an aggregate of all five proto-elements, **light predominates in the eye, ether in the ear, earth in the smell, water in the taste and air in the touch**; **verse 14-(1)** explains that each sense contacts objects of a similar predominant element, owing to innate affinity and ubiquity.

## The Mind — Super-Sensual Director of the Senses

> Verses 4–7. What the mind is and how perception happens.

**Verse 4** defines the mind: it is super-sensual, designated *Sattva* or Psyche, called by some *Cetah* (consciousness); its function depends on the presence of the mental object and the spirit, and it is the cause of the activity of the sense-organs. **Verse 5** explains the appearance of multiplicity: because of the multiplicity of mental objects, sense-objects and impulses, and of the combinations of the qualities *Rajas*, *Tamas* and *Sattva*, the one mind appears multifaceted — but there is no multiplicity of minds, for a single mind cannot contact many sense-objects simultaneously, and therefore all sense-organs do not function at one and the same moment. **Verse 6** defines temperament: whatever trait manifests most frequently in a man's mental make-up, of that mentality he is said to be, on account of his predominant association with it. **Verse 7** states the condition of perception: the sense-organs, when led by the mind, are capable of contacting the sense-objects.

**Verse 13** completes the mental anatomy: the mind, the mind-objects, the understanding and the spirit constitute the aggregate of spiritual elements and qualities — the source of good or bad activity or inactivity; and action, which is also called therapeusis, depends on the substance.

## Right and Wrong Contact — the Physiology of Impairment

> Verses 15–17. The three faults and the remedy.

**Verse 15** is the preventive core of the chapter: by **over-contact, non-contact and mis-contact** of the senses with their objects, the senses, together with the mind, become vitiated and lead to the **impairment of understanding** in their own respective spheres; right contact, on the other hand, regaining the normal state, conduces to the **enhancement of understanding**. **Verse 16** extends the doctrine to the mind: the object of the mind is that which is thinkable; right, excessive, deficient and erroneous perceptions are respectively the causes of the order and the disorder of the mind and understanding.

**Verses 17-(1–3)** give the protective regimen: wholesome contact of the sense-organs and their objects; the proper performance of actions after intelligent and repeated scrutiny; resort to the habitual use of agents that counteract the prevailing traits of clime, season and one's own constitution — and the remembrance and practice, by all who desire their welfare, of the rules of right conduct.

## The Two Objects of the Good Life

> Verses 18-(1–2). Health and the conquest of the senses.

**Verse 18-(1)** declares that by the observance of these rules one achieves at once both the objects — **health and the conquest of the senses** (*indriyavijaya*). **Verse 18-(2)** then announces the full code: "O Agnivesha! we shall now describe the entire code of right conduct." What follows is the classical *sadvritta*, the most complete ethical and hygienic code in the ancient medical literature.

## The Positive Code — What the Good Life Includes

> Verses 18-(3–7). The observances of sadvritta.

**Reverence and daily rites**: reverence for the gods, cows, brahmanas, preceptor, elders, adepts and teachers; tending of the ceremonial fire; wearing of holy herbs; bathing twice daily; repeated cleansing of the excretory orifices and the feet; trimming the hair, beard and nails thrice a fortnight.

**Dress and person**: clean and untorn apparel; a cheerful disposition; the use of perfumes; well-dressed and well-toiled hair; the daily use of oil for the head, ears, nostrils and feet; smoking; an umbrella, staff and turban; the wearing of shoes; looking ahead while walking with heedfulness.

**Speech and conduct**: taking the initiative in conversation; a pleasant face; helping the distressed; burnt-offerings, sacrifice and charitableness; saluting in public places; peace-offerings; hospitality to guests; food-balls to the manes; wholesome, measured, palatable and timely speech; self-control and piety; freedom from anxiety; fearlessness, modesty, sagacity, high spirits, dexterity, forgiveness, goodness and faith; the service of those superior in modesty, intellect, learning, birth and age, and of adepts and preceptors.

**Habits and company**: relaxation from work before feeling the strain; fellowship with all creatures; winning over the angry; consoling the frightened; befriending the destitute; truthfulness; a peaceful disposition; bearing harsh words from others and overcoming impatience; showing a tranquil disposition; and removing the causes of passion and aversion.

## The Interdictions — What the Good Life Avoids

> Verses 19–27. The negative code, in full.

**Speech and honesty (verse 19-(1))**: no untruth; no taking of another's goods; no coveting of another's wife or wealth; no delight in vengeance; no sin, even against a sinner; no exposure of another's shortcomings; no prying into secrets; no company of the irreligious, the disloyal, the arrogant, the depraved, the feticidal, the mean and the wicked.

**Movement and rest (verse 19-(2))**: no bad mounts; no hard, knee-high seats; no unspread, uncovered, narrow and uneven beds; no roaming on jagged mountain peaks; no tree-climbing; no bathing in rapidly flowing waters; no resting under the shade of a bank; no lingering near conflagrations.

**Bodily etiquette (verses 19-(3–4))**: no loud laughter; no audible release of wind; no yawning, sneezing or laughing with an uncovered mouth; no nose-picking, teeth-gnashing, nail-drumming or bone-striking; no scratching of the ground, idling with grass-blades or kneading clods; no unseemly gestures; no gazing at luminaries or at undesirable, unclean and inauspicious sights; no despising of a dead body; no crossing the shadow of a tutelary tree, flag or teacher; no night visits to temples, totem trees, public halls, parks, crematoria or scaffolds; no entry alone into an uninhabited house or forest.

**Relations (verse 19-(5))**: no wife, friend or servant of sinful conduct; no contending with superiors; no consorting with inferiors; no currying favour with the crooked; no shelter of an untrustworthy man; no spreading of panic; no rash acts; no excess of sleep, waking, bathing, drinking or eating; no long sitting on the haunches with knees up.

**Environment (verse 19-(6))**: no approach to cruel, fanged or horned animals; avoidance of headwinds, severe sun, frost and storms; no provoking of quarrels.

**Bathing and fire (verse 19-(7))**: no inattentive tending of the sacrificial fire; no warming oneself over a fire underneath; no bathing while fatigued or without first washing the face, or naked; no drying the head with a cloth worn while bathing; no thrashing of tresses; no putting on the same clothes after a bath.

**Thresholds and journey (verse 19)**: step not out of doors without touching precious stones, sacrificial ghee, worshipful and auspicious objects and flowers; walk not with holy objects to the left and things of a dissimilar nature to the right.

**The great meal rules (verses 20-(1), 20)**: eat not without wearing a jewel on the hand; without a bath; in tattered clothes; without prayer, Homa-rites, offerings to the household gods and the manes; without first feeding elders, guests and dependants; unscented or ungarlanded; without cleansing the hands, feet and face; with an unclean mouth; facing the north; listlessly; waited on by an undevoted, unmannerly, unclean or hungry attendant; in improper vessels, place or time; amidst a crowd; without offering to Agni, besprinkling the food with sanctified water or saying scriptural chants over it; while reviling anyone; nor eat food of a vile description or served by ill-wishers; nor stale articles, with the exception of flesh, greens, dry vegetables and fruits. Leave no remnants except of curds, honey, salt, roasted-grain-flour and ghee; eat not curds at night; eat not roasted-grain-flour alone, at night, after a meal, in large quantities, at both meals or interspersed with draughts of water; tear not food with the teeth.

**Posture and urges (verse 21-(1))**: sneeze, eat and sleep not in a crooked posture; attend not to other work while pressed by natural urges; void not spittle, flatus, stools or urine facing the wind, fire, water, moon, sun, a brahmana or an elder; make not water on the road; blow not the nose amidst a crowd, while eating, or during auspicious acts like Japa, Homa, study and Bali-offerings.

**The sex rules (verses 22-(1–2))**: contemn not nor confide overmuch in a woman; divulge not secrets to her; place her not in power; indulge not in the sex-act with a woman who is in her courses, diseased, unclean, unfit, of undesirable appearance, conduct or nature, unskilful, unresponsive or desirous of another; mate not with another's wife, with a female of another species, or in extra-genital organs; not under a tutelary tree, in a public hall, at cross-roads, in a public park or crematorium, in the house of a brahmana, teacher or god; not during the twilights or forbidden days; not in an unclean condition; not without an aphrodisiac, without having made up the mind beforehand, without the necessary erotic urge, without nourishment, or in an over-eaten condition; not in an awkward position; not hard pressed by the urges of urine and faeces; not afflicted by fatigue, physical exercise, fasting and exhaustion; not in a place without privacy.

**Study (verses 23–24)**: speak not ill of good people and elders; practise not necromancy, worship or studies in an unclean condition; study not during unseasonal lightning, a lurid glow, conflagration, earthquake, festival, meteoric showers, eclipses, the new moon day or the two twilights; study not without the teacher's instruction, slurring or overstressing letters, raucously or in falsetto, without punctuation, too hurriedly or too leisurely, spiritlessly, very loudly or very low.

**Timing and friendship (verse 25-(1))**: transgress not the majority decision; break not a rule; move not at night in an improper place; resort not to dinner, study, the sex-act or sleep at the two twilights; contract not friendship with the very young, the very old, the greedy, the foolish, the diseased and the impotent; develop not a taste for drinking, gambling and the company of prostitutes.

**Speech and mind (verses 25, 26)**: divulge not secrets; contemn not anyone; be not self-conceited, inept, obstructive or carping; speak not ill of the brahmanas; raise not the staff against a cow; defy not the elders, teachers, guilds and kings; talk not over-much; break not with relations, companions and helpers in adversity; be neither timid nor overbold; be not ungenerous to dependants; distrust not kinsmen; take not pleasures alone; regard not character and social observances as a tax; trust not everyone nor distrust everyone; be not always ruminative.

**Mind and emotion (verses 27-(1), 27)**: let not slip the right moment of action; undertake nothing without deliberation; be not a slave to sense-appetites; pander not to the fickle mind; over-burden not the senses and understanding; over-procrastinate not; give not way to anger and joy; nurse not sorrows; be not arrogant in success nor dejected in defeat; remind yourself constantly of the vanity of things; be decided as to causes and effects and devoted to benevolent enterprises; grow not complacent with achievements; lose not heart; recall not calumny.

## The Auspicious Life and Brahmacharya

> Verses 28–29. Purification and the highest devotion.

**Verse 28** asks that libations of holy ghee, barley, *til*, sacrificial grass and rape seed be poured into the sacrificial fire only in a clean condition. **Verse 28-(1)** prescribes the auspicious bath, chanted over with the scriptural texts — "May the Fire not leave me," "May the wind grant me life," "May Vishnu grant me strength," "May Indra grant me virility," "May the waters enter me auspiciously," "The Waters are indeed the source of happiness" — with laving of the lips twice, besprinkling of the feet, and touching of water to the cavities of the head, the heart and the crown.

**Verse 29** closes the code with its highest value: be devoted to **brahmacharya** (celibate or disciplined life), knowledge, charity, friendliness, kindness, joy, impartiality and peace.

## The Reward — A Hundred Years of Diseaseless Life

> Verses 30–34. The promise and the closing.

**Verse 30** summarises the chapter's contents: the five pentads, the mind, the tetrad of causes, and the regimen of good conduct in detail. **Verse 31** makes the famous promise: he who rightly observes the rules of health laid down here will not be deprived of the full measure of the **hundred years of diseaseless life**. **Verses 32–33** add the social and spiritual fruits: such a man, approved of good men, fills the world with his fame, attains wealth and spiritual merit, becomes the friend of all creatures, and attains the supreme world of the doers of good. **Verse 34** adds the sage's humility: whatever other observances there may be, not spoken of here, provided they are good, are always to be respected — in the opinion of Atreya. The colophon marks the completion of the chapter and of the second tetrad of chapters concerning healthful living.

## Key Points for Study

- **The five pentads**: faculties, materials, organs, objects and perceptions — the complete classical model of sensation, each sense dominated by one proto-element (light in the eye, ether in the ear, earth in the smell, water in the taste, air in the touch).
- **The mind directs the senses**: perception happens only when the mind leads the organs to their objects; the single mind is multifaceted, and all organs do not function at once.
- **Three faults, one rule**: over-contact, non-contact and mis-contact of the senses impair understanding; right contact enhances it. Health is managed at the level of sensory contact.
- **Sadvritta is preventive medicine**: the code of right conduct — hygiene, oiling, bathing, dress, speech, food, sleep, study, sex and social rules — is presented as the practical means of keeping the senses and mind normal.
- **The promise**: observance of these rules yields health and the conquest of the senses (*indriyavijaya*) — and the full measure of a hundred years of diseaseless life.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is indriya-upakrama in Ayurveda?

Indriya-upakrama, the discipline of the senses, is Sutrasthana Chapter 8 of the Charaka Samhita. It explains the five pentads of sensation (faculties, materials, organs, objects and perceptions), the role of the mind in perception, the three kinds of faulty sensory contact that impair understanding, and the full code of right conduct (sadvritta) that preserves the health of the senses and mind.

### What are the five pentads (pancha-panchaka)?

The five pentads are the five sense-faculties (sight, hearing, smell, taste, touch), the five sense-materials (ether, air, light, water, earth), the five sense-organs (eyes, ears, nose, tongue, skin), the five sense-objects (sound, touch, shape, taste, smell) and the five sense-perceptions (visual and the other perceptions).

### What is sadvritta?

Sadvritta is the classical code of right conduct — the daily and social observances and interdictions taught in this chapter: reverence and daily rites, cleanliness, oiling of head and feet, proper dress, wholesome and timely speech, charity, good company, correct food habits, and restraint of the mind and emotions. It is the practical discipline that keeps the senses, mind and understanding normal and is regarded as the foundation of preventive medicine.

### How do over-contact, non-contact and mis-contact affect health?

The chapter teaches that faulty contact of the senses with their objects vitiates the senses and the mind and impairs understanding — over-contact (excess of any sensory indulgence), non-contact (deprivation) and mis-contact (wrong or perverse use). Right contact restores normalcy and enhances understanding; hence the discipline of the senses is a direct means of preventing disease.

### What is meant by the hundred years of diseaseless life?

Verse 31 promises that he who rightly observes the rules of health laid down in this chapter will not be deprived of the full measure of the hundred years of diseaseless life — the classical Ayurvedic ideal of a complete life-span lived without disease, attained through the discipline of the senses and the code of right conduct.

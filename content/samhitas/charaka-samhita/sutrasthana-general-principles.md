# Sutrasthana (Sutra Sthana) — General Principles of Ayurveda

## TL;DR — Executive Summary

**Sutrasthana** is the first of the eight sections (sthanas) of the **Charaka Samhita** and the philosophical and practical foundation of the entire text. Its 30 chapters define what Ayurveda is, establish the framework of the three **doshas**, dietetics, daily and seasonal regimen, therapeutic procedures (oleation, sudation, emesis, purgation), disease classification, and the professional conduct expected of a physician. Every clinical concept developed in the later sections — pathology (Nidanasthana), therapeutics (Chikitsasthana) and the others — is first announced here in seed form. For the student, this section is the starting point; for the practitioner, it is the reference against which all later teachings are measured.

---

## What Is Sutrasthana?

The Sanskrit word *sutra* means "thread" — and this section threads together the fundamental aphorisms of the science of life (*Ayurveda*). It is often called the section of **general principles** because it treats the body of Ayurvedic knowledge in its most condensed, rule-like form.

The classical Charaka Samhita opens with the celebrated definition of Ayurveda as the discipline that teaches what is wholesome (*hita*) and unwholesome (*ahita*) for life, the causes of longevity, and the proper measure of life itself. Sutrasthana then builds outward from this definition into a complete system: the physician, the patient, the medicines, and the attendant — the four limbs of therapy — and the conditions under which each succeeds or fails.

## The 30 Chapters at a Glance

### Diet, Regimen and the Code of Daily Living

- [Measure in Eating (matrashita)](sutrasthana-ch-05-measure-in-eating-matrashita) — Chapter 5. The correct quantity of food: what "fullness" means, why over-eating is the root of many diseases, and the method of assessing one's digestive capacity.
- [The Seasonal Dietary and Regimen of Man (tasyashita)](sutrasthana-ch-06-the-seasonal-dietary-and-regimen-of-man-tasyashita) — Chapter 6. The six seasons and the diet, activity and habits suited to each, so that the body stays in balance through the year.
- [Natural Urges (vega) Should Not Be Suppressed](sutrasthana-ch-07-natural-urges-vega-should-not-be-suppressed) — Chapter 7. The thirteen natural urges, the diseases caused by suppressing them, and the equal danger of forcing them.
- [Diet and Dietetics (annapana-vidhi)](sutrasthana-ch-27-diet-and-dietetics-annapana-vidhi) — Chapter 27. The master chapter on food: cereals, pulses, meats, vegetables, fruits, wines, water, milk, sugarcane and cooked foods, with their properties and the rules of combining them.
- [The Various Kinds of Food and Drink (ashita-pita)](sutrasthana-ch-28-the-various-kinds-of-food-and-drink-ashita-pita) — Chapter 28. How food and drink are digested, the eight factors governing diet, and the general rules of eating.

### Therapeutics and the Physician's Tools

- [The Purging Cassia (aragvadha)](sutrasthana-ch-03-the-purging-cassia-aragvadha) — Chapter 3. The medicinal uses of *Cassia fistula* and the logic of selecting a remedy for a given constitution.
- [Oleation Therapy (sneha)](sutrasthana-ch-13-oleation-therapy-sneha) — Chapter 13. Internal and external oil therapy: the kinds of fats, their actions on the doshas, and the correct administration.
- [The Sudation Therapy (sveda)](sutrasthana-ch-14-the-sudation-therapy-sveda) — Chapter 14. Fomentation and sweating procedures, their thirteen varieties and their contraindications.
- [Lightening (langhana) and Roborant (brimhana) Therapies](sutrasthana-ch-22-lightening-langhana-and-roborant-brimhana-therapies) — Chapter 22. The two opposing classes of treatment — reducing and strengthening — and when each is appropriate.
- [Impletion Therapy (santarpana)](sutrasthana-ch-23-impletion-therapy-santarpana) — Chapter 23. Nourishing therapies for emaciation and debility, with their indications.
- [The Armamentarium (upakalpa) of the Physician](sutrasthana-ch-15-the-armamentarium-upakalpa-of-the-physician) — Chapter 15. The complete equipment, medicines and attitude a physician must carry into practice.

### Physiology, Pathology and the Nature of Disease

- [The Salutary and Unsalutary Influences of Vata](sutrasthana-ch-12-the-salutary-and-the-unsalutary-influences-of-vata) — Chapter 12. The five functions of *vata* and the actions that aggravate or pacify it.
- [The Diseases of the Head (shiroroga) and of the Heart (hridroga)](sutrasthana-ch-17-the-diseases-of-the-head-shiroroga-and-of-the-heart-hridroga) — Chapter 17. Classification of headaches and cardiac affections.
- [The Three Kinds of Edema (shotha)](sutrasthana-ch-18-the-three-kinds-of-edema-shotha) — Chapter 18. Swellings and their causes, classification and treatment.
- [The Eight Abdominal Affections (udara-roga)](sutrasthana-ch-19-the-eight-abdominal-affections-udara-roga) — Chapter 19. Ascites and the other abdominal disorders.
- [The Major List of Diseases (maharoga)](sutrasthana-ch-20-the-major-list-of-diseases-maharoga) — Chapter 20. The great enumerations of disease from which the later pathology section builds.
- [The Origin of Man and Disease (purusha-samjnaka)](sutrasthana-ch-25-the-origin-of-man-and-disease-purusha-samjnaka) — Chapter 25. The six elements of the person and how disease arises in the embodied self.
- [Discussion Among Sages Regarding Tastes (rasa)](sutrasthana-ch-26-discussion-among-sages-regarding-tastes-rasa) — Chapter 26. The six tastes, their elemental composition and their therapeutic actions.
- [The Ten Resorts of Life (dasha-prana-ayatana)](sutrasthana-ch-29-the-ten-resorts-of-life-dasha-prana-ayatana) — Chapter 29. The ten seats of life in the body.

### The Physician and Professional Conduct

- [The Discipline of the Senses (indriya-upakrama)](sutrasthana-ch-08-the-discipline-of-the-senses-indriya-upakrama) — Chapter 8. The physician's mastery over the senses as the first qualification for practice.
- [The Fourfold Basic Factors in Therapeusis (cikitsa)](sutrasthana-ch-10-the-major-fourfold-basic-factors-in-therapeusis-cikitsa) — Chapter 10. The physician, the medicine, the attendant and the patient — and the qualities each must possess for treatment to succeed.
- [The Three Pursuits (eshana) of Man](sutrasthana-ch-11-the-three-pursuits-eshana-of-man) — Chapter 11. Life, wealth and the hereafter — the three pursuits that shape human action, and the physician's duty within them.
- [The Eight Censured Persons (nindita-purusha)](sutrasthana-ch-21-the-eight-censured-persons-nindita-purusha) — Chapter 21. The eight body types considered unfit or difficult to treat.

## Key Concepts Introduced in Sutrasthana

Several foundational ideas appear for the first time in this section and reappear throughout the Samhita:

- **The three doshas** — *vata*, *pitta* and *kapha* — as the operational forces of physiology, with their seats, qualities and seasonal behaviour.
- **Agni (digestive fire)** — the principle that governs digestion and metabolism; its strength determines health and disease.
- **The six tastes (rasa)** — sweet, sour, salty, pungent, bitter and astringent — and their elemental basis, introduced in detail in [Chapter 26](sutrasthana-ch-26-discussion-among-sages-regarding-tastes-rasa).
- **The three supports of life (trayopastambha)** — food, sleep and regulated celibacy — the tripod on which the body stands.
- **The four limbs of therapy** — physician, medicine, attendant and patient — the practical frame of all clinical work ([Chapter 10](sutrasthana-ch-10-the-major-fourfold-basic-factors-in-therapeusis-cikitsa)).
- **Daily and seasonal regimen** — the preventive core of Ayurveda, set out in [Chapter 6](sutrasthana-ch-06-the-seasonal-dietary-and-regimen-of-man-tasyashita) and expanded across the section.

For quick reference, key terms such as [dosha](/glossary), [agni](/glossary), [rasa](/glossary) and [srotas](/glossary) are explained in the AyurShakti [glossary](/glossary) of Sanskrit medical terms.

## Why Sutrasthana Matters Today

Modern Ayurvedic practice still leans on this section more than any other. The chapter on dietetics ([Chapter 27](sutrasthana-ch-27-diet-and-dietetics-annapana-vidhi)) remains the classical reference for food-based medicine; the seasonal regimen ([Chapter 6](sutrasthana-ch-06-the-seasonal-dietary-and-regimen-of-man-tasyashita)) is the ancestor of today's *ritucharya* protocols; and the account of natural urges ([Chapter 7](sutrasthana-ch-07-natural-urges-vega-should-not-be-suppressed)) is quoted whenever the modern clinician discusses bowel habits and bladder habits with patients. Reading the section as a whole reveals the internal consistency of the system: the same principles of balance, measure and cause-and-effect govern diet, drug and behaviour alike.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does the word "Sutrasthana" mean?

*Sutra* means thread or aphorism, and *sthana* means section or place. Sutrasthana is therefore the "section of aphorisms" — the opening part of the Charaka Samhita in which the fundamental rules of the science of life are laid out in concise, thread-like statements.

### How many chapters does Sutrasthana contain?

The classical Sutrasthana contains 30 chapters. On this site, chapter 27 (Diet and Dietetics) is split into its constituent sub-chapters — cereals, pulses, meats, vegetables, fruits, greens, wines, waters, milk, sugarcane, cooked foods and food additives — so that each topic can be studied and searched separately.

### What is the most important teaching of Sutrasthana?

Its signature teaching is that health is maintained by measure and balance: correct diet, correct regimen and the restraint of natural urges, supported by the physician's skill and the patient's cooperation. The famous opening definition of Ayurveda — the science that tells us what is wholesome and unwholesome for life — is the seed from which the entire section grows.

### Is Sutrasthana relevant to modern Ayurvedic practice?

Yes. The chapters on dietetics, seasonal regimen and therapeutics are still taught in Ayurvedic colleges and applied in clinics today. The section's method — classifying causes, observing symptoms and matching treatment to constitution — is the same evidence-first discipline that modern practice expects.

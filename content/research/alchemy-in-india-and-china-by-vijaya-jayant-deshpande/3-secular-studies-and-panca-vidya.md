3. Secular Studies and Panca-Vidya

The interests of the Buddhist travelers were not limited to religious studies. Many of the Indian travelers were originally Hindu Brahmins who were later converted to Buddhism. They had learned the secular sciences as a part of their traditional basic instruction in their studentship. This is also true in the case of Nagarjuna, the famous Buddhist patriarch of the second century AD. A study of the biographies of some of the travelers reveals a remarkable fact that they had studied "Panca Vidya", i.e., astronomy; lipi (writing), mathematics, medicine and tantra in their early life. A number of them had also studied at Nalanda where alchemy was a part of the curriculum. In AD 247 a great scholar from Central Asia named Geng Hui had collected a large number of canonical and non-canonical texts from India and had translated fourteen of these works into Chinese. ་ Dao Lin, who had taken the Indian name Silaprabha, visited Tamralipti in Bengal in the fourth century AD and studied Sanskrit, Vinaya (Buddhist Philosophy), yoga and tantra. Subsequently, Jing Shang in the early fifth century became a Buddhist monk and visited Khotan in Central Asia, studied various branches of Hindu science

100 including medicine under the Indian monk named Buddhasena. Further, Jnanabhadra, in the sixth century AD, had studied Buddhist Tripitaka and specially mastered "Vinaya". He translated a work called "Panca Vidya" or "Wu Ming Lun"4 in collaboration with other monks. The ' five sections of this book were Sabdasastra (science of words, i.e., grammar or philology, Bhaisajyasastra (medicine), Silpasastra (architecture), Mantrasastra (probably tantra including alchemy) and Mudrasastra (printing). Gunabhadra was an Indian monk who visited China during the Song dynasty (960-1279). He learned the art and sciences in his early life and was adept in "Panca Vidya".

1. Introduction (the Alchemical Exchange between India and China)

We have seen in earlier chapters that alchemy developed on parallel lines in India and China during the medieval period. In both countries, the growth of alchemy began with the search for the "herb of immortality" and led to the development of the metallic-mineral elixirs, especially the bold mercurial preparations of the early medieval period and to the elixirs prepared largely from the substances derived from the plant kingdom, a trend of the late medieval period. We observe that in both the systems gold-making and elixir-synthesis were the characteristic features of the alchemical practice. A notable fact is that during this period, there were very close contacts through the Buddhist monks and other travelers between these two culture areas. Direct references would include the translated texts from one language into another or the episodes described which are indication of exchange of ideas, in ancient texts and travelogues which themselves are of an authenticated date. The indirect references are sometimes obtained from etymological and linguistic studies. Transmission of ideas is sometimes accompanied by the borrowing of words from one language into another. We observe that a new idea when it travels from one place to another takes along with it related words from the original

94 language. The case of "Tea" is a similar one. "Tea" has its origin in China. The Chinese were the first to use tea-leaves for making the interesting drink with its peculiar flavour and medicinal uses. Tea came to India via Chinese central Asia where it was known as "cai". The North Indian name chai for tea probably takes the Chinese central Asian name. In Fu kian, from where tea went to Europe, it was locally known as "te" and the English name "tea"'s origin can be traced back to this. Likewise, while studying the transmission of scientific ideas we find that certain scientific terminology, names of substances or methods are transmitted to the other culture. Again, there are two categories of transmitted or diffused words. Some are translated and some others, like "tsai" to "chai", are transliterated into the other language. In the case of "transmission of alchemical and chemical ideas" between India and China we will discuss evidence by using both direct and indirect references that we have come across during the study. It will help to determine the nature, direction and period of certain transmissions. In the present case, the foremost requirement, that of having contacts between the two culture areas, of enough frequency and over a sufficient period, is

95 satisfied beyond doubt; through the travels of medieval Buddhists. Furthermore, the Buddhists had a keen interest in science, especially alchemy and medicine. The characteristic features of Indian and Chinese alchemy, with noticeable similarities in alchemical notions, ingredients and methods direct our attention to the possibility of exchange of alchemical ideas between them. Apart from the fact that China and India are geographically close, they also had close contacts through merchants, monks and other travelers since very early times. The visits of the Buddhists continued over a millennium from the third century AD to the fourteenth. A large number of Buddhist texts were translated into Chinese, which forms the Chinese version of Buddhist Tripitaka or Da Zang. There are references to alchemy in some of these texts. In old Chinese texts also we come across episodes which indicate the alchemical interests and knowledge of the Buddhists. The contribution of the Buddhists in the overall progress of alchemy in China can be studied in two contexts; one being the contribution by the Chinese Buddhists and the second is the contribution by Indian Buddhists visiting China in the medieval period. Studies by scholars such as Waley, Needham, Sivin, etc. provide us with several instances of transmission of both the above-mentioned categories. To begin with, we would

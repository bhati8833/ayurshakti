## 📜 1. Section Overview & Classical Context

Agriculture and Animal husbandry in the Puranas

This page relates ‘Meaning and Scope of the word Purana’ of the study on agriculture and animal husbandry in the Puranas—a category of Sanskrit literature describing the culture and heritage of ancient Indian society. Besides detailing animal care, the six chapters of this essay also documents various Puranic agricultural science: ploughing, sowing, soil-types/fertility tests, transplantation, manuring, irrigation/water-management, tanks, rainfall–Sun dependence, horticulture, plus crop lists (e.g., Vrihi, Yava, Godhuma) and festivals/rites.

Part 1 - The Meaning and Scope of the word Purāṇa

The Purāṇas are an unfailing source of information that helps us to reconstruct the history of Indian culture and civilization. By culture and civilization we mean the history of Indian polity, of Indian society, the history of religion and philosophy, legal history, the history of arts and crafts, architecture and iconography besides royal dynasties and the period of their rule.[1] The only species of Indian literature, which can claim, next to the Vedas, to reach back to great antiquity, is the Purāṇa.[2] Long before the beginning of the Christian era it came also to be used as the designation of a class of books dealing, among other matters, with old-world stories and legends.[3] The word purāṇa means old story (purāṇam ākhyānam).[4] The Śabdakalpadruma and the

Vācaspatyam explain Purāṇa as that which remains in the past and deals with the past.[5] Purāṇas are the old narratives or ancient legends having informative contents.

Yāska in his Nirukta defines Purāṇa as–

purā navaṃ bhavati[6]
i.e. it was new in the days of yore.

purā navaṃ bhavati[6]

i.e. it was new in the days of yore.

Sāyaṇācārya in the Ṛgveda[7] explains the word purāṇyā as—

purā kṛtayā.

purā kṛtayā.

So the term purāṇa indicates ‘ancient one’ according to this explanation.

The etymology of the term purāṇa is furnished by the Vāyu Purāṇa as follows—

yasmāt purā hyanatīdaṃ purāṇaṃ tena tat smṛtam[8]
i.e. that which lives from very remote times.

yasmāt purā hyanatīdaṃ purāṇaṃ tena tat smṛtam[8]

i.e. that which lives from very remote times.

The Purāṇas have their roots in the Vedic literature; many a legend, from the Ṛgvedic hymns and from the Brāhmaṇas, reappears in the Purāṇas.[9] They are thus composed with a view to get the knowledge revealed in the Vedas through stories. The Brāhmaṇa, who may know the four Vedas with the Aṅgas and Upaniṣads, should not really be regarded as having attained proficiency, if he should not thoroughly know the Purāṇa. He should reinforce the Veda with the Itihāsa and Purāṇa.[10]

The majority of people act according to the popular rituals which are recorded in the Purāṇas. The Purāṇic tradition is a combination of the Vedic ritualistic and popular mythical traditions.

That is why the Purāṇa is regarded as the fifth Veda in addition to the four Vedas i.e., the Ṛgveda, the Yajurveda, the Sāmaveda and the Atharvaveda—

purāṇaṃ pañcamo vedaḥ.[11]

purāṇaṃ pañcamo vedaḥ.[11]

The Chāndogya Upaniṣad too mentions the Itihāsa-Purāṇa as the fifth Veda—

itihāsapurāṇaṃ pañcamaṃ vedānām.[12]

itihāsapurāṇaṃ pañcamaṃ vedānām.[12]

The Skanda Purāṇa states that the Purāṇas are not inferior to the Vedas. The Purāṇas are as steady as the Vedas. All the Vedas are firmly rooted in the Purāṇas. It opines that whatever is not seen in the Vedas or Smṛtis, is seen in the Purāṇas.[13] The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa mentions Itihāsa and Purāṇa as subjects of study by a householder. It has been mentioned in the context of revelation on Pañcamahāyajña.[14] The Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa opines that it is called Purāṇa because it was in olden times.[15]

The Śabdakalpadruma[16] too contains the same meaning—

purā purvasmin kāle bhava iti
i.e., which was in ancient times.

purā purvasmin kāle bhava iti

i.e., which was in ancient times.

The Matsya Purāṇa gives the definition of the word purāṇa as—

purātanasya kalpasya purāṇāni vidurbudhāḥ.[17]
‘containing the records of past events’

purātanasya kalpasya purāṇāni vidurbudhāḥ.[17]

‘containing the records of past events’

The Padma Purāṇa says that it is called Purāṇa because it is interested in the past and therefore, it describes about the past.[18] Amarasiṃha in the Amarakoṣa describes Purāṇa as ancient tales.[19]

The Purāṇas have been utilized through centuries not only for educating the mass mind and infusing it with the nobler ideas of life but also for tactfully solving the religious, social and economic problems which were created in ancient India by the rise of various religious movements and the repeated invasions made by foreign races.[20] These works afford us, more than other works of the time a great insight into all phases and aspects of Hinduism.[21] For the modern Hinduism, the Purāṇas are religious treatises of divine origin. Hence, they have been a great source of inspiration to the mass-mind, especially, on religious matter.[22] That is why, the Purāṇas ought to have more careful study than has previously been dedicated to them.

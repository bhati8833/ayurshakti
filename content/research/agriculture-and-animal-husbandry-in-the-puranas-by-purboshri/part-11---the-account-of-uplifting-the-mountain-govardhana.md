Part 11 - The Account of Uplifting the Mountain Govardhana

The Bhāgavata Purāṇa mentions about the arrangements for the annual sacrifice to the rain-God Indra(Indra-yāga) for the protection of the agricultural products in its tenth Skandha.[1] In the Bhāgavata Purāṇa Nanda explained to curious Kṛṣṇa that god Indra is the presiding deity of the rainfall and clouds are his manifestations. They shower water which is the very source of pleasure and existence of all creatures.[2] People worship Indra, the ruler of clouds and regulator of the Earth, by performing sacrifices.[3] It is on the leftovers of the sacrifice that people survive for achieving their three purposes in life (viz. dharma, artha and kāma, which are known as trivarga). The rain-god is the genuine bestower of fruits to individuals who exercise in cultivation, trade and commerce etc.[4] He, who gives up the traditional religious rites, out of passion, greediness, panic or hatred, does not attain his real good.[5] Hearing his father’s words Kṛṣṇa replied with a view to arise extreme anger in Indra.[6] Kṛṣṇa said that it is not necessary to regain the favour of a divinity for the profit of mankind because a being comes into existence only by its own past deeds i.e. karma and passes away through the force of its own karmas.[7] A particular God is not to be worshipped. They who leave one God and run to worship another God do not gain anything.[8]

According to the Viṣṇu Purāṇa, there are four branches of knowledge-logical, scriptural, practical and political—

ānvīkṣikī trayī vārttā daṇḍanītistathāparā/ 
vidyācatuṣṭayaṃ tvetat…//[9]

Agriculture, commerce and tending of cattle; the knowledge of these three professions comprises the practical science (vārtā).Agriculture is the survival of farmers, buying and selling are the survival of traders and kine are people’s support.

Various types of gifts might be given to the Brāhmaṇas, grass to the cows and sacrifice to the mountain Govardhana.[12] Circumambulation to the cows, the Brahmins and the mountain Govardhana might be performed.[13] Following the advice of Kṛṣṇa, all the inhabitants performed sacrifice to the mountain Govardhana, worshipped the Brāhmaṇas and offered grass to the cows. Nanda and all the women-folk with other cowherds circumambulated the mountain Govardhana.[14] Then, Kṛṣṇa himself assumed another form of huge size and consumed the food which were offered to the mountain, announcing that he is the mountain Govardhana itself.[15] Seeing the other form of Kṛṣṇa, the inhabitants of Vraja exclaimed with surprise that the mountain takes any form he desires, he punishes those who show disrespect to him.

So, for the welfare of all, they must bow with reverence to him. Thus, the mountain, the cows and the Brāhmaṇas were worshipped according to the directions of Lord Kṛṣṇa.[16]

When Indra came to know that his worship had been stopped, he got very angry upon the cowherds and Nanda.[17] Considering himself as the Supreme Ruler of the Universe, Indra then ordered the host of clouds called Saṃvartaka to ruin the Universe saying that the cowherds who are forest dwellers, depending on a mortal like Kṛṣṇa, had disregarded a deity.[18] Riding on the elephant Airāvata and accompanied with the troop of Maruts, Indra followed the cloud Saṃvartaka, with a view to destroy the whole locality of Nanda and the Cowherds.[19] Thus, Indra commanded and freed the clouds from their bonds as a result of which the clouds forcefully tormented Gokula with heavy showers.[20] Suffering from the torrential downpour, the cowherds then resorted to their rescuer Kṛṣṇa who gave them courage and requested to defend them from angry Indra.[21] Observing the words of the cowherds Kṛṣṇa came to know that it was the work of offended Indra, he asked the cowherds to enter comfortably into the hollow under the mountain Govardhana, which he uprooted with one of his hand and held it up caringly as if it were a mushroom.[22] Kṛṣṇa thus upheld the mountain for seven days.[23] Observing the power of Kṛṣṇa, Indra was extremely surprised. Being shorn of pleasure and saddened of his plan, he controlled his Saṃvartaka clouds.[24] Thus, Kṛṣṇa caused the clouds to stop pouring down and saved the cowherds from the horrible anger of Indra whom he fully defeated.

Here, the belief in the divinity of the mountains led to the worship of the mountains. Indra became angry caused by the disrespect shown to him and the rain fell in consequence of it. As a result of worshipping the mountain Govardhana, the clouds forcefully tormented Gokula with heavy showers, according to the command of angry Indra. This clearly brings out the tradition of worshipping the mountains for timely rain. Timely rain is very much necessary for the process of agriculture. Therefore, this myth can be related with agriculture.

In the Viṣṇu Purāṇa it is clearly pointed out that when the clouds are seen distended with rain, the Earth becomes neither barren of corn, nor uncovered with abundance, and nor is man distressed by hunger—

nāśasyā nātṛṇā bhūmirna bubhukṣārddito janaḥ/ 
dṛśyate yatra dṛśyante vṛṣṭimanto balāhakāḥ//[25]

S.S. Dange opines in his The Bhāgavata Purāṇa: Mytho-Social Study that the heap of boiled rice offered to the mountain Govardhana makes it the corn-spirit, and a special festival of offerings on the mountain identified with Kṛṣṇa shows Kṛṣṇa to be a vegetation God.[26] The Viṣṇu Purāṇa too narrates the story of uplifting the mountain Govardhana.[27] According to the Viṣṇu Purāṇa, as Kṛṣṇa preserved the cattle of Gokula by raising up Govardhana, the chief of mountains, so Indra being grateful, installed Kṛṣṇa as Upendra. And as Lord Kṛṣṇa was Indra of the cows, so he was called as Govinda.[28]

Part 8.5 - Contents of the Viṣṇu Purāṇa

The Viṣṇu Purāṇa is a sectarian work belonging to the Pañcatantras, and purely Vaiṣṇava from beginning to end.[1] In this work Viṣṇu is praised and glorified as the supreme being, as the one and only god with whom also Brahmā and Śiva are identical.[2] The Viṣṇu Purāṇa is an early work composed most probably in the last quarter of the third or the first quarter of the fourth century A.D.[3] Pargiter in his Ancient Indian Historical Tradition, too opines that it is a late Purāṇa composed as a single whole upon a consistent plan, and not a collection of materials of various times, as we find in the Vāyu, Brahma and Matsya. From its account of Buddhism, and Jainism it appears to have been composed after Brāhmaṇism had recovered its supremacy, so that it cannot be earlier than about the 5th century A.D., and it is Brāhmaṇical.[4] According to the Agni Purāṇa, the Viṣṇu Purāṇa was narrated by sage Parāśara. It deals with the events which were occurred in the cycle of time known as the Varāha-kalpa. This Purāṇa contains twenty three thousand verses.[5] In the last chapter of the Viṣṇu Purāṇa it is declared that this Purāṇa is the best of all preservatives for one who is frightened of worldly reality and this is the remover of all deficiencies.[6]

The origin of the Viṣṇu Purāṇa is cited in the last chapter of the Viṣṇu Purāṇa itself. According to it, this Purāṇa was originally composed by the sage Nārāyaṇa. Then it was spoken by Brahmā to Ṛbhu. Ṛbhu imparted it to Priyavrata. Priyavrata then communicated it to Bhāguri.[7] Bhāguri gave it to Tambamitra, and Tambamitra to Dadhīci. Dadhīci recited it to Sārasvata, Sārasvata gave it to Bhṛgu.[8] Bhṛgu imparted it to Purukutsa, who then communicated it to Narmadā. Narmadā imparted it to the Nāga king Dhṛtarāṣṭra, and to Apūraṇa of the same race,[9] by whom it was then communicated to their monarch Vāsuki. Vāsuki imparted it to Vatsa, and Vatsa to Aśvatara.[10] Aśvatara repeated it to Kambala and Elāpatra After that the sage Vedaśirā going down to Pātāla, received the whole Purāṇa from the Nāgas.[11] Vedaśirā gave it to Pramati, and Pramati communicated it to intelligent Jātūkarṇa.[12] Jātūkarṇa taught it to some other holy persons, and through Vasiṣṭha’s consents it came to Parāśara and Parāśara finally imparted it to Maitreya.[13] It is divided into six books called aṃśas.

Again, each of the parts consists of a number of chapters.

(1) The first book is occupied chiefly with the details of creation, primary (sarga) and secondary (pratisarga); the first i.e., sarga explains how the universe proceeds from prakṛti or eternal crude matter; the second i.e., pratisarga explains in what manner the forms of things are developed from the elementary substances previously evolved, or how they reappear after their temporary destruction.[14] This book begins with a dialogue between Parāśara, the grandson of Vasiṣṭha and his pupil Maitreya. Maitreya inquires Parāśara, the origin and nature of the universe.

This book deals with details of creation, recreation[15], measure of time[16], origin of the four castes[17],origin of Rudra[18], legend of the churning of ocean[19], legend of Dhruva[20], story of Vena and Pṛthu[21], legend of Prahlāda[22], etc.

(2) The second book opens with a continuation of the kings of the first Manvantara; amongst whom, Bharata is said to have given a name to India, called, after him, Bhārata-varṣa.[23] It gives a fantastic description of the world at first[24]. The seven continents and the seven oceans are described, in the midst of which Jambudvīpa is found with the golden mountain of Meru-the abode of the gods. In Jambudvīpa lies Bhāratavarṣa i.e. India whose countries, mountains and rivers are enumerated. In connection with the name Bhāratavarṣa, then[25] a legend is told of the ancient king Bharata.

(3) The third book deals firstly with the account of the several Manus and Manvantaras, then, names of the twenty-eight Vyāsas who lived in different ages, division of the Vedas[26],duties of religious student, householder, hermit[27], funeral sacrifices,[28] etc.

(4) The fourth book contains all that the Hindus have of their ancient history. It is a tolerably comprehensive list of dynasties and individuals: it is a barren record of events.[29] It deals with dynasties of the kings, birth of Ikṣvāku,[30] dynasty of the Moon,[31] dynasties of the kings of the Kali age,[32] etc.

(5) The fifth book of the Viṣṇu Purāṇa is exclusively occupied with the life of Kṛṣṇa.[33] It is the longest book in this Purāṇa which gives us the account of marriage of Vāsudeva and Devakī, Yogamāyā who enters into the womb of Yaśodā and Hari who enters into the womb of Devakī,[34] holding of Govardhana Mountain by Kṛṣṇa,[35] Kṛṣṇa’s marriage with Rukmiṇī,[36] destruction of the Yadu race[37] etc.

The last book contains an account of the dissolution of the world, in both its major and minor cataclysms; and, in the particulars of the end of all things by fire and water, as well as in the principle of their perpetual renovation, presents a faithful exhibition of opinions that were general in the ancient world.[38] The sixth book is the shortest among all the books. It describes the nature of the Kali age,[39] nature of elemental dissolution (prakṛta pralaya),[40] description of Brahma yoga, and the greatness of the Viṣṇu Purāṇa.[41]

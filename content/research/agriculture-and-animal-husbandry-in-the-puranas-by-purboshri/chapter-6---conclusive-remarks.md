## 📜 1. Section Overview & Classical Context

Agriculture and Animal husbandry in the Puranas

This page relates ‘Conclusive Remarks’ of the study on agriculture and animal husbandry in the Puranas—a category of Sanskrit literature describing the culture and heritage of ancient Indian society. Besides detailing animal care, the six chapters of this essay also documents various Puranic agricultural science: ploughing, sowing, soil-types/fertility tests, transplantation, manuring, irrigation/water-management, tanks, rainfall–Sun dependence, horticulture, plus crop lists (e.g., Vrihi, Yava, Godhuma) and festivals/rites.

Chapter 6 - Conclusive Remarks

India is known to be a land of agriculture. Here most of the people are connected with agriculture and animal husbandry. Agriculture made real headway during the Ṛgvedic age, although the practice of ploughing can be traced back to Indo-Iranian times (Vide, Majumdar, R.C., The Vedic Age, p. 398). Agriculture is ranked as the topmost among all the professions, since it is the foundation for health, prosperity and culture of the people of a nation. Even in the modern times the real basis of national wealth and national economy in India is agriculture. The Śukla Yajurveda enjoins to practice agriculture so as to reap good crops (Vājasaneyī Saṃhitā , 4.10; 9.22). The Ṛgveda too assures that a farmer is bound to obtain immense wealth and profuse crops (Ṛgveda Saṃhitā , 7.93.2). Again, the seer in the Ṛgveda prays to the god Indra, for a son, who would be the owner of cultivated lands (Ibid., 6.20.1). Plenty of information is available in the Purāṇas regarding agriculture and animal husbandry, which reveals that ancient Indians had profound knowledge on all agricultural operations. Like agriculture special care was taken for tending animals also. In the Vedic and Purāṇic society, agriculture was a means to produce food grain so that human beings and other living creatures can sustain their life. Thus, since ancient times in India, agriculture and animal husbandry was considered to be a real source of wealth, prosperity, cattle and happiness.

The thesis Agriculture and Animal Husbandry in the Purāṇas -A Study comprises of six chapters in total.

The First Chapter

The First Chapter is titled as ‘An Introduction to the Purāṇas’. Here at the very outset the meaning and scope of the word purāṇa is discussed. The Purāṇas are the old narratives or ancient legends having informative contents. Yāska in his Nirukta defines Purāṇa as–purā navaṃ bhavati i.e. it was new in the days of yore. The Śabdakalpadruma and the Vācaspatyam explain Purāṇa as that which remains in the past and deals with the past. The meaning of the term purāṇa according to various Purāṇas is also mentioned here.

While discussing the characteristics of the Purāṇas both the Pañca Lakṣaṇa(fivefold characteristics) and Daśa Lakṣaṇa(tenfold characteristics) are discussed.

The Pañca Lakṣaṇa given by the Matsya Purāṇa and the Vāyu Purāṇa are–

1. Creation (sarga),
2. Dissolution and re-creation(pratisarga),
3. The lineage line (vaṃśa),
4. Jurisdiction of the fourteen Manus, belonging to the solar and lunar races (manvantara), and
5. The genealogy of the kings of the solar and lunar races (vaṃśānucarita).

It has been pointed out that the Amarakoṣa, Śiva Purāṇa, Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa, Viṣṇu Purāṇa, Skanda Purāṇa, Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa and Kūrma Purāṇa also mentions about these fivefold characteristics. Mentioning the fivefold characteristics of the Purāṇas the Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa states that these are appropriate for the Upapurāṇas only. The Bhāgavata Purāṇa and the Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa discusses about the Daśa Lakṣaṇa. The Padma Purāṇa looks on the various Purāṇas as the various limbs of Viṣṇu. Various opinions have been met with concerning the authorship of the Purāṇas. It is established that the authorship of the Purāṇas may be ascribed to Kṛṣṇa Dvaipāyana Vedavyāsa, the son of Satyavatī and Parāśara. Mentioning the date of the Purāṇas it has been held by S.D. Gyani that the earliest part of the Purāṇas was compiled not later than the last quarter of the 4th century B.C. Number and Division of the Purāṇas are too given.

The number of both the Purāṇas and the Upapurāṇas are said to be eighteen. Next to this, the classification of the Purāṇas into Sāttvika, Rājasika and Tāmasika,based on guṇa as stated by the Padma Purāṇa and the Garuḍa Purāṇa has been mentioned. Viṣṇu, Nāradīya, Bhāgavata, Garuḍa, Padma, and Varāha are Sāttvika Purāṇas, Brahmāṇḍa, Brahmavaivarta, Mārkaṇḍeya, Bhaviṣya, Vāmana and Brahma are Rājasika Purāṇas and Matsya, Kūrma, Liṅga,Śiva, Skanda and Agni are Tāmasika Purāṇas. According to the Matsya Purāṇa, there are other Purāṇas which are called Saṃkīrṇas(of mixed type). In these Purāṇas, the narration centers round Sarasvatī and the manes. After discussing the division of the Purāṇas, the contents of the five Purāṇas viz. the Agni Purāṇa, the Bhāgavata Purāṇa, the Matsya Purāṇa, the Vāyu Purāṇa and the Viṣṇu Purāṇa, have been discussed thoroughly. In the last part of this chapter justification of studying the five Purāṇas and literature review is given.

The Second Chapter

The Second Chapter is on ‘Agriculture and its Importance’. In the beginning of the chapter an idea is given about the agriculture in pre-Vedic period. After that the meaning of the term kṛṣi is mentioned. The word kṛṣi is used to denote agriculture, which literally means the act of ploughing. This word is derived from the root kṛṣ which means ‘to plough, till, cultivate’ with the suffix ik.

Importance of agriculture is traced out there after. In this regard the viewpoint of Kṛṣi-parāśara has been given. Importance of the agricultural products in general and of rice as a daily food in particular is emphasized by Parāśara because it is the agricultural crop, especially rice that keeps the population alive by providing it with food and nourishment. Therefore, Parāśara has advocated that the people shall take up agriculture at the cost of everything else. In the Purāṇic society, agriculture was a means to produce food grain so that human beings and other living creatures can sustain their life. The Viṣṇu Purāṇa states that agriculture is the survival of farmers karṣakāṇāṃ kṛṣirvṛttiḥ. The Bhāgavata Purāṇa mentions that the main profession of the Vaiśyas was to carry on business (vārtā-vṛtti). Vārtā comprises of tending cattle, tilling agricultural fields, carrying on trade and lending for profit. The Vāyu Purāṇa states that Svayambhu Brahmā devised plans for the purpose of agricultural cultivation. He ordained animal husbandry or cattle rearing, trade and agriculture for the Vaiśyas. Agriculture from the point of view of the Ṛgveda Saṃhitā, the Yajurveda Saṃhitā and the Atharvaveda Saṃhitā is discussed. Various methods of agriculture are met with as revealed in the Purāṇas. They are ploughing, favourable time for various process of agriculture, sowing of seeds, types of soil, process for checking the fertility of the soil, rites and methods of sowing and transplantation, manuring, irrigation and water management, excavation as well as consecration of tanks, rainfall, dependence upon the Sun as a support of agriculture, gleaning etc. Horticulture as a branch of agriculture is also marked out here. Under horticulture, the process of planting and the procedures of watering the trees are portrayed. Various agricultural products, oil seeds, vegetables and fruits which are found in the Purāṇas are mentioned. The Vāyu Purāṇa and the Viṣṇu Purāṇa refer to seventeen kinds of grāmya oṣadhis such as vrīhi, yava, godhūma, aṇu, tila, priyaṅgu, udāra, koradūṣa,satīnaka, māṣa,mudga, masūra, niṣpāva, kulattha, āḍhaka, canaka, śaṇa etc. Again fourteen kinds of yajñīya oṣadhis which were produced for the purpose of offering in sacrifices are also mentioned. They are-vrīhi, yava, māṣa, godhūma, aṇu, tila, priyaṅgu, kulattha, śyāmāka, nīvāra, jarttila, gavedhukā, markaṭaka and veṇu-yava. Along the above said fourteen kinds of grain, the Vāyu Purāṇa mentions one more grain named kuruvinda. Among the oil-seeds, ikṣu (sugarcane), tila (sesame), sarṣapa (mustard), rājikā (a mustard-type seed), eraṇḍa (castor-seeds), siddhārthaka (white mustard) kṣauma (linseeds), and akṣa are mentioned.

Various concepts related to agriculture are mentioned in this chapter. The Goddess Earth is prayed as the possessor of all seeds, all gems and all cereals (Agni Purāṇa, 41.24). The Agni Purāṇa has described the synonymous words of agricultural products and its related terms in the chapter 366. Auspiciousness of cereals and paddies are mentioned in the Purāṇas. It is stated that cereals and paddies, except those of the black species are auspicious and ensure the success of one’s mission, when looked at by a man at the time of leaving house (Ibid., 230.1). Other grains such as barley, wheat, sesamum, green gram and black gram, when they are consecrated by the offerings, they are dear to Viṣṇu (Vāmana Purāṇa ,95.21-22). Festivals and rites such as Āgneya Utsava, Indra Mahotsava, Madana Mahotsava and Saṃkrānti which are related to agriculture, are too mentioned. Agricultural products that are used as gifts in various rituals are mentioned in this chapter. The Agni Purāṇa says that gifts (dāna) are the best remedy for the gain of both the worlds; there is none who does not become favourable by means of gift or dāna. This chapter also holds discussion on the agricultural products used as medicines in various diseases which are mentioned in the Purāṇas. The Garuḍa Purāṇa states the medicinal value of various agricultural products.

The Third Chapter

The Third Chapter deals with the ‘Purāṇic Myths Related to Agriculture’. Here at the very outset derivation of the term ākhyāna is given. It is derived as āṅ khyā lyuṭ /kathanam iti.The Sanskrit term ākhyāna indicates myth, story or tale. The meaning of the word ākhyāna according to various sources is met with in this chapter. M. Monier Williams in his A Sanskrit-English Dictionary defines ākhyāna as a tale, story, and legend. The meaning of myth according to the Encyclopaedia Britannica and The Encyclopaedia of Religion and Ethics has also been conversed thereafter . Sukumari Bhattacharji opines that a myth is a statement of the state of affairs in illo tempore, i.e. something which happened at the beginning of time with cosmic or minor etiological creations subsumed under it. The concern and obligation of the socially responsible man is to preserve creation by re-enacting through prescribed sacraments that which the gods performed for creation and preservation (Literature in the Vedic Age, volume 1. p.128).

In this chapter all total 12 myths have been fully discussed which are directly or indirectly related with Agriculture. They are—

1) The myth of Pṛthu, the son of Vena—Pṛthu is the name of a semi-mythical dignitary who is mentioned in the Atharvaveda as the originator of agriculture.According to the Purāṇas, the Earth swallowed the medicinal herbs, the sacred grass, which was used at sacrifices and holy rituals because the kings disrespected her. Then Pṛthu got angry and wanted to punish Earth. But the Earth agreed to be milked and by this way corn was produced. According to the Brahma Purāṇa, Pṛthu, the tiger among men, milked the vegetable kingdom. Even today the subjects subsist on those food-grains and edibles.

2) The myth of Kuru—The references of ploughing and tilling the field that found in this account indicate the practice of agriculture in the days of the Purāṇas.

3) The myth of Indra and Ahalyā—Ahalyā means unploughed land or the land which is not suitable for growing crops. Ahalyā represents Gautama’s infertile land. Indra is the god of rains. Gautama’s hermitage and field becoming barren required Indra’s rains. Before Indra came to Ahalyā, she remained sterile. But after Indra had carnally united with her, she became fertile and gained the power of productivity. This signifies the land which became suitable for growing crops i.e. became suitable for agriculture.

4) The myth of Indra and Vṛtra—Indra is said to be the personification of thunderstorm here. The heavenly waters are obstructed by the cloud-demon, Vṛtra. Indra, therefore, smites down this cloud-demon by means of his vajra or thunderbolt, and releases the waters, which then flow down in the form of rain-showers. Timely rain is very much necessary for carrying on the process of agriculture. It was Vṛtra who withheld the timely rains, brought on distressing droughts that hampered the progress of agricultural work, resulting in a poor produce of food-grains. Indra shouting in great anger cut Vṛtra into pieces part by part and the waters flowed into the ocean. After this blessing has been received, the sun shines, and Earth again is bright. The land of Earth became suitable for agriculture.

5) The myth of Gautama and Gaṇeśa—In this story of sage Gautama as we find the references of the field of Gautama, full with barley and paddy, so it clearly indicates that the practicing of agriculture existed in the days of Purāṇas.

6) The legend behind the name Govardhana-Tīrtha—Here, in this myth, it is met with that people in the days of Purāṇas, used bullocks for the act of ploughing. This myth too gives us reference about the practice of agriculture which prevailed in the days of the Purāṇas.

7) The Account of King Ākāśa—Reference of using golden plough for tilling is met with in this story. This indicates the practice of agriculture in the days of the Purāṇas.

8) The account of Uplifting the mountain Govardhana—Here, the belief in the divinity of the mountains led to the worship of the mountains. Indra became angry caused by the disrespect shown to him and the rain fell in consequence of it. As a result of worshipping the mountain Govardhana, the clouds forcefully tormented Gokula with heavy showers, according to the command of angry Indra. This clearly brings out the tradition of worshipping the mountains for timely rain. Timely rain is very much necessary for the process of agriculture. Therefore, this myth may be related with agriculture.

Moreover, the myth of Viśvāmitra, Myth behind the creation of Agriculture, Myths about the production of Agricultural Products, Establishment of vārtā-vṛtti are also elaborated in this chapter.

The Fourth Chapter

The Fourth Chapter is entitled as ‘Animal Husbandry in the Purāṇas’. At the very beginning the derivative meaning of the term paśu and paśupālana are given. The word paśu indicates cattle (both singly and collectively).It is derived as sarvamaviśeṣeṇa paśyati, dṛś-ku, paśādeśaḥ. The term paśupālanam is used to denote animal husbandry, which literally means the tending or rearing of cattle (paśūn pālayati iti). Then it discusses about the Animal Husbandry in the Vedic period. After that the four-fold classification of creatures according to the Purāṇas, viz. aṇḍaja (oviparous), udbhijja(germinating ones), svedaja(those generated by warm vapours and sweat) and jarāyuja(viviparous), is mentioned. Another system of classification is indicated by the grāmya and āraṇya paśus. At the commencement of Tretā yuga, grāmya paśus and āraṇyakas were created by Prajāpati. The grāmya (i.e. the domesticated) animals are: go, aja, meṣa, aśva, aśvatārā and gardabha. The āraṇya (i.e. wild) beasts are: svāpadas, dvi-khuras (i.e. having two hoofs), hastī, vānara, pakṣis and sarī-sṛpas(sapta grāmya paśava, sapta āraṇyakas). The Bhāgavaṭa Purāṇa again mentions twenty-eight kinds of creatures.

The cow and the horse were the most important of the domesticated animals. The respect for the cow which is seen in the Vedas is prominent in the Purāṇas. Cows provide the best nourishment to all sorts of animal. They are the holiest and the best of all auspicious sights. They are the stepping stones to heaven and the most blessed animal. The Agni Purāṇa discusses the diseases of cow and their remedies in details.All these are discussed in this chapter. Under the heading ‘Feeding and Nourishment of cow’ it is there stated that a calf should be given a food which consist of māṣa, tila, godhūma, payaḥ, kṣīra and ghṛta(clarified butter), mixed with lavaṇa, to develope well. Moreover, preservation of cow-life, worshipping of cow, rearing and tending, rites performed for the welfare of cow, expiation for killing a cow, cows used as gifts in various rituals, pañcagavya(produced from cow) used in different occasions are also met with. Precautionary and curative measures have been elaborated in the Purāṇas for the treatment of diseased animals. The Agni Purāṇa states that the gods, the Brāhmaṇas, the cows and the holy sages are the objects of worship as they are the chief support of the universe. The bull figures in the Purāṇas mostly as a vehicle of Śiva who is also mentioned as Vṛṣabha-dhvaja or Vṛṣavāhana (i.e. having the Bull standard). Bullock carts (śakaṭa) were used for travelling.Qualities and breeds of bull are mentioned. Bulls were also used as gifts.

The Vāyu Purāṇa shows elephants as objects of gifts to Brāhmaṇas in śrāddha and mentions it as a Yajñīya animal. Breeds of elephants, management of elephants, characteristics of elephants, treatment and care of elephants, feeding and nourishment, preservation of elephant-life, care taken for war-elephants, rites performed for the welfare of elephants, kingdoms noted for elephants and kings who had elephants, elephants used as chariots, elephants used as gifts are mentioned. The horse is said to have born of the Creator’s feet in the Purāṇas.

Characteristics of horses, breeds of horses, favourable period for riding a horse, management of horses, positions of horse, treatment and care of horses, feeding and nourishment of horses, rites performed for the welfare of horse, preservation of horse-life, kingdoms noted for horses, horses used for gifts and horses used for chariots are met with in this chapter. Reference of other animals such as buffalo, goat, donkey, dog, camel, sheep, deer are also found. In the last of the chapter the traditional herbs and plants prescribed for the treatment of animals are mentioned as found in the Purāṇas.

The Fifth Chapter

The Fifth Chapter is on ‘Modern Relevance of Purāṇic Agriculture and Animal Husbandry’. At first, Agriculture and Animal husbandry in the post-Purāṇic Sanskrit works and its relevance with the Purāṇas are discussed. Here how the seeds are sown, the process of doing treatment of the seeds, the process of ploughing before sowing of seeds, about the fertility of the soil, about irrigation and water management, protection and care of the trees which are being planted, and importance of the cattle manure for the stable and suitable growth of paddy according to the post-Purāṇic Sanskrit texts are discussed in detail. Under the Animal Husbandry section breeds of animals, rearing and tending of animals, nourishment and feeding of animals, preservation of animal life etc. according to the post-Purāṇic Sanskrit works are discussed fully. Relevance of these topics with Purāṇic agriculture and animal husbandry is also traced out here in this chapter.

After that, Agriculture and Animal husbandry in the modern age of 20-21st century is met with. Under this heading firstly major crops and tools used in modern agriculture are mentioned. In India, generally three crops of paddy are raised in a year: autumn, winter and summer. Other food crops grown in India are wheat, millet, barley, maize, pulses, potato, etc. Various kind of oilseeds such as ground nut, mustard, sesamum, castor, linseed, safflower, etc. and fibre crops such as cotton, jute etc. are also grown in India. It is established that the harvesting of the different crops is carried out at present only by physical work and the implements used are only normal sickles, the cutting edge of which is specially hollowed out for this reason. Moreover, threshing machines, winnowing machines, feed cutting machines, stone threshing roller, wheat threshing roller etc. are also used. In modern times, tractors are being used for farming processes in India. Under this heading there also comes classification of soil, sowing of seeds, manuring, plant protection practices, and irrigation etc. Festivals related to agriculture are also talked about. Among the festivals related with agriculture Akṣayya Tṛtīyā, Bihu of Assam, Dīpāvalī, Poṅgal of Tamil Nadu are discussed fully.

In the Animal Husbandry section breeding policy, classification of breeds, feeding of cattle, management of cattle, rearing of cattle, indigenous plants for the treatment of diseases of animals in modern era have been discussed. These aforesaid topics discussed in this chapter can be summarized as follows-

Classification of breeds: According to M.S. Randhawa, the Indian breeds of cattle can be classified into the following three groups:

(a) Milch breeds.

(b) General-utility breeds: In this group, there are two types of cattle:

(i) Short-horned, white or light-grey cattle, and
(ii) Lyre-horned, grey cattle.

(c) Draught breeds.

Feeding of cattle: In India we have a large number of tree species whose leaves can be used for feeding livestock. While tree leaves are used sparingly for cattle and buffaloes; most of the sheep, goats and camels in the country are maintained on tree leaves. Trees of Prosopis, Acacia and Ficus species and the well-known neem are probably the only source of green material for summer in many parts of the country.

Management of cattle: This is a general term, denoting all operations connected with the maintenance of cattle. It includes feeding, providing accommodation, providing drinking water, grooming and washing animals, treating sick animals and training young stock.

Rearing of cattle: In rearing cattle, especially for dairy purposes, three things should be constantly kept in mind; viz., Breed, Feed and Trouble.

The Sixth Chapter

The Sixth Chapter deals with the summary of the thesis along with conclusive remarks. Thus, from the foregoing discussion of the chapters, it can be concluded that ancient Indian literature in the form of the holy Vedas and Purāṇas is flooded with various information on agriculture and animal husbandry. Agriculture and animal husbandry are closely related to each other. In India most of the people are connected with agriculture and animal husbandry. Agriculture and animal husbandry provides occupation, directly and indirectly, for the great majority of the people of our country. The agriculture in India has continued to keep the production high. Many large-scale and small scale industries are being set up in connection with agricultural products. All the modern agricultural technologies are applied in these industries. With the advent of modern scientific technological methods and applications, the traditional systems of agriculture and animal husbandry are developing day by day. In spite of this development, many people still follow the traditional agricultural methods. In the field of animal husbandry as well, a large number of people still depend on indigenous applications.

The Purāṇas are one of the foremost contributors of agriculture and animal husbandry in ancient India. Following the Vedic as well as the Purāṇic texts a number of sages like Varāhamihira, Kauṭilya, Kāśyapa, Parāśara, Surapāla, Śārṅgadhara and Chakrapāṇi Miśra contributed a lot to enhance Indian agriculture.

These were the pioneers of agriculture who knew farming very well and wrote texts like Bṛhatsaṃhitā, Arthaśāstra, Kāśyapīyakṛṣisūkti, Kṛṣi-parāśara, Vṛksāyurveda, Upavana-Vinoda and Viśvavallabha, in Sanskrit. We came to know that before the advent of modern science and technology, the process of agriculture and animal husbandry was fully known by the ancient Indians. Even today, in the modern age of 21st century, many Indian farmers follow the indigenous or traditional methods or techniques which have been laid down in our ancient treatises long ago. Hence, it is apparent from the above discussion that modern agriculturists unquestionably are indebted to the ancient treatises like the Vedas and the Purāṇas, since most of the techniques and methods of agriculture and animal husbandry are borrowed by our modern farmers from these ancient treatises.

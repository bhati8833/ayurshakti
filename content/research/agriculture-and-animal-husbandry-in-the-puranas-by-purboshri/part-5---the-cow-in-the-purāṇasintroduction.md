Part 5 - The Cow in the Purāṇas—Introduction

The cow and the horse were the most important of the domesticated animals. The respect for the cow which is seen in the Vedas[1] is prominent in the Purāṇas. The Vāyu Purāṇa speaks that the cow, which symbolizes the gāyatrī metre[2], had its birth from the belly of the Creator.[3] In the Agni Purāṇa, Dhanvantari suggests that a king should preserve the breed of cows in his country.[4] According to him the cows are holy and propitious.[5] The benefits of donating cows to a Brāhmaṇa are mentioned in the Matsya Purāṇa.[6] It states there that a cow should be given along with her calf and utensils made of kāṃsya. Kāṃsyam is fit to be a milking vessel and it is used for gift along with a cow.[7] The giving away of such a cow results in immense good advantages. The Matsya Purāṇa states that the cow is like the Earth with its mountains, forests, etc., till it bears calf in her womb. At that time one who gives it away he gets the profits of having given away the land with four oceans.[8] The urine of the cow is endowed with the quality of neutralizing all sorts of poison.[9] The body of a cow always has a pleasant smell.[10] The cows are the daughters of heavenly cow Surabhi and mothers of the three worlds. It is stated in the Agni Purāṇa that all animals depend on the cow for their nourishment. The cow serves the gods with the food they live upon, which only consists of libations of clarified butter offered to them during a religious sacrifice.[11] The gods in the heaven are pleased with the libations of clarified butter prepared from the milk of a cow which are offered to them in the course of a sacrifice, consecrated with the Mantras of the Vedas. The gods show food on earth in return of the libations of clarified butter which are offered to them.[12]

About the purity of the cow it is said in the Purāṇas that except the mouth her whole body is pure.[13] Their backs are also considered pure.[14] The cow is the sole purifying principle among all other animals.[15] The place which has once been sprinkled over with the urine of a cow, bad luck never effects that place.[16] Cows provide the best nourishment to all sorts of animal.

They are the holiest and the best of all auspicious sights. They are the stepping stones to heaven and the most blessed animal—

gāvaḥ pavitraṃ paramaṃ gāvo māṅgalyamuttamam/ 
gāvaḥ svargasya sopānaṃ gāvo dhanyāḥ sanātanāḥ//[17]

gāvaḥ pavitraṃ paramaṃ gāvo māṅgalyamuttamam/ 
gāvaḥ svargasya sopānaṃ gāvo dhanyāḥ sanātanāḥ//[17]

Cows are mentioned as the abodes of all beauty, they are also considered the holiest daughters of the god of creation i.e. Prajāpati.[18] The cows and the Brāhmaṇas form the two branches of the same family. The mantras reside in the Brāhmaṇas and the libations reside in the cows.[19] In the Garuḍa Purāṇa, the Lord prays that the cows may remain ever in his heart.May the cow, the majesty of all living beings, dispel his sins. Again the Goddess Lakṣmī is prayed to assume the form of a cow and remove the sins.[20] Even the cow-stall was also considered sacred in the Purāṇas.

At the śrāddha ceremony if fire is not available to offer into, then the offering may be placed in a cow-stall.[21] The Agni Purāṇa enjoins that the pools from where a cow would drink should be regarded as a holy place.[22] The ground on which cow stands upon was considered sacred. No sort of diseases could spoil that ground which was purified by the breath of a cow.[23] If a cow comes in front in the course of journey, it was deemed as a good scene.[24] The tail of a cow was waved round the face of a person for auspiciousness. The term gopucchabhramaṇam, indicates waving of cow’s tail over children to remove fear from their minds, which was an ancient Yādava practice.[25] Goḥ karīṣam refers to dried cow dung placed on the head of afflicted children to remove evils; which was also an ancient Yādava practice.[26] Yakṣī, one of the ten pīṭhas for images with three mekhalās, is the Vedī of prosperity.It gives one plenty of cows.[27]

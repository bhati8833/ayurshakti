PREFACE The entire world consists of teachers for the wise, and enemies for the fools. Therefore the knowledge conducive to health, longevity, fame and excellence coming from any unfamiliar source should be received, assimilated and utilized with earnestness, as a step towards attaining perfection of Ayurveda. Charaka Samhita Science, is essentially a quest, an incessant, insatiable quest for knowledge. And as long as mysteries abound in the universe, the quest would go on. Rooted deep in experience and observation, the quest has progressed right from the times of primitive man started observing things around him, and has fledged out to limitless outerspace today, where he is observing things now. It is this impassionate quest which is the life line of science. Caught between the two extremes of the vanished past and the unknown future - it is this immanent zeal that impels us through the cornucopia of the secrets of life, is synonymous with.

Even in this age of sophistication the main tools of scientific enquiry remains essentially the same as it was ages ago experience and observation. As tools, these are as effective as ever because the fact remains that nature is inexorable and acts only through immutable laws which She never transgresses. It is in our attempt to identify and understand these laws that the various disciplines of science came about; one among which is medicine. Somewhere in the dark ages, when man started curiously observing the behavior of the wild animals and started experimenting with juices, fruits, flowers, leaves, roots and tubers of the plants around him, the seed of medicine was sown. In India, this scientific tempo was sustained for a long time and reached its peak around the period when the treatise began to be written - popularly known as the Samhita Kala. At the same time, elsewhere in the west, the quest to understand and to conquer nature developed an attitude of drifting away from its all encompassing effects somewhere during the Renaissance period. Rapid strides of scientific progress diverted the attention of the scientists into the eyepiece of the microscope. And away from the all important relationship of man and his environment, the holistic view of man as an epitome was totally forgotten. ii

Fortunately for India this was not the case. Here, it was this very concept of holism that formed the mainstay of both philosophical speculation as well as medicine. Thus when the western intelligentsia came up with the Germ theory, oriental seekers were speaking in simpler terms of the different forces and facets of nature, of the harmonious interplay of these in all living beings, of man himself as epitome of the universe, of ambient harmony as health, of dis-equilibrium, vitiation and disharmony as a disease, of Pancabhuta, of Tridosha and of Agni. Medicine was essentially an art to begin its with, even in the west. It remained so till the eighteenth century. It had long given up philosophical speculation with the advent of Hippocrates on the scene somewhere around 460 370B.C. Today when the line of demarcation is growing thinner and thinner day by day and blending imperceptibly into one another, the art of medicine comes to lie in the skillful application of all scientific disciplines to human problems. With the fast horizon of knowledge and the astonishing rate of growth of our understanding of the world around us, the wheel has come full circle. The more we understand life the more we realise that 111

there is ever more to it. We realize that the biological systems are indeed very complex and that life cannot be studied by dissecting it into minuscule units and in isolation. It has to be studied in toto; as a whole, as an inevitable and integral part of nature. This subtle but revolutionary paradigm shift, in the field of sciences, has had the salutary effect of shifting the focus once again on holism and oriental science like Ayurveda that had advanced, advocated, and adhered to this concept. Though belated, the realization has drawn intellects from all over the world to the oriental way of thinking and the theories and concepts it expounded. Now it has been recognized that traditional medicine, like the earth itself has special powers of healing particularly particularly the deep seated disorders, that was overlooked in the rush to develop a standardized technology based medicine. The concept of Marma in Ayurveda and Tamil manuscripts is one among the concepts that has attracted world wide attention. levance In Ayurveda the word marma refers to certain vital points on the human anatomy. It is a main part of Salyatantra. According to Susrutha these are the conglomeration of Mamsa, Asthi, Sira, Snayu, Sandhi. iv

Marmas are the seats of life and any injury to these marma points will invariably produce diverse symptoms of injuries even instantaneous death in some cases. In Tamil scriptures a very exhaustive as varmas are description of Varma points is given extensively used in warfare in general and self defence in particular. Also a vivid description is available in this literature on the treatment of each and every marmabhighata (injury to marma). These are mostly prevalent in the Nadar community and those who practise medical system are called as Varmani or Marmani. There is yet another type of practice which is mostly in Malabar, that is North Kerala called Abhyasa marma. This is mostly practised as a part of martial art and the ones who teach this are called Gurukkal. The present study is undertaken with a view to pin point various marma points, compare them anatomically and also to correlate these with Tamil and Kulayana schools of marma points. Though specific references to the treatment of marmabhighata are pitiably rare, if not lacking altogether, in classical Ayurvedic literature, the same has been extensively dealt with in the Tamil literature. It was felt that a study of the classical Tamil

literature and voluminous Ayurvedic exposition on the various aspects of marma with its special reference to its applied anatomy would be very much in the interest of updating the knowledge on the subject. I became interested in both these systems at an early age. My father, being a renowned practitioner in this particular field has always encouraged and exposed me to the depths of this vast treasure of knowledge. After having examined both systems in detail, it became clear to me that the differences between the two are more semantic than substantial, by changing a few key terms the corpus knowledge of one system can be translated into the other, thus widening the scope of each. This thesis consist of Preface Introduction 1. 2. 3. Classification of marma 4. Concept of Trimarmeeya 5. Comprehensive study of marma with its applied anatomy 6. Conclusion 7. Appendix 8. Bibliography vi

The Preface consists of the brief preamble for the purpose and relevance of this study. Part One, The Introduction has three sections. Section one deals with introduction of Ayurvedic marma sastra. Section two deals with the introduction of Tamil varma sastra. In the section three a brief introduction of Kulayana marma sastra is given. Part Two, too consists of three sections, one mainly deals with the classification of marma as detailed in Ayurvedic texts. Section two deals with the Tamil varma classification. Section three deals with Kulayana marma classification. Trimarmeeya a unique classification done by the Charaka is dealt with separately. Part Three, The main section consists of a comprehensive study of marma. Here a fixed pattern is made use of : I. Etymology II. Description III. Surface Anatomy IV. Anatomical Structures corresponding to this marma. V. Discussion vii

The data available in Ayurveda is so very compact, that all the three authors who dealt with this marma sastra agree in names, numbers, locations and symptoms. But this is not the case in Tamil and Kulayana marma sastras. Here each author has considered the names, locations, symptoms and prognosis from his on view point. Hence it has become highly impossible for standardization of each marma. However effort has been made to standardise the linear measurement, time and force where ever possible. This is probably a comprehensive study of marma from this perspective. Such an in depth study on marma cikitsa which is part of India's traditional medical and martial heritage, dating from around twelfth century AD needs to be undertaken having recourse to various paradigm. As nothing can claim completeness or finality in life, so it is with this humble endeavor. The multitude of anatomical figures and drawings required to expose the entire gamut of this study coupled with limitation of space compels selection and to this extend imposes limitation. Similarly with the diverse views with regard to etymology, site identification, diagnosis, treatment and revival techniques bordering almost on contradiction existing within the Tamil varma sastra viii

and Kulayana marma system restrict presentation of information in totality making selective study imperative. Where the Tamil varma and Kulayana marma do not directly correlate or correspond to the Ayurvedic marmas which form the base of the study, attempt has been made to include the same in an appendix at the end of each chapter. * ix

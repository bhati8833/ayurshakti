## 📜 1. Section Overview & Classical Context

Charaka Samhita and Sushruta Samhita

This page relates ‘Medicine and Religion (Introduction)’ of the study on the Charaka Samhita and the Sushruta Samhita, both important and authentic Sanskrit texts belonging to Ayurveda: the ancient Indian science of medicine and nature. The text anaylsis its medical and social aspects, and various topics such as diseases and health-care, the physician, their training and specialisation, interaction with society, educational training, etc.

Medicine and Religion (Introduction)

Healing and faith have shared some form of association since the earliest times. Belief in magico-religious elements has influenced the concept of disease and the nature of treatment in antiquity. As the ancient physicians had to constantly battle with the unknown, in complex and less understood cases, they often fell back on variables derived from the world of the supernatural to explain the cause of physical diseases. The tremendous progress made by scientific medicine notwithstanding, the role of religious beliefs has not been completely negated. Even today many patients and physicians take comfort in faith and spirituality when therapeutics can do precious little. The use of religion to help cope with illness, especially in serious cases, is probably widespread today.[1]

According to D.P. Chattopadhyaya, the only discipline in ancient India that had the promise of being fully secular and contained clear potentials of the modern understanding of natural science was medicine.[2] Though medicine had taken “the momentous step from magico-religious therapeutics to rational therapeutics, i.e., in the terminology of the physicians themselves from daiva-vyāpāśraya bheṣaja to yukti-vyāpāśraya bheṣaja,”[3] it could not emancipate itself from magical elements, ritualism and mysticism. The extant medical treatises of Caraka and Suśruta are situated in a religious context and much of the analogous medical evidence also occurs in a similar framework–for instance in Buddhist and Jain canonical and semi-canonical texts. The overlap of medicine and religion in antiquity arises from the shared common ground of human concerns, that is, to live in harmony with the natural world. By religion we understand a system of beliefs and practices centering on the worship of the supernatural. Fundamental to Vedic theism is the concept of Ṛta or the moral order of the universe. It represents the concept of divine ordinance: “…the Law which God from before the beginning hath set for himself to do all things by.”[4] Man is, therefore, required to obey rules because nature herself does so, and this obedience ensures the essential cohesion in nature and society.[5] Viewed from this perspective, failure to abide by the laws of nature affects the individual’s well being which gives rise to disease. The concern of Āyurveda is living in harmony with nature as both the universe and the human bodyare composed of the pañcamahābhūtas or the five basic elements. Besides, medicine is concerned with the complete physical, mental and spiritual well-being of the individual.

In this chapter we shall identify the ritualistic and magical aspects of medicine and examine the nature of their intersection in our sources. The discourses of both Caraka and Suśruta Saṃhitās are rooted in the Brahmanism. Adherence to formalistic elements of Brahmanism is evident throughout the medical Saṃhitās, particularly in the display of reverence for Vedic rites and observance of the Vedic rules. Both the Caraka Saṃhitā and the Suśruta Saṃhitā espouse authority of the Vedas; consequently being an āstika or having faith in the authority of the sacred texts is regarded essential for success in therapeutic application.[6] It is one of the desirable attributes of the patient[7] as also of sāttvika individuals.[8]

The nāstikas (non-believers in the authority of the Vedas) are criticised for their core beliefs, such as, denial of the existence of the soul (atman),[9] non-acceptance of perception as a source of knowledge and rejection of belief in gods, sages and siddhas.[10] They are declared as the worst of the sinful (pātakebyaḥ paraṃ caitat pātakaṃ naāstikagrahaḥ) for whom everything happens by chance (yadṛcchopahatātmanaḥ).[11] Non-belief (nāstikya) is a sin that can cause foetal abnormalities.[12] Both medical authorities argue in favour of the existence of the permanent (śāsvata)[13] soul as an entity that is distinct from the body. It is responsible for the individual’s ego, actions, the fruits of actions, transmigration from one body to another and memory.[14]

The body (śarīra), mind (sattva) and the soul (ātman) are like a tripod of life:

sattvamātmā śarīraṃ ca traya-etat-tridaṇḍavat.[15]

sattvamātmā śarīraṃ ca traya-etat-tridaṇḍavat.[15]

The medical texts have incorporated performance of yajña (fire ritual),[16] offering of pūjā (prayers),[17] practice of penance (tapa),[18] homa (fire oblations),[19] bali (offering/propitiatory oblation),[20] maṅgala (auspicious rituals)[21] and prāyaścitta (rituals for counteracting effects of possible sinful deeds)[22] in Āyurvedic therapeutics in conjunction with the conventional clinical measures. We have noted in chapter 4 that religious rites as a therapeutic measure are particularly advised in the context of fever, consumption, epilepsy and psychic disorders. Auspicious rites are prescribed at the commencement of most procedures, as for instance, prior to the administration of a dose of medicated oil or ghṛta for oleation (snehan),[23] prior to emesis[24] and purgation[25] as well as at the commencement and completion of rāsāyanic treatment. References to Vedic rites occur not only in the context of therapeutics; they have been adopted at the student’s initiation ceremony,[26] for obtaining progeny of desired qualities,[27] or for the protection of the new born and the mother in peurperium.[28] It is not only in treatment that the religious element is evident. Procurement of drugs is also preceded by the observance of fast and auspicious rites, prayers to the gods, the Aśvins, cows and brāhmaṇas.[29]

Impersonal supernatural forces are believed to impact humans in both positive and negative ways. Abhicāra (spells or magic), abhiśāpa (curse) and abhiṣaṅga (possession by evil spirits) cause harm by way of diseases[30] but their evil effects can be warded off by counteractive incantations and rituals.[31] Not all seizures (abhidharṣaṇa) are caused by evil spirits; they are also attributable to supernatural beings (grahas) ranging from the gods and ṛṣis to piśācas and rākṣasas.[32] It is believed that such types of seizures occur on the particular days designated to each class of grahas; for instance, possession by the gods generally takes place on the first and thirteenth days of the bright fortnight (śukla pakṣa) at an opportune moment, and so on.[33]

In order to protect the individual from seizures, effects of sorcery and curse, the physicians resort to measures that can be described as elements of magic. Japa (incantation),[34] wearing jewels (maṇi), tying auspicious talisman (maṅgalya) and poisonous substances (viṣa) on the body (dhāraṇa),[35] touching auspicious objects[36] or drugs,[37] visiting saints (darśana),[38] observance of vows (vrata)[39] and Vedic rules (Vedoktān niyama),[40] apart from drugs (auṣadha), are believed to be both protective and therapeutically beneficial to the patient. Amulets of the drugs mūṣikā and ajaruhā tied on the arm or the wrist are believed to neutralize the toxic effects of poisoned food, and are particularly prescribed for the king.[41] Amulets were believed to provide protection from all malevolent beings. When Rama was to leave Ayodhyā for the Daṇḍaka forest, his mother tied some herbs (oṣadhi) on his right hand to protect him from rākṣasas.[42] Recitation of mantras in cases of poisoning, particularly snake bites, is one of the best instances dependence on the magical element in medicine.[43] Their efficacy is derived from the power of truth and penance contained in them that has bequeathed by the sages.[44] Mantras are believed to be potentiated by truth, spiritual knowledge and penance.[45] They also mentally therapeutic, and hence, are recommended to provide consolation to a patient, as for instance to a woman after she gives birth to a stillborn baby.[46]

James G. Frazer points out that while religion seeks to propitiate, magic normally acts on its own, that is, it seeks to constrain.[47] By “magic” is meant the power to control the supernatural beings.[48] In the Vedic rituals, as it has been pointed out by Jan Gonda, there is no hard-and-fast line between the magic and the religious domain. In invoking the gods and in offering sacrifices to them words would be pronounced which show the belief in man’s mastery over powers and natural forces without divine intervention.[49] According to Frazer, “In medical practices, words, incantations, ritual and astrological observances are magical; this is the realm of the occult and of the spirits, a world of ideas which imbues ritual movements and gestures with a special kind of effectiveness, quite different from their mechanical effectiveness.”[50]

Thus, magico-religious elements, such as, ritual offerings, incantations, discussion of religious scriptures, constant association with the virtuous and the observance of ethical precepts of celibacy, truthfulness and charity are included in Āyurvedic thrapeutics. In fact, these are the measures that are recommended for protection during epidemics.[51]

Ritualistic elements are also present in the preparation of drugs. Hymns are chanted during preparation of drugs,[52] at the time of preservation[53] and for potentiating them. Rituals provide protection from unknown forces; therefore they have to be performed in the storage area for drugs every day.[54] Rāsayānic drugs like Soma[55] and similar rejuvenating drugs[56] are administered only after potentiating them with recitations from the scriptural texts or after treating with sacrificial rites. Kevalāmalaka rasāyana is an example of how drugs are believed to acquire miraculous powers through incantation. The treatment requires the individual to live for a year on cow’s milk, recite the Sāvitrī mantra and observe celibacy. At the end of the year on a suitable day in the fortnight of Pauṣa, Māgha or Phālguna after observing fast for three days, he enters a garden of āmalakī and climbs a tree bearing large fruits. Holding one fruit in hand he recites the Brahmā mantra till the fruit is impregnated with amṛta (ambrosia). By consuming these fruits he becomes brilliant like the gods.[57] Thus, potency lies not in the fruit itself but in the words of the spell. The marvellous effects on the body and mind that rasāyanic treatment is claimed to produce, such as longevity of thousand years, phenomenal memory, absence of disease, etc., are attributable to belief in magic.

It is noticed that generally an auspicious day is selected for conducting events and procedures whether it is the student’s initiation,[58] conducting emesis or purgation,[59] surgery for draining an abscess,[60] or taking a medicated bath during pregnancy.[61] Besides, gathering of certain drugs is recommended during particular constellations, and there is one instance where it is laid down for procuring an animal product.[62] The anśumat variety of the soma plant is to be gathered keeping in mind the auspiciousness of the day, time, moment and the asterisms. It is collected according to the rites enjoined for a sacrifice, bathed ceremoniously by the priests and offered to the fire to the chanting of hymns before its juice can be administered.[63] The chanting of mantra is also essential during the collection of seven other plants which are deemed to have similar therapeutic benefits with Soma. These and eleven other ambrosial drugs are said to contain the remnant of the nectar partaken by the gods.[64]

Significance has been given by the ancient physicians to oneiromancy and augury which are believed to reveal information about the physiological state of the patient. Divination through dreams,[65] interpretation of signs observed in the messengers,[66] the shadow cast by the patient[67] as well as of omens occurring on the way to or at the time of arrival or inside the patient’s house[68] can indicate the disease and hold the key to the prognosis of the disease. Along with clinical symptoms, these omens are known as pūvarūpa[69] or riṣṭas.[70] Caraka designates the section on signs of imminent death as Indriya-sthāna wherein the term “indriya’ means the signs and symptoms (liṅga) of life (indra).[71]

With the incorporation of Vedic rites and rituals in medicine, it becomes necessary to accord proper deference to brāhmaṇas which is evident from their presence at the initiation ceremony of the medical student,[72] at the maternity home[73] or during the child’s naming ceremony.[74] Respectful behaviour towards the deities, cows, brāhmaṇas, preceptors (guru), elderly, siddhas (perfected beings endowed with supernatural qualities) and teachers (ācāryas) as well as offering oblations to the sacred fire and performing sandhyā at dawn and dusk have been included in normative ethics (sadvṛtta) that ensures good health.[75] Prayers to cows and brāhmaṇas are enjoined upon the medical student as well.[76] Appearance of brāhmaṇas and cows in the patient’s dream[77] or their sighting on the way to the patient’s house are regarded as a favourable signs.[78] Sighting of brāhmaṇas house or devotion to them on the part of the patient are signs indicative of speedy recovery from illness.[79] Veneration of brāhmaṇas and cows is important principle of normative conduct in the medical texts so much so that the desire to cause injury upon them as well as ascetics is taken as a premonitory symptom of exogenous psychic disorder (unmāda).[80]

It is interesting to keep in mind that while on the one hand, there is an inextricable link between religion and medicine, on the other, religion and morality are also closely intertwined. Adherence to ethical precepts, such as, truthfulness, devotion to parents, teachers and respected persons, charity, etc., is prescribed for the well being of the patient. It is significant that Caraka includes prāyascitta (atonement) as a synonym of bheṣaja or medicine.[81] According to the commentator that just as prāyascitta removes diseases produced by sins, so do medicines remove diseases; therefore prāyascitta becomes synonymous with medicine.[82]

For protection (rakṣāvidhāna) from unknown forces the patient recuperating from wounds is advised to observe the five yama and five niyama precepts.[83] Niyama is also advised for the patient suffering form fever[84] and psychic disorders.[85] Yama and niyama are two of the preparatory stages described in the Yogasūtra of Patañjali intended to inculcate moral restraint and discipline for further ascetic practice. Yama is a set of restraints consisting of non-violence (ahiṃsā), truthfulness (satya), non-stealing (asteya), chastity (brahmacarya) and absence of greed (aparigraha).[86] The practice of these five restraints leads to five observances or niyama. Cleanliness or purity (śauca), contentment (santośa), penance (tapas), self-study (svadhāyaya) and devotion to God (īśvarapraṇidhāna) are the constituents of niyama.[87] Cleanliness may be regarded as consisting of two aspects: They have been accommodated in medicine so as to impose restraints on the patient. Yoga is essentially theistic in that it admits the existence of God.[88] Thus, the moral basis of the social environment of the medical Saṃhitās is derived from brahmanical sources.

After examining the magico-religious elements of medicine, we shall now look at the role of the principal deities mentioned in the two treatises in their proper contexts. The texts accord the foremost position to Brahmā as the composer and first exponent of Āyurveda[89] who then passed on the knowledge to other deities. According to the Caraka Saṃhitā, the deities associated in the transmission of Āyurveda are Dakṣa, the Aśvins and Indra, and it was Indra who taught sage Bharadvāja.[90] The Suśruta Saṃhitā traces the transmission of this science through Brahmā to Prajāpati, followed by the Aśvins and Indra before it was acquired by Dhanvantari.[91] We shall also examine the association of these deities with healing. Despite the cardinal position accorded to Brahmā, we notice that subsequently he gradually loses his pre-eminence in the practice of medicine to Śiva and Viṣṇu. It will also be our endeavour to analyse this shift.

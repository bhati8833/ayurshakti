# 27. The Position of the Physician in Society

> **TL;DR:** Chapter 27 of **Charaka Samhita And Sushruta Samhita By Nayana Sharma** by **Nayana Sharma** explores key findings on *The Position of the Physician in Society*. It bridges traditional Sanskrit literature with contemporary medical science.

---

## 📜 1. Section Overview & Classical Context

Charaka Samhita and Sushruta Samhita

This page relates ‘Position of the Physician in Society’ of the study on the Charaka Samhita and the Sushruta Samhita, both important and authentic Sanskrit texts belonging to Ayurveda: the ancient Indian science of medicine and nature. The text anaylsis its medical and social aspects, and various topics such as diseases and health-care, the physician, their training and specialisation, interaction with society, educational training, etc.

The Position of the Physician in Society

We shall now look the position of the physician in ancient Indian society from different perspectives-that of the orthodox brahmanical society and the non-brahmanical view as reflected in the medical texts, Buddhist and Jaina works as well as in epigraphs. We shall first turn to the Vedic corpus which provides us significant information on this issue.

In the earliest text of the Vedic corpus, the divine twin physicians, the Aśvins, are glorified as great healers and surgeons. They are described as “rich in wondrous deeds”, and as “heroes worthy of our praise.”[1] Besides, like many other gods, they are also described as having great wisdom.[2] No less than 51 hymns in the Ṛgveda have been composed in their praise celebrating their medical and surgical feats. This is the largest number dedicated to any deity after Indra, Agni and Soma,[3] indicating the importance accorded to the divine physicians and to physicians in general by the Ṛgvedic seers. The divine physicians are called upon to accept the offering of sacrificial food.[4] One hymn (X.97) written in praise of the healer (bhiṣak) was written by a physician who was also a poet. The composition of a Vedic hymn itself signifies the esteem accorded to a healer in Ṛgvedic society.[5] This hymn describes the healer as a wise brāhmaṇa (vipra).

The attitude towards physicians, however, undergoes a drastic transformation the later Vedic texts as well as in the Brahmanical law books, which reveal an attitude of intense disapproval towards healers and the healing profession, so much so the Aśvins had to suffer ritual exclusion from the sacrifices. The divine physicians are declared impure, and so is the medical profession itself reflecting the extreme antipathy of the law-givers for physicians. The denigration of the medical practitioners in later Vedic corpus and the law books is a subject that has drawn the attention of many scholars. The first clear pronouncement against physicians occurs in the Taittariya Saṃhitā (VI.4.9).

This significant passage reads:

“The gods said of these two (the Aśvins): “Impure are they, wandering among men and physicians.”[6] Therefore a Brāhman should not practice medicine for the physician is impure, unfit for the sacrifice.”[7]

“The gods said of these two (the Aśvins): “Impure are they, wandering among men and physicians.”[6] Therefore a Brāhman should not practice medicine for the physician is impure, unfit for the sacrifice.”[7]

The divine physicians are, thus, declared impure “as they are the doctors roving among men.”[8] Therefore, they are deprived of libations at soma sacrifices. The text explains how the Aśvins had to negotiate for their share of the sacrifice when the gods approached them seeking medical help to put back the severed head of the sacrifice. The Aśvins used the occasion to elicit a boon that libations be drawn for them as well. The gods agreed reluctantly; the twin physicians are first purified by the bahiṣ ānas and receive their share of the libations.[9] The ritual pollution of the Aśvins is reiterated in the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa (4:1:5:13) in the context of the rejuvenation Ṛṣi Cyavana, where the Ṛṣi characterises them as incomplete and imperfect for which they were excluded from the sacrifice to be performed by the gods at Kurukṣetra.[10]

Perhaps the most trenchant condemnation comes from Indra in the Anuśāsana-parva of the Mahābhārata in another version of the same legend. He explains that the gods cannot drink Soma in the company of the Aśvins for they are outcasts and the most degraded.[11]

The metamorphosis of the noble and the “irreproachable”[12] deities into the “most degraded” of the gods is a conundrum for the scholars of ancient Indian medicine. Some scholars like Filliozat and Gopal read the above quoted passage from the Taittariya Saṃhitā the manifestation of sectarian differences among the rival Yajurvedic schools., i.e., between the Caraka- śākhā (a school of the Black Yajurveda) and the Taittiriya-śākhā (a school of the White Yajurveda). The scholars of the White Yajurveda and the Taitttiriyas often blame the Carakas.[13] Filliozat contends that the term “cara’, which is used in the Brāhmaṇa texts for “roving”, is a synonym of the name of the Caraka school. He believes that people belonging to the Caraka-śākhā gave medicine a place of honour and that the Carakas included the physician Caraka.[14] Hence, the passage can be interpreted as a censure of both the Caraka priests and the doctors.[15]

The second premise of Filliozat’s argument concerns the impurity of the Aśvins about which there is unanimity in the different saṃhitās. They were not originally soma-drinkers, and even the Ṛgveda describes them as “the drinkers of nectar” (madhupa).[16] While the other saṃhitās are silent about the grounds of their impurity, the Taitttiriya Saṃhitā assigns the cause to the wanderings of the Aśvins among men, or as the passage states “the two roving among men” (manuṣya carau). Their promiscuity with humans places them below the gods, and since the term employed to say that they are “roving”, that is, cara is a synonym of the Carakas, therefore, “one may believe that the Taittiriyas have fired a passing shot at the rival school of Carakas.”[17]

Gopal accepts Filliozat’s hypothesis and suggests that sectarian rivalries and jealousies among the Vedic people in early times may have given birth to ideas about the low social position of the physicians. He contends that the profession at this point of time was confined mostly to the members of the Caraka-śākhā. They were possibly not very particular about meticulously following the rules about Vedic rituals, as is indicated by their not partaking of soma, which was one of the characteristic parts of the Vedic sacrificial system. It is not unlikely that the Carakas approved the use of liquor as medicine and hence their rival the Taittariyas criticised them.[18] It is also probable that their involvement with their duties as a physician was in itself one of the causes which made them lax in following all the rules of Vedic ritualism.[19]

D.P. Chattopadhyaya, however, finds Filliozat’s hypothesis “completely unwarranted” for it is difficult to establish any reference to Caraka-adhvaryus in the particular passage in question.[20] Moreover, he fails to find any association of the Caraka-adhvaryus with physicians and neither does Filliozat provide any convincing correlation barring the similarity in name with the celebrated medical treatise. He also points out that this censuring of the Aśvins is a persistent theme of the entire Yajurvedic tradition that comes down to us; and it is also occurs in the texts belonging to the Caraka-adhvaryus themselves-the Kāṭhaka, Kapiṣṭhala-kāṭha-Saṃhitā and the Maitrāyaṇi Saṃhitā.[21] The second premise that the Aśvins were not Soma-drinkers even in the Ṛgveda does not stand scrutiny when several hymns in the text enjoin the divine physicians to drink soma prepared for them.[22]

Soma is specifically prepared for them:

“Awaken (the Aśvins) who yoke (their chariot) in the morning, may the Aśvins come here for the drink of this Soma.”[23]

“Awaken (the Aśvins) who yoke (their chariot) in the morning, may the Aśvins come here for the drink of this Soma.”[23]

The abundant Soma drink is a treasure laid out for Aśvins (Ṛgveda I.183.4)[24] who have Agni, Indra, Varuṇa, Viṣṇu, Ādityas, Rudras, Vasus among others as their companions (Ṛgveda VIII.35.1-4).[25] Of their association with the sacrifice, Ṛgvedic poets are unambiguous. The Aśvins” visits to the sacrifices are the most frequent (Ṛgveda V.41.3), and their attachment to the sacrifice is compared to that of an axle to the yoke and navel of the chariot (Ṛgveda V.43.8).[26] As for their “roving nature” and interaction with mortals, it may be pointed out that this trait is common to many other deities of the early period. “In the dim antiquity of Ṛgvedic poetry, the very difference between man and god is yet to take a clearly defined form.”[27] Indra is praised as the chief among men (nṛtamaḥ) while Agni is addressed as the chief human being among the human beings (nṛnāṃ nṛtamaḥ).[28] There are several instances of Indra intermingling with humans. It is, therefore, strange that the A vins should be singled out and characterized as “impure” for the same trait.

In the opinion of Chattopadhyaya, the crucial factor here is the medical practice of the Aśvins that required far more commitment to democratic values that was tolerable to the priests. As Bloomfield comments, medicine is condemned in the Yajurveda because “the practice entails promiscuous, unaristocratic mingling with men.”[29] In the later Vedic tradition they are considered polluting and hence do not qualify for the sacrificial share. Interaction with the masses is particularly loathsome for the upper echelon of society. This position is accepted in later legal literature as well.

A differing view is given by Zysk who holds that despite the laudatory hymns to the Aśvins in the Ṛgveda, the precise social role of the masters of healing cannot be ascribed.[30] He refers to a Ṛgvedic hymn (9.112.1) that places healers (bhiṣaj) between carpenters (takṣan) and priests (brāhmaṇa). Like the uneducated carpenters, healers repaired what was injured or broken, and like the learned priests, they commanded esoteric knowledge. The physicians were a particular group of professionals who combined the craftsmanship of the woodwright with the intellection of the priest.[31] He contends that the Aśvins were respected but never granted a seat among the ritualists of the sacrificial cults; therefore he does not concur with D.P. Chattopadhyaya that physicians in the early Vedic period were held in high esteem.[32]

The relevant verse is rendered by Griffith as follows:

“We all have various thoughts and plans, and diverse are the ways of men. The Brahman seeks the worshipper, wright seeks the cracked, and leech the maimed.”[33]

“We all have various thoughts and plans, and diverse are the ways of men. The Brahman seeks the worshipper, wright seeks the cracked, and leech the maimed.”[33]

The verse makes no reference to the physician gods, only to the physician in the context of the diverse objectives of the men. Therefore, it is difficult to accept Zysk’s reading. We have already cited instances of the association of the Aśvins with the sacrificial rite.

We may cite another:

“They (the Aśvins) are the knowers of the sacrifice (and) of the holy rite who drink with free will (i.e. pleasure) the sweet of Soma.[34]

“They (the Aśvins) are the knowers of the sacrifice (and) of the holy rite who drink with free will (i.e. pleasure) the sweet of Soma.[34]

Therefore, Zysk’s contention that the Aśvins were not honoured in the Ṛgvedic pantheon does not stand up to scrutiny. Contempt for the physician has no sanction in the ancient poetry of the Ṛgveda.[35] It is not the Aśvins alone but some of the other deities are also celebrated for their medical skills. They include Rudra, Soma, Varuṇa, Ap, Maruts, and so on.[36] The ascription of ritual impurity to the Aśvins, thus, appears to be a deliberate attempt of the later priesthood to denigrate them. There is the view that the worship of the twin deities was not favoured by some Vedic schools of the later period, perhaps because the system of sacrifice led to the acceptance of some new deities in preference to the older ones.[37] In the Mahābhārata account of the Cyavan episode, it was Indra who raised objection to the offering of Soma to the Aśvins. Sukumari Bhattacharji is inclined to view this as a fallout of the conflict of the two cults-that of the Aśvins and Indra.[38]

Chattopadhyaya brings to our notice that eulogistic references to the divine physicians are true of a very early period when Vedic society itself was comparatively simple and was yet to witness the stabilization of the hierarchical norm which takes place from the period of the Yajurveda.[39] The passages from Taittiriya Saṃhitā and Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa, in fact, herald the beginning of brahmanical counter-ideology against the medical profession. According to Zysk, it is association of healers with the Atharvaveda that lies at the root of the brahmanical attitude of derision. In the Vedic period, this Veda was not considered a principal śruti (revealed) scripture. Moreover, the physicians often moved out of the ambit of Aryan society in search of drugs which brought them into contact with the non-Aryan peoples. Undoubtedly these encounters enhanced their knowledge but they also “…caused them to be widely perceived as inferior beings polluted by contact with impure people,”[40] though he is of the opinion that this attitude existed from the early Vedic period but received articulation only in the later Brāhmaṇas. He asserts that their special knowledge was not accepted as part of the orthodox brāhmaṇic intellectual tradition in the late Vedic tradition.[41] The growing predominance of brāhmaṇas in this period was accompanied by the increasing rigours and orthodoxy of the four-fold varṇa system, and hardening of attitudes towards the notions of relative pollution and purity.[42]

Chattopadhyaya argues that the development of brahmanical contempt stems from the incompatibility of the aspirations of the physicians with the ideals of the emerging stratified society dominated by the priests. The ancient physicians aspire for the knowledge of nature as a whole.[43] The ideology of hierarchical society, on the other hand, is geared towards regulation and management of men and not the knowledge of nature,[44] and hence, inimical to the scientific aspirations. The general theoretical climate generated by the later Vedic corpus of literature and the brahmanical law-codes takes the form of a counter-ideology[45] that so drastically alters the social position of physicians.

The wanderings of the healers and their contact with the non-Aryans might well have given rise to an empirical orientation that became antagonistic to brāhmaṇic orthodoxy in the later Vedic period.[46] We can assume that they probably organized into sects and roamed the countryside from the phrase “roving physicians” (cāraṇavaidya), the title of a lost recension of the Atharvaveda.[47] The roving nature of the divine physicians is known from the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa. The reason stated for their exclusion is their association with men.

The gods said,

“We will not invite you; ye have wandered and mixed much among men, performing cures.”[48]

“We will not invite you; ye have wandered and mixed much among men, performing cures.”[48]

In the course of their wanderings physicians gathered information on new drugs and remedial treatments from other communities. It is probable that it was not just their interaction with people beyond the Aryan society that aroused the repugnance of the brahmanical orthodoxy. As we know from the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa, they also performed cures on men, whereby we may be reasonably assume, that some of the Aryan knowledge on remedies and healing must have been transmitted to the non-Aryan communities. The Āyurvedic tradition of discussions and debates on medical issues[49] testifies to the exchange of medical data among healers.[50] The objective of Āyurveda is the welfare of all living creatures for the Aśvins are said to have imparted the knowledge of Āyurveda to Indra for the welfare of the recipient and people.[51] Yet, it is interesting to note that in the classical medical texts, physicians are restrained from revealing of some of the special formulations. Caraka categorically emphasises that the rejuvenation recipes (rasāyanic formulae) should not be described or even mentioned to a person with evil designs, to one who is not a dvijāti among others.[52] Therefore, there appears to be a tradition of holding in secrecy those formulations particularly treasured for their miraculous benefits. The cures that the Aśvins performed, on patients irrespective of their social standing in course of their wanderings, possibly divulged some of the special remedies and treatments, thereby implying a dilution of the control of brahmanical priests over this crucial knowledge.

It is also probable that there developed conflict of interest between the healer and the priest. The Vedic healer was also a priest with knowledge of rituals and magical spells who functioned alongside the sacrificial priest. In course of time competition between the two for power and prestige emerged. Gradually healers came to be considered by the sacrificial priests as impure and contaminating, and hence were eventually excluded from the higher, more sacred circles of priestly culture.[53]

It is hardly surprising, therefore, that the Upaniṣads are as good as silent about the medical discipline and its practitioners. In the whole of the Upaniṣadic literature, reference to healing (bheṣajakṛtaḥ) occurs only once.[54] Other than another passage in the Maitrī Upaniṣad mentioning bhiṣak in the metaphorical sense, Chattopadhyaya points out that there is no other reference to bhiṣak or bheṣaja in the principal Upaniṣads.[55] Brahmanical inhibition against the medical profession became so strong that in course of time Takśaśilā, a reputed centre of medical study, was declared an impure region.[56]

The social and ritual impurity of the medical practitioner is reiterated in the Brahmanical law books. He is included by earliest law-givers like Āpastamba,[57] Gautama,[58] and Vasiṣṭha[59] in the list of people whose food is unacceptable to a brāhmaṇa. However, in the following passages from the law-codes of Vasiṣṭha, it is interesting to note that from the long list of impure persons, the physician and the surgeon are among a handful who are deemed particularly polluting.

“The following are unfit to be eaten: food given by a physician, a hunter, a harlot, a law enforcement agent, a thief, a heinous sinner, a eunuch, or an outcaste; as also given by a miser, a man consecrated for a sacrifice, a prisoner, a sick person, a man who sells Soma, a carpenter, a washer-man, a liquor, dealer, a spy, an usurer, a leather-worker, a śūdra, a man who bears arms, a lover of a married woman and a husband who countenances it, an arsonist, or one who does not execute those who deserve the capital punishment; food given after yelling: “Who will eat this?; food given by a corporate body or by a courtesan.”[60]

Vasiṣṭha allows certain items (such as food given unasked) to be accepted from even such persons but categorically forbids offerings from the following persons:

“Almsfood given by physicians, hunters, surgeons, fowlers, eunuchs and unchaste wives is not to be accepted even if given unasked.”[61]

“Almsfood given by physicians, hunters, surgeons, fowlers, eunuchs and unchaste wives is not to be accepted even if given unasked.”[61]

Practice of medicine is deemed a secondary sin causing loss of caste.[62] This repugnance for the medical practitioner is further heightened in the law-codes of Manu. The degree of abomination he evoked in the brahmanical system can be comprehended from the directive that prohibits individuals from making a gift of food to a medical practitioner with warning of grave consequences for the donor. The food turns into blood and pus (bhiṣaje pūyaṣ oṇitam)[63] implying that the donor becomes a creature that subsists on pus and blood. Likewise, the food of a physician is as vile as pus (pūyaṃ cikitsakasya annam).[64] His position was akin to that of a śūdra; his food was not to be taken and neither was he considered fit to receive food.[65] Bhīṣma echoes similar sentiments in the Śānti-parva and Anuśāsana-parva of the Mahābhārata. The physician is also excluded from sacrifices to the gods and to the manes by both Manu[66] and Viṣṇu.[67]

The brahmanical denunciation of medical profession notwithstanding, the general social outlook towards brāhmaṇas came in for gradual modification with significant changes occurring in the socio-religious environment. Zysk draws our attention to two developments. First he postulates that the denigration faced by the medical priests turned them into members of a fringe-group which included heterodox wandering ascetics like the Buddhists who renounced the trappings of orthodox ideologies and practices. They had a radically different worldview and of the place of mankind in it. In fact the understanding of the relationship between humans and nature in early Buddhism was not very different from that which contributed to āyurvedic medical thought.[68] The emergence of Buddhism as an alternative to the hierarchical and discriminatory ideology of the brahmanical orthodoxy provided a liberal and more equitable environment in which the socially stigmatized medical practitioners could secure a dignified position and pursue their vocation.[69] Not only did it foster a culture of medicine, physicians like the legendary Jivaka received much appreciation.[70]

The four fold structure the Four Noble Truths parallels the practice of doctors of the Buddha’s day:

diagnose the disease,

identify its cause,

determine whether it is curable, and

outline a course of treatment to cure it.

The Buddha takes on the role of a spiritual physician.[71]

More importantly the Buddha himself is identified as a physician (bhissako) and surgeon (sallakatto) in the Itivuttaka.[72] In the Milindapañha, the Buddha says, “...I am the supreme Healer and Physician.”[73] By the early centuries of the Common Era, the epithets of “vaidyarāja” (the king of physicians), “vaidyottam’ (best among physicians), “mahāvaidyarāja” and “mahāśayakartā (the great surgeon), are bestowed on him in Lalitavistāra and “bhaisajyarāja” in Saddharmapuṇḍarīka.[74] These developments had a gradual impact on social attitude towards physicians as absolute disapproval slowly made way for a better social standing of the medical practitioners. Medicine is accorded much importance in the Buddhist vihāras.[75] It is noteworthy that while brahmanical priestly class regarded Takśaśilā as an impure region, the Buddhist sources refer to it as a centre of learning where tikiccā (cikitsā) is taught.[76] We have references to Jaina physicians in epigraphs. We have referred to Pāpaka, son of a learned Jaina physician Ambaṭa, earlier. The Saidapur inscription speaks of the greatness of a Jaina surgeon named Aggalayya who flourished under the patronage of the Chālukya king Jayasimha II (1015-1042 CE).[77]

The second positive development in this context, as Zysk points out, is the change in the position accorded to the Atharvaveda, the text which physicians hold in the highest esteem. The medical compendia declare that Āyurveda is derived from the Atharvaveda itself. Āyurveda is an upāṅga of the Atharvaveda,[78] and hence it is to this Veda that physicians profess their devotion.[79] However, this text was held to be of lower order than the other three Vedic texts, and it is the inferior position accorded to the Atharvaveda that entailed a low social position for those who respected it the most. However, by the time of the final redaction of the medical treatises during the early centuries of the Common Era, the Atharvaveda came to be included with the other three Vedas in a fourfold classification of sacred Hindu texts.[80] This implied that the physicians and their healing art too became part of the brāhmaṇically based Hindu religious tradition.[81] The medical treatises assert that this science was propounded by none other than Brahmā himself[82] and that it has a glorious tradition of transmission thorough other deities to humans.[83] Besides, the use of such epithets as eternal (śāśvata), sacred (puṇya) and divine (svargya) for this discipline, though in the context of surgery (śalya), is also significant.[84] In a sense it is an attempt to claim the same position for the discipline of medicine as the Vedic corpus itself.

Other than their association with the Atharvaveda, the notion of impurity of physicians can be attributed to the use of materials that are conventionally regarded as polluting and disgusting. Physicians and surgeons, in particular, had to handle various animal parts. Students practiced surgical procedures on parts like urinary bladders, hairy skins, vessels and teeth of dead animals. The flesh, blood, skin, faeces, and other parts of animals were required in various medicinal preparations. Surgeons necessarily had to come in contact with professions which are placed low in the social order, such as blacksmiths (karmāra) for manufacturing their instruments;[85] leech-gatherers to procure leeches for blood-letting therapy and cremation-ground workers to procure corpses for dissection.

In the brahmanical law books healers are given a very low social position. According to the Uśanas, the bhiṣak is the offspring of a clandestine union between a brāhmaṇa and a kṣatriya girl and he maintains himself by studying Āyurveda in its eight parts, or astronomy, astrology and mathematics.[86] Manu designates the art of healing as an occupation for the base-born and it is specifically assigned to the ambaṣṭhas.[87] According to the lawgivers, the ambaṣṭha as an anuloma sprung from the marriage of a brāhmaṇa man with a vaiśya woman[88] or an offspring of a kṣatriya with a vaiśya woman (according to Gautama as interpreted by Haradatta). Uśanas lays down that he may live by surgery, while Haradatta commenting on Āpastamba Dharmasūtra, says that ambaṣṭha and śalyakṛnta are synonymous.[89]

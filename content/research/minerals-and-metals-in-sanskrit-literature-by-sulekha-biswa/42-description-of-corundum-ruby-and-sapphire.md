4.2. Description of Corundum, Ruby and Sapphire

[Full title: Item-wise Discussion on Gems with Decreasing Hardness; (2): Corundum, Ruby and Sapphire (hardness 9)]

It has been indicated in Table VII-2, that pure corundum (Kuruvinda) is colourless, and specific lattice substitutions in A 1203 induce specific colour and produce gems such as red ruby or padmaraca, blue sapphire or indranila etc. These three minerals have high hardness (9), and only diamond is harder than them (Agastimata 238). Kautilya mentioned the use of Kuravinda as an abrasive (AS. 2.14.48).

VII-14 Buddhabhatta made an interesting statement that ruby and sapphire cannot be scratched by any mineral other than diamond and corandum (Ratna-pariksha 137) ; similar statements have been repeated in Garuda-purana 1.70.27 and Manimala 213% kuruvindam, tatha vajram vimucya anyena kenacit | na sakyam lekhanam karttum padmaraga indranilayoh ||  Samma (1984: 51-52 ) has expressed surprise in the above statements since ruby and sapphire have the same Al 2 O 3 lattice as corundum, and are supposed to have similar hardness. However trace substitution by low energy oxides of Cr, Fe etc. might lower surface free energy and hardness of Al 203 by a small amount, and this may be detectable when pure corundum powder is mounted on a grinding wheel. Bauer (1968:282) has reported slight differences in hardness values of the varieties of corundum, and the fact that ruby is slightly softer than sapphire ancient India Padmaraca or red ruby used to come to the/from the gen-beds of Sri Lanka. The Areca-palms was of the river Ravanaganga, overgrown with Areca-palms was strewn with varieties of ruby (Garuda-purana 1.70.3-17). Padmarac was also known as manikva and cunni (from curnika, small pieces). Bes ruby has been described as red like lotus (padmaraga), transparent, brilliant, heavy and clear. Agastimata 213 classified rib on the basis of the amount of radiance emitted above, below and on sides, then held against the sun. If the radiance spreaded all around, indicating lack of crystallinity, then the piece was considered to be spurious (Rayanaparikkha 68).

VII-15 Ruby was known to exist in different shades of red. Padmaraga was the red variety with a tinge of white, nilagandhi red with a blue or black tint, saugandhika yellowish red. The last one was probably known in the West as the orange padparadscha (a corrupt form of the word padmaraca), the yellowish aurora-red gem from Sri Lanka (Herbert Smith, Gemstones pp. 289-90). Dull-hued red variety 'with its lustre remaining within' was sometimes called kuruvinda (Garuda-purana 1.70.13). Crimsonyellow variety was known as karundaka or kaurandaka (Manimala 407; Garuda-purana 1.74.2) A transparent variety slightly red and yellow was known as kasavaka (Ratna-pariksha 218; Garuda-purana 1.74.3) A red variety which appeared to be smeared with milk (dugdhaliptasma) was defined as lasunapadam, like lasuna or garlic (Navaratnapariksa 115). Rose-red balas-ruby belongs to an altogether different category. It is not Al 2 O 3 but a spinel or mixed oxide, Mgal 204. It is softer than ruby, its hardness being eight in the Moh's Scale. Like Al 2 O 3, it is colourless when pure, chromium substitution making A 1203' it red. Spinels come in a wide variety of colours, but here we are concerned with rose-red spinels which have been known as balas rubies or rubis balais. Spinels are abundant in the ruby mines of Badakshan. In the 13 th Century A. D., Maroco Polo collected spinels with rubies in the province of Balascia on the Upper Oxus, which is identical with the Badakshan of the present day. Bauer writes (1968: 298) that the term 'balas-ruby' is derived from the place-name Balascia. However, we propose that both these names were derived from the Sanskrit term balasuryaka, the crimson-coloured morning sun. Kautilya mentioned balasurvaka as a variety of padmaraga or ruby (AS 2.11.29). Thus it is quite likely that ever since Kautilya's

VII-16 time, the Indians were familiar with the spinel ruby mines of Badakshan. These mines remained famous for more than a millennium. In 1315, Thakkar Pheru wrote: atitejah agnivarna lala namdakhasatha dese  (Rayanaparikkha 104) At that time, spinel ruby was known as 1 al. At times, ruby (9), spinel ruby (8), hyacinth variety of Zircon (7) and hessonite variety of garnt (74), appeared to have similar reddish yellow colour, but these could be easily distinguished from one another by scratch tests since their hardness values (given in parenthesis) are different. Indranila or sapphire was known to the Indians as a different category of gem-mineral (AS 2.11.31), but we know now, that this is blue corundum (Vide table VII-2). According to Buddhabhatta (Ratna-pariksha 195-196; also supported in Garuda-purana 1.72.17-18 and Manimala 405), a sapphire in which the colours of the rainbow sparkle is an indranila, and the one which turns the milk in a bowl blue, is a mahanila. The Agastimata however labels the cond variety as indranila (Agastimata 268), the sapphires from Sri Lankamahanila and the rest of the sapphires from other sources as plain nilas. Utpala, mentioned in Arthasastra and Manimala, is greenish blue and could be aquamarine rather than sapphire. The Ratnasastra texts described indranila occurring in different shades of blue, and we know to-day that sapphire can be deep-blue, the most admired tone being 'intense cornflower-blue'. Sapphire could be whitish blue leuco-sapphire or somanaka (Ratna-pariksha 219; Garuda-purana 1.74.3) or possess a yellowish tinge ('Oriental topaz').We have already mentioned that sapphire is slightly harder than ruby (Bauer 1968:282).The word'sapphire came from the old Arabic (also Hebrew) sapeer'meaning 'to scratch" to scratch' , some thing which is hard.Its hardness

VII-17 enabled it to be distinguished from less hard blue gems like. aquamarine, lapis lazuli, and blue-coloured materials like quartz, glass or marble (Garuda-purana 1.72.14). Garuda Purana mentioned various defects (grain boundary, holes etc.) of some sapphire samples, their occasional contamination with mica and moderate thermal resistance (Garuda-purana 1.72.6 and 11). The Indians received the best variety of sapphire from the Padmakara province of Sri Lanka which must have been around the modern Ratnapura on the Kaluganga. (Manimala 397 Like blue to yellow transition, sapphire was known to have blue to red transition also, and such reddish blue coloured corundum samples could be called either sapphire or ruby. As a matter of fact the Arabs guessed (without any scientific knowledge) that sapphires and rubies belonged to a transitional single group of gem mineral, and they called this Yaqut which, according to Al-Afkani, occurred in seven varieties (Sarma, 1984:61; Wiedemann, 836-840). Both ruby and sapphire crystals, specially the dark coloured ones, are dichroic i.e appearing differently coloured when viewed through mutually perpendicular axes. The famous 'Sapphire Marveilleux' appears blue by day and red amethystine by night (Manimala, p. 459). Sapphire with a dash of red has been known as tittibha (Manimala 423). A deep blue gem dashed with red was known as nilanga or violet-ruby (Manimala, part 2, pp.514-515).. p, This Asterias or star Sapphire is a special variety which when viewed against light appears like a six-rayed chatoyant star. is due to the reflection of light from the surface of a multitude of microscopically small tubular cavities or rifts enclosed in the crystal, and grouped into three sets, each of which is parallel to a face of the hexagonal prism (Bauer, 1968: 284). Most Ratnasastra texts have discussed the pricing pattern Sapphire was considered to have different of rubies and sapphires. shades of colour which were equated to 'Castes' e.g. whitish blue (brahman), reddish blue (Ksatriya), yellowish-blue (Vaisva) and black-blue (sudra) etc. (Rayanaparikkha 82).

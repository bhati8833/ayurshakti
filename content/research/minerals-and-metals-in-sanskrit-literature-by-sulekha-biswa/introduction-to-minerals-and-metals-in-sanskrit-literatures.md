Introduction to Minerals and Metals in Sanskrit literatures

This dissertation seeks to record ancient India's long acquaintance and sustained expertise in the fields of gems, non-gem minerals metals and alloys, as revealed from the Vedic and Sanskrit literatures. The period from the Rgveda to the Rasa-Ratna-Samuccaya covers nearly three millennia. Traditionally, the 'ancient period in India is said to have ended around 1200 A.D. with the firm grip of the Muslims over this sub-continent. Our narrative however includes Rasaratna-samuccaya, an early 14th century A.D. treatise, since it provides a comprehensive review of the earlier Sanskrit literatures and the state-of-the art in the field of our interest. We have also given references to some Pali and Prakrt texts, to substantiate our findings. A major problem about the literary sources, specially in tem of their correlation with the archaeological data, is concerned with their dates. The problem is specially acute with the Rgveda; we cannot accept Max Muller's proposition of 1200 B.C. as the date of the Rgveda, since this is the accepted date of the Mahabharata - - famed archaeological site of Hastinapura. The events of the Sarasvati river valley, as described in the Rgveda, must have taken place in the third millennium B.C., witnessing a fully grown Harappat civilization. This point shall be elaborated later. We also have controversies regarding the dates of Panini, Kautilya, Nagarjuna etc., on which we would submit our considered opinions.

I-2 The writings on minerals and metals in the Sanskrit literature ere surprisingly scanty and scattered. These have been often peripheral to other topics of interest such as philosophy, theology, poetry and history as in the Vedic literatures, and later grammar, political economy, prognostication, astrology, medicine and alchemy etc. Fortunately, the writers and compilers of the above topics had some interest in gems, non-gem minerals, metals, alloys etc., and thus we have been benefitted from their treatises. Another fact that stands out is that the references to minerals and metals appeared much after the actual uses had already been made. This was partly because, as we have explained, the writers were primarily interested in subjects other than minerals and metals. Another reason, probably the major one, behind this time-lag was the rigid division of labour and casteism which discouraged fast diffusion of knowledge. The metalsmiths were low-caste people, debarred from Vedic scholars were often ignorant of metallurgy. The traditional scholars took some interest in astronomy, mathematics and geometry on accout of their involvement with the rituals, which were not concerned with smelting, pottery-making etc. The gap between metallurgy and scholasticism however narrowed somewhat in the Kauryi and Gupte eras studying the Vedas, and the We have tried to elucidate the ancient Sanskrit technical terms in the light of our modern sciei tific knowledge. In several cases, doubts and controversies persist. More research is necessar to fully explain certain terms such as vaikintake, capala, pulska, bhisma etc.

I-3 Chapters II and III deal with the evidences from the Rgveda and other Vedic literatures, respectively. The writings of Panini (Chapter IV) and Kautilya (Chapter V) throw a flood of light on the ever-increasing use and knowledge of minerals and metals attending the second phase of urbanisation in the ancient India. The data on Mahabharata (Chapter VI) show how the epic was enlarged with metallurgical references inserted during the Historical period after 500 B.C. One millennium of gemmology studies in India has been review in Chapter VII. The studies on medicine were initiated during the age of Atharvaveda, when botanical as well as mineralogical recipes. were tried on patients, and their effects studied. From Caraka to Patanjali to Nagarjuna, the Rasasastra was developed in stages which grew to its full stature during the Tantric period of alchamy (700 to 1300 A.D.). Around 1300 a.D. started the iatro-chemical period. Some fifteen centuries of progress in Rasasasta (reviewed in Chapter VIII) also reflected India's continuing involvement in gems, minerals and metals. Chapter IX presents the highlights of the 14 th century A+D. text Rasa-Ratna-Samuccaya which gives us almo the last word about 'ancient' India's progress in the field of our study. The last chapter (X) contains our concluding remarks, and highlights the following points pertaining to the Sanskrit literatures on minerals and metals in ancient India:

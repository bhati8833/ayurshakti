7. Metallic Ores mentioned in the Arthashastra

The Arthasastra is the earliest Indian text dealing with the mineralogical characteristics of metallic ores and other mineralaggregate rocks. It recognised ores in the earth, in rocks, or in liquid form, with excessive colour, heaviness and often strong smell and taste (2.12.1). The text described a 'liquid oce' which seems to be a petrolei um like bio-geo-genetic material. This liquid flows inside a hole, a cave, a table-land, dug-out mountain whose regions are known to contain gold ore. This liquid is coloured yellow, viscous, clear and heavy and thrown in water, spreads on the surface like oil' (2.12.2-3). The description agrees to that of petroleum of asphalt basis as found in the Punjab and Baluchistan (Roy, 1946: 113). A very similar material with stronger small and taste was known to be bitumen or silajatu (2.12.4). These materials were erroneously believed to contain gold and mentioned as 'metallic ores' or ahatu. The Arthasastra described a gold-bearing ore which Roy (1946: 113-114) has interpreted as typical auriferous pyrite. We reproduce the translation of the relevant section (2.12.5) of the arthasastra with Roy's interpretation in the parentheses:

V-15 66 Those ores which are obtained from the planes, or slopes. of mountains; which are either yellow (pyrite is yellow), or red as copper, or reddish yellow (pyrite often becomes pseudomorphous after limonite and then develops brown colour) which are disjoined (individual crystals of pyrite), and marked th blue lines (grain boundary of chalcopyrite and pyrite).... which are marked with spots like drops of curd (quartz is the curdwhite mineral), dazzlingly bright yellow as turmeric (native gold), when broken, show lines, spots or svastikas of fine sand (minute crystals of pyrite occur between larger crystals; when isolated, such layers readily crumble away and became like sand grains; separation of metallic gold in the form of dings has been reported the svastika represents the skeleton of a gold crystal separated out of gradually decomposing auriferous pyrite); which contain globular masses (decomposed pyrite showing iron hat); and when rated, do not split out eit foam and smoke (1.e. sulphur dioxide gas) 37 Similarly, the silver ore described in the Arthasastra (2.12.6) seems to be a complex sulphide ore containing silver: colour of a conch-shell, camphor, vimalaka (pyrite?) sasycka (copper sulphate, an oxd dation product of associated copper sulphide), containing lead (calena), anjana or antimony (as sulphide), smells like raw meat (smell of sulphur), ores which are disjoined (cube-shaped particles of cleaved calena), grey or

V-16 blackish white (colours of galena, argentite or tetrahedrite); when roasted do not split, but emit much foam and smoke (galena and argentite evolve SO 2 gas, and galena forms white incrustation of lead oxide or charcoal)". The Arthasastra described the sources and the qualities of good grade gold and silver ores (2.13.3-5 & 10-12). The quality had to be tested through streek on suitable stone-plate (2.13.17-27). a Copper ores were stated (2.12.12) to be heavy, greasy, tawny (chalcopyrite left exposed to air, tarnishes), green (colou of malachite), dark blue with yellowish tint (azurite), pale red or red (native copper). Lead ores were stated to be (2.12.13) greyish black, like Kakamecake (this is the colour of galana), yellow like pigeon bile (lead ore gossan), marked with white lines (quartz or calcite gangue minerals) and smelling like raw flesh (odour of sulpher). Tin ores could be (2.12.14) grey like saline earth (witherite?) or brown like burnt earth (cassigterite Iron ore was known (2.12.15) to be greasy stone, pale red colour (orange limonite) or of the colour of the sinduvara flower (hematite) Lahiri (1968, 1971) commented that 'the rock with greasy feel seems to be phyllite, and that with the pelo red colour, jasper'. This matches with the characteristic of the bedded iron ores of Bihar and Orissa.

V-17 After describing the above metallic ores or dhatus of specific metals, the Arthasastra writes: kakanda bhujapatravarno va vaikrttakadhatuh  'That which is of the colour of Kakanda (crow's egg) or birchleaf is vaikrintaka ore' (2.12.16). This presumably refers to the ore of vaikrhtaka (mentioned as a metal in 2.12.23; 2.17.14 Zand 4.1.35), the identity of which is not known. Roy (1946: 113) suggested that since magnetite is black and becomes brown on surface oxidation being changed to hematite, vaikrntaka ore is magnetite. In that case Vaikrntaka metal must be iron itself which used to be produced by the South Indians starting from the magnetite ore. It was later known as Kantaloha. Kangle (1986: Part II, p. 108 footnote) however quotes a view that vaikrintaka could be an alloy or metal made in China. This could be nickel metal or nickel alloy. It may be recalled that during the third to first century B.C., the Chinese coppernickel alloy was used in Taxila and north-west India for coinage, jewellery and fancy goods. This was known as pai-t'ung or tutena (ue) or white copper. It is not certain whether vaikrintaka natal was nickel or magnetite-based iron. Apart from colour or external appearance, another importan property of ore was known to be its density or heaviness, a variab property raf 1 acting the metal contents sarva dhatunam gauravavrddhau satvavrddhi  'In the case of all ores, when there is increase in heaviness, there is increase

V-18 in metal-content' (2.12.7). This phenomenon is due to the fact that in most cases, the metal-bearing minerals are heavier than the associated gangue minerals.
